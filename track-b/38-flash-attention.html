TRACK B · SCALING & ARCHITECTURE

L38 · FlashAttention and IO-Awareness

A naive attention layer writes the L×L score matrix to HBM and reads it back several times — for 4k tokens that is 134 MB of traffic per head to do 4.3 GFLOP of work. FlashAttention reorders the computation so the matrix never exists in memory, and the honest reason it is faster is that the GPU was never FLOP-bound.

~30 min difficulty ●●●●● build: memory-bandwidth model of attention
HBM round-trips SRAM tiling arithmetic intensity online softmax roofline no L×L materialisation recompute over store

1 · Why it exists #

Attention looks compute-heavy: every token compares with every other token. On paper, a 4k-token sequence with one head of dim 128 costs 2×128×4096² = 4.295×109 FLOPs — 10.9 µs at peak on an H800, and 27 µs at 40% MFU. Across 32 heads that is 1.37×1011 FLOPs. That sounds trivial. In 2019 it was not, and the reason was not FLOPs.

A standard implementation is a chain of separate kernels: QKT writes the L×L score matrix to HBM; the softmax kernel reads it, computes row maxima and denominators, writes probabilities; the AV kernel reads those probabilities back. For L = 4096 in fp16, that matrix is 4096² × 2 bytes = 32 MiB per head. The naive flow touches it roughly four times (write, read, read, plus intermediate max/sum arrays), giving 134.2 MB of HBM traffic per head to support 4.295 GFLOP — and 134.2 MB at 3.35 TB/s is 40 µs, longer than the 27 µs of math even at 40% MFU.

That is an arithmetic intensity of 4.295×109 / 1.342×108 = 32.0 FLOP/byte. An H800 can deliver ~989 TFLOP/s of bf16 math against ~3.35 TB/s of HBM bandwidth, so its ridge point — the intensity at which math and memory balance — is 989/3.35 ≈ 295 FLOP/byte. At 32, the kernel is nine times below the ridge: it is memory-bound by a factor of nine, so it runs at ~11% of peak FLOPs no matter how good the math is.

The failure, then: pre-2022 attention left 89% of the GPU idle, and context length was limited by memory pressure more than by compute. FlashAttention arXiv:2205.14135 and FlashAttention-2 arXiv:2307.08691 fixed the data movement, not the algorithm.

Modern attention is a memory-traffic problem disguised as a math problem. FlashAttention does exactly the same FLOPs as naive attention — it just stops paying to round-trip a huge intermediate through slow memory. That is why it is called IO-aware rather than faster math.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): cooking from a cookbook stored in a warehouse. Reading the whole book onto your kitchen counter once and cooking everything from it (tiling) beats fetching one page at a time, walking back, and re-fetching pages you already read (naive attention). The recipe does not change; the number of warehouse trips does.

What the analogy gets right

Hierarchy of memory sizes: kitchen counter = SRAM/registers (the H100-class has ~228 KB of shared memory per SM), warehouse = HBM (tens of GB). Bottleneck is round-trips, and locality is the whole game.

Where it breaks

You cannot simply “keep the book” because the L×L matrix does not fit — at 128k tokens it is 32 GiB per head. FlashAttention does not avoid the intermediate; it never materialises it, recomputing the needed pieces inside a tile and carrying forward a running softmax correction.

Online softmax, in one sentence

Softmax needs a global maximum and denominator. The trick is that both can be accumulated block by block: keep a running max m and running normaliser ℓ, and when a new block raises the max, rescale the accumulated output by exp(mold − mnew) before adding the new block's contribution. The result is exactly the same as computing the full softmax at once — no approximation — and it is why a single pass over K/V tiles is sufficient.

m(j) = max(m(j−1), maxi∈block j si)  ;  ℓ(j) = em(j−1)−m(j)ℓ(j−1) + ∑i∈block j esi−m(j)
o(j) = em(j−1)−m(j)o(j−1) + (es−m(j)/ℓ(j)) Vblock m = running row max, ℓ = running denominator, o = running output. The rescaling factors are what make the tiled computation exact. Only O(L·d) state (o, m, ℓ) is kept in SRAM; the O(L²) matrix exists only as a tile of size (block×block) at a time.

3 · The mechanism #

The roofline, computed

Arithmetic intensity — FLOPs per byte moved — decides whether a kernel is compute-bound or memory-bound. A device's ridge point separates the regimes:

QuantityH800-class value
Peak bf16 tensor math989 TFLOP/s
HBM bandwidth~3.35 TB/s
Ridge point989×1012 / 3.35×1012 = 295 FLOP/byte
Shared memory per SM~228 KB
fp16 tile, 128×128128 × 128 × 2 = 32 KB

Why tiling wins: the traffic arithmetic

For one head, L = 4096, d = 128, fp16:

  • Naive: the L×L matrix is 32 MiB. Writing it, reading it for the row max, reading it for the denominator, and reading it for the AV product is roughly 4 × 32 MiB = 134.2 MB of HBM traffic per head, plus Q, K, V (each 4096 × 128 × 2 = 1 MiB).
  • FlashAttention: Q stays in SRAM. K and V are streamed in tiles of 128 rows; with block size 128 you make L/128 = 32 passes over the K/V tiles, each pass reading L × d × 2 = 1 MiB per matrix. Traffic is approximately 2 × 32 × 1 MiB = 64 MB per head including Q (naive is 134.2 MB per head).
  • The real asymptotic win is different: FlashAttention's traffic is O(L·d) rather than O(L²). At L = 4096 the constant factor is only ~2× because 32 passes is a lot; at L = 32,768 it is 256 passes over 8 MiB tiles versus a naive matrix of 32,768² × 2 = 2 GiB touched four times = 8.59 GB — and because the tiled traffic grows linearly while the naive traffic grows as L², the gap widens exactly where you need it.

The real payoff: memory capacity

The traffic numbers above are modest at 4k. The decisive benefit is that the L×L matrix never has to exist:

Sequence lengthScore matrix, 1 head, fp16Score matrix, 32 headsPractical naive-attention requirement
4,09632 MiB1 GiBfits, but adds 1 GiB of peak memory
32,7682 GiB64 GiBexceeds one accelerator
131,07232 GiB2 TiBimpossible without sharding the sequence

So FlashAttention's primary contribution is capacity, not raw speed: it makes 32k–128k context fit in a fixed memory budget, and the speed follows because a memory-bound kernel stops being memory-bound. That is also how it paved the way for long-context training runs, which is why it precedes every long-context result in this track.

The design pattern you should generalise

Recompute over store

Cheaper to recompute a tile than to persist a giant intermediate and read it back. This is the same decision as gradient checkpointing (L20), applied at kernel level.

Fuse the chain

Naive attention is 3 kernels (QK, softmax, AV) with 2+ HBM round trips between them. FlashAttention is 1 kernel with no round trips. The entire speedup is the fusion.

To audit a kernel claim, ask for three numbers: achieved FLOP/s, bytes read from HBM per unit of work, and peak memory. A kernel can be correct, mathematically identical, and 5× slower purely by losing locality. Your BugAgents “4-point promotion gate” should include a bandwidth measurement for any kernel-level change, or the speed claim is not falsifiable.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — the arithmetic-intensity calculator

The model that predicts whether attention will run fast. Slower than 1/ridge means memory-bound; the fraction of peak you can expect is intensity/ridge.

def intensity(L, d, bytes_el=2, touches=4): flops = 2 * L * L * d # QK^T plus AV, per head traffic = touches * L * L * bytes_el # naive: L x L intermediate, multiple round trips return flops, traffic, flops / traffic RIDGE = 989e12 / 3.35e12 print(f"ridge point = {RIDGE:.1f} FLOP/byte") for L in (1024, 4096, 16384, 131072): f, t, i = intensity(L, 128) print(f"L={L:>7,} FLOPs={f:.3e} bytes={t:.3e} intensity={i:8.2f} % of peak≈{100*min(1,i/RIDGE):5.1f}%")

Ridge is 295.2 FLOP/byte. For naive attention the intensity is independent of L — it is 2·d/(touches·bytes_el) = 2·128/(4·2) = 32.0 FLOP/byte at every length, so naive attention is capped near 32/295.2 = 10.8% of peak forever. That constant is the punchline: the quadratic FLOPs are not the problem, the quadratic traffic per FLOP is.

Experiment 2 — prove the traffic bound with a real timing shape

Predict wall-clock for a naive-attention kernel from bytes alone and from FLOPs. The ratio tells you how much of a memory penalty you are paying at each length.

python -c " bw=3.35e12; fl=989e12*0.40 for L in (4096,16384,65536): flops=2*L*L*128; bytes_=4*L*L*2 print(f'L={L:>6,}: by FLOPs {flops/fl*1000:7.3f} ms by bytes {bytes_/bw*1000:7.3f} ms -> limited by {\"memory\" if bytes_/bw>flops/fl else \"compute\"}') "

Measured ratio bytes/FLOPs = 3.69 at every length (4096: 0.040 ms vs 0.011 ms; 65,536: 10.26 ms vs 2.78 ms) — constant, which is itself diagnostic: naive attention's intensity does not improve with L. Note this uses 40% MFU for the FLOP estimate, so it understates the pure roofline gap; against a compute-bound kernel at 40% of peak, the memory penalty is 3.69× and the ceiling on achievable FLOP efficiency is 40%/3.69 ≈ 10.8%, matching the roofline. This is the falsifiable prediction: if your measured naive-attention time matches the FLOPs number rather than the bytes number, your “naive” implementation is already tiled.

Experiment 3 — the online-softmax equivalence test

The whole method depends on the tiled softmax being exactly equal to the full one. Verify it numerically; this is a 20-line proof you can run.

import numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(38) L, d, B = 512, 64, 64 q, K, V = rng.normal(size=(1, d)), rng.normal(size=(L, d)), rng.normal(size=(L, d)) # exact s = (q @ K.T) / np.sqrt(d); p = np.exp(s - s.max(1, keepdims=True)); p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True) exact = p @ V # tiled, online softmax m = np.full((1,), -np.inf); l = np.zeros((1,)); o = np.zeros((1, d)) for j in range(0, L, B): sb = (q @ K[j:j+B].T) / np.sqrt(d) m_new = np.maximum(m, sb.max(1)) scale = np.exp(m - m_new) pb = np.exp(sb - m_new[:, None]) l = l * scale + pb.sum(1) o = o * scale[:, None] + pb @ V[j:j+B] m = m_new tiled = o / l[:, None] print("max abs diff:", np.abs(exact - tiled).max()) # ~1e-16

Measured max absolute difference: 1.67×10-16 — floating-point noise, i.e. the tiled computation is exact, not an approximation. That is the property that makes FlashAttention safe to use as a drop-in replacement rather than a quality trade — and the property your falsification should target: if you see a diff around 10-3, the rescaling factor uses mnew where it should use mold.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Assuming FlashAttention reduces FLOPs. Symptom: your capacity model predicts a speedup that does not appear, or a scaling-law extrapolation assumes cheaper attention. It computes the same FLOPs; it changes traffic and memory. Only sparsity changes the FLOPs.
  • Falling back silently. Symptom: long-context throughput drops on sequences longer than a threshold (often multiples of the block size) because the kernel hit an unsupported shape and used a slower path. Check kernel selection counters, and benchmark exactly your production sequence lengths including odd ones.
  • Masking cost. Symptom: attention slows by 20–40% when a custom mask is added, because the fast path handles only a few mask patterns and falls back. Arbitrary attention bias is a common cause.
  • Numerical differences in long chains. Symptom: outputs drift slightly against a reference implementation and a test comparing exact floats fails. This is expected fp accumulation order, not a bug — compare with a tolerance and check that error does not grow with depth.
  • Ignoring the KV traffic at decode. Symptom: prefill gets faster and decode does not, because decode is bandwidth-bound by the cache (L33), not by the attention score matrix. FlashAttention optimises the wrong term for decode throughput; cache compression is the lever there.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. An H800 runs 989 TFLOP/s bf16 with 3.35 TB/s of HBM. What is its ridge point, and what fraction of peak can a kernel with intensity 32 FLOP/byte reach?

Reveal answer

Ridge = 989×1012/3.35×1012 = 295.2 FLOP/byte. At intensity 32, achievable = 32/295.2 = 10.8% of peak ≈ 107 TFLOP/s. That is the theoretical ceiling for naive attention regardless of how well written the math is — and it is why FlashAttention (~60% of peak on real kernels) was such a large practical win.

Q2. For L = 4096, one head of d = 128 fp16, what is the size of the score matrix, and how much HBM traffic does touching it four times imply?

Reveal answer

4096² × 2 bytes = 33,554,432 B = 32 MiB. Four touches = 134,217,728 B = 134.2 MB. At 3.35 TB/s that is 40.1 µs of pure transfer, versus 4.34 µs of math at full peak (10.9 µs at 40% of peak) — so the traffic alone is 3.7× the math time. That gap is exactly the 32/295.2 = 10.8% efficiency ceiling, and it is why a naive kernel cannot be rescued by writing better arithmetic.

Q3. Why is the online-softmax rescaling needed, and what breaks if you forget it?

Reveal answer

Each new block may raise the row maximum, which changes the exponent shift used for all previously accumulated terms. Without rescaling the running output and denominator by exp(m_old − m_new), contributions from earlier blocks are computed under a different normalisation and the result is wrong — not merely imprecise, but inconsistent. The symptom is an error of order 10-2–10-1 that grows with block count.

Q4. At 131,072 tokens with 1 head fp16, how large is the naive score matrix, and what does that imply for naive attention at long context?

Reveal answer

131,072² × 2 = 3.44×1010 B = 32 GiB for a single head. With 32 heads it is 1 TiB, and even for a single head it exceeds the memory of one accelerator plus its headroom. So naive attention is not merely slow at 128k, it is infeasible without sequence sharding — which is the capacity argument for FlashAttention, and why every long-context training run in this track assumes it.

Q5. Diagnostic: prefill got 3× faster after enabling a fused attention kernel but generation throughput is unchanged. Why, and what should you optimise instead?

Reveal answer

Decode is bound by the KV cache read, not by the attention score matrix: at batch 1 and long context, every step streams the whole cache, and the score-matrix traffic is trivial by comparison (one query against L keys). So a kernel that removes intermediate traffic does not help decode. Optimise the cache instead: MLA/GQA (L33), token-wise compression (L34), KV quantization (L40) and batching to amortise weight reads (L44).
