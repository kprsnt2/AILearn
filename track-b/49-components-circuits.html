L49 · Induction Heads, Circuits, and What It Means for Reliability

An induction head is the smallest complete algorithm found inside a transformer: a previous-token head writes a marker, and a second head matches on it and copies what followed. It is why in-context learning works for patterns — and why it works reliably only inside the distribution the pattern has been seen in.

1 · Why it exists #

You give an agent a document with a table of codes and ask it to reformat them. It runs correctly for twelve rows and then substitutes the code from row 4 into row 13. Nothing in the prompt changed. The model did not "forget" — a specific, localisable mechanism failed, and you can name it.

That mechanism is an induction head, and it is the best-understood circuit in a transformer state as of 2026-10. Its job is the single most useful in-context behaviour: if the pattern A…B appeared earlier, then when you see A again, predict B. It is what lets a model copy a name, follow a formatting convention demonstrated three rows up, or continue an arbitrary sequence it has never been trained on.

Before this was understood, the failure looked like stochastic unreliability and the response was folklore ("use fewer examples", "lower the temperature"). Once you can detect induction heads and measure their activation pattern, the failure becomes diagnosable: the pattern occurred too rarely in the context, the marker was ambiguous because a more frequent pattern competed, or the relevant head was suppressed. That is a testable hypothesis instead of a shrug.

The model implements in-context pattern matching as an explicit two-head algorithm. Anything you ask it to do by “looking like an earlier example” runs through that circuit — so its reliability limits are the circuit's limits, and they can be measured.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): two people annotating a transcript. Person one (early layer) writes a sticky note on every word saying "the word before me was X". Person two (later layer) looks for a place where the previous word equals the current word, finds the sticky note from that earlier position, and reads off what came after it.

What the analogy gets right

Two modules, one writes a summary that another reads, and composition across layers — not depth alone — produces the capability. It also gets the failure mode: if the sticky note is ambiguous or the earlier occurrence is missing, the answer is a plausible-but-wrong copy.

Where it breaks

The sticky note is a vector added to the residual stream, not a discrete annotation — so it coexists and interferes with everything else written there (L48). Matching is a dot product, so it is graded: "close enough" matches produce partial copies. That gradedness is why induction failures look like substitutions rather than crashes.

The algorithm, stated precisely

  1. Previous-token head (usually in an early layer): for each position t, attends to position t−1 and writes the previous token's identity into the residual stream at t.
  2. Induction head (usually a later layer, often the first layer after a phase change): its query at position t contains (current token, previous token). It matches against keys from earlier positions that carry the same pair — i.e. an occurrence of the same bigram — and its output is a copy of the value at the position after that match.

Net effect: complete the pattern. Seen "A B … A", predict "B". This is the mechanism behind the pre-tokenizer surprise that models can continue arbitrary nonsense strings after one example and behind the observation that in-context learning emerges as a sudden improvement in loss during training.

3 · The mechanism #

scoreinduction(t, s) ≈ (qt(cur) + qt(prev)) · (ks(cur) + ks(prev)) The query at the current token encodes both the current token and the token before it (because the previous-token head wrote the latter into the residual stream). The key at a candidate earlier position encodes the same pair. Expanding the product gives a term matching the current token to itself and a term matching the previous token — the "Q-composition" that makes induction work. Head indices here are conceptual; real models have many heads sharing the work.

Worked: the sequence algebra of one induction step

Consider the context … f 4 7 … f 4 [?]. At the final position, the previous-token head has written "4" into the stream. The induction head's query therefore encodes (f, 4). Earlier in the sequence, position of the second "4" has a key encoding (4, f-prev) and is followed by 7. The highest-scoring earlier position is that "4", so the head's attention weight lands there and the value it reads — the embedding of the token that followed, 7 — is added to the output. Prediction: 7.

Two properties fall out of that trace, and both are reliability facts:

  • It needs the earlier occurrence in the context. No A…B earlier means no match, so the head's score is diffuse and the prediction is whatever the rest of the model says. That is why in-context examples when they are needed, and why they stop being needed once the pattern is in the weights.
  • It competes with other matches. Attention is a softmax over all positions; a token repeated 40 times in the context produces 40 candidate matches, and the selected one depends on tiny score differences. Higher repetition means more chances, and also more chance of picking the wrong copy.

How to detect one, quantitatively

The standard test constructs sequences with a long random prefix and a repeated bigram, then measures the loss on the token that should be copied compared with the loss on a control token. A model with active induction heads shows a large gap; a model without shows none.

induction score = −log p(correct copy)  —  ( −log p(control) )  =  log p(control) − log p(copy) Measured in nats. A large positive score means the model strongly prefers the copied token: evidence of an active induction circuit. A score near zero means the model is not doing in-context pattern matching for this input. Compare the score before and after ablating candidate heads to attribute it.

Where the "emergence" comes from

In training loss curves, induction heads typically appear abruptly rather than gradually, and the loss improves in a step at the same moment. The mechanism is a bootstrapping loop: a previous-token head is useful on its own and gets learned early; once its output is available, an induction head becomes useful and can be learned quickly; and an induction head makes copying so valuable that it accelerates everything else. It is a circuit, not a scale threshold — which also explains why adding parameters without enough data does not produce it.

This is the connection to audit discipline: you now have a targeted, falsifiable diagnostic for one class of agent failure. Run an induction-score probe on your own prompt templates; a template with a low score is asking the model for in-context pattern matching it may not perform. That is a checkable property of the prompt, not a mood about the model.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — build an induction probe and score a model

The exact test you should run on every model you deploy. It takes one GPU-minute and covers the copying behaviour most agents depend on.

import torch def induction_score(model, tokenizer, seed=0, n=20, prefix=50): g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed) scores = [] for _ in range(n): rand = torch.randint(1000, 28000, (prefix,), generator=g) a, b = rand[7].item(), rand[23].item() seq = list(rand.tolist()) + [a, b] + list(torch.randint(1000, 28000, (10,), generator=g).tolist()) + [a] ids = torch.tensor([seq]) with torch.no_grad(): logits = model(ids).logits[0, -1] p = torch.softmax(logits.float(), -1) copy_lp = torch.log(p[b]).item() # expected: the token that followed a ctrl = rand[40].item() ctrl_lp = torch.log(p[ctrl]).item() # control: a random unrelated token scores.append(ctrl_lp - copy_lp) return sum(scores) / len(scores) # from transformers import GPT2LMHeadModel ; m = GPT2LMHeadModel.from_pretrained('gpt2') # print(induction_score(m, None, seed=1))

On a small trained model the score is large and positive (typically several nats): the model strongly prefers the copied token. Repeat with the repeated bigram removed from the sequence — the score should collapse to near zero, which is the control that proves the probe measures copying and not a general token preference. Do that control before trusting any number.

Experiment 2 — the reliability boundary, simulated

The most useful property of an induction circuit: copying degrades as the number of competing near-matches grows, and the degradation curve is sharp. Model it as a top-1 competition between the intended match and a growing set of decoys.

import numpy as np def copy_accuracy(n_decoys, true_margin=2.0, decoy_var=1.0, trials=2000, seed=49): # the intended earlier occurrence scores slightly higher than a random # near-match; with more near-matches, one of them wins by chance rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) hits = 0 for _ in range(trials): s_true = true_margin + rng.normal(0, 0.5) s_dec = rng.normal(0, decoy_var, n_decoys) if n_decoys else np.array([-np.inf]) hits += int(s_true >= s_dec.max()) return hits / trials for nd in (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100): print(f"competing near-matches={nd:4d} copy_accuracy={copy_accuracy(nd):.3f}")

Measured: 1.000 / 0.955 / 0.923 / 0.832 / 0.747 / 0.544 / 0.377 / 0.231 for 0 / 1 / 2 / 5 / 10 / 25 / 50 / 100 competitors. Read that as a product statement: with 10 near-duplicate patterns in context you copy correctly three times in four; with 50 you are wrong more often than right. That is the production failure “row 13 got row 4's code” — not forgetting, but losing a top-1 competition among similar-looking matches. Mitigation: reduce ambiguity (unique delimiters, echoed row ids, fewer near-duplicate examples) or reduce the batch size per call.

Experiment 3 — estimate the token count at which in-context copying becomes necessary

A planning calculation: how many demonstrations do you need before the pattern is better served by the prompt than by relying on weights?

python -c " # each demonstration costs tokens; copying is more reliable with more demonstrations # but each additional demonstration raises competition for the induction match for demos in (1,3,5,10,20): tokens = demos*80 print(f'{demos:3d} demos = {tokens:5d} tokens of context; marginal prefill cost at 13B active = {2*13e9*tokens:.2e} FLOPs') print() print('with ~500-token documents per demo, 5 demos = 2500 tokens = ') print(2*13e9*2500) "

Measured cost of demonstrations at 80 tokens each: 1 → 80 tokens (2.08×1012 FLOPs), 3 → 240 (6.24×1012), 5 → 400 (1.04×1013), 10 → 800 (2.08×1013), 20 → 1,600 (4.16×1013). At 500 tokens per demonstration, 5 demos = 2,500 tokens of prefill (6.50×1013 FLOPs). Each demonstration is both a token cost and a candidate competitor in the induction competition of Experiment 2 — so beyond a handful you add cost and ambiguity without improving the pattern. That is why 3–5 clean examples usually beat 20.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Wrong-copy substitution. Symptom: outputs contain a value from the correct pattern but the wrong instance (row 4's code in row 13). Classic induction competition; reduce ambiguity or name instances explicitly.
  • Pattern never demonstrated. Symptom: the model produces a plausible but arbitrary format on the first instance and becomes consistent afterwards. Give one clean example before the batch, not after.
  • Distractor poisoning. Symptom: adding an irrelevant but superficially similar example degrades accuracy on the real task. Near-miss matches consume the induction match; keep examples on-pattern.
  • Assuming circuits are stable across versions. Symptom: a prompt that worked reliably on model v1 becomes flaky on v2. Circuit implementations differ between checkpoints; treat prompt reliability as version-specific and re-probe.
  • Over-attributing to one head. Symptom: ablating the "induction head" only partially changes behaviour, which seems to refute the theory. Capabilities are usually distributed across many heads; attribution studies need to measure effect sizes, not assume single-head responsibility.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Describe the two heads involved in induction and what each writes or reads.

Reveal answer

A previous-token head in an early layer attends to position t−1 for each token and writes the previous token's identity into the residual stream at t. An induction head in a later layer forms a query from both the current token and that previous-token marker, matches it against earlier positions carrying the same pair, and copies the value from the position after the match. Composition of the two across layers, not the head alone, produces pattern completion.

Q2. A context contains the same bigram 30 times before the final occurrence. Give the two competing effects on copy accuracy and the net expectation.

Reveal answer

Positive: more occurrences mean the pattern is certainly available and there are many chances that one match is strong. Negative: the induction softmax now competes over ~30 near-identical candidate matches, so the probability the correct (most recent or most relevant) one wins shrinks. Net: reliability improves from 0 to a handful of occurrences and then degrades as repeats accumulate, which is why a few clean examples beat many ambiguous ones.

Q3. Diagnostic: the same prompt gives 100% correct reformatting on 10 rows and fails at row 40, always by copying from a nearby earlier row. What is happening and what are two fixes?

Reveal answer

Induction competition in a long context: with 40 rows of the same shape, each new row has dozens of near-matches, and attention occasionally selects a neighbouring row instead of the intended one. Fixes: (a) make rows unambiguous — unique delimiters or explicit id echoed in the output; (b) process in batches small enough to keep competition low (e.g. 10 rows per call) and assemble results, trading a little context cost for determinism.

Q4. Why might an induction-score probe be a better deployment gate than a general benchmark?

Reveal answer

Because it targets the capability your product actually depends on (in-context copying of patterns) and it is falsifiable in one GPU-minute with a built-in control (remove the repeated bigram; score should collapse). A general benchmark averages across many capabilities your product never uses, so it can be flat while the specific circuit your agent needs has changed.

Q5. Why does in-context learning appear as a sudden improvement in training rather than a gradual one?

Reveal answer

Because it is a two-stage composition: a previous-token head must exist and be useful first, and only then does an induction head become learnable, since the induction head's benefit depends on the marker being available. That bootstrapping produces an abrupt gain once the second stage becomes viable — a circuit appearing, not a capability scaling smoothly (which is why L29's metric-artefact discussion does not apply to this particular step).
