L41 · Distillation: Teacher→Student, and Reasoning Distillation

A hard label is one bit per token; a teacher's distribution is the whole shape of its uncertainty, and a student trained on it learns faster. The catch: every distilled token costs a teacher forward pass, and a reasoning student inherits every habit of its teacher — including the bad ones.

1 · Why it exists #

You want the quality of a large model at the price of a small one. Quantization (L40) gets you 4× by spending precision; distillation gets you an order of magnitude by spending pre-existing knowledge instead.

Two failures motivate it. First, a hard-label target is astonishingly sparse as a training signal: for a vocabulary of ~105 tokens, the target is one-hot, so the gradient tells the student “increase this token, decrease all others” with no information about which others are plausible. A teacher's distribution over the same token says “this token, but also these three are reasonable and that one is definitely wrong” — many bits of structure per position, not one.

Second, the production failure that makes this urgent: a 552B-total MoE with 16B active is already at 34.5× compression versus dense-equivalent, and you still may not be able to serve it for a given latency target. At that point the only remaining lever is a genuinely smaller architecture — and the cheapest way to build one is to make it imitate something that already works.

Hinton et al.'s original distillation framing, and the modern reasoning-distillation line, both rest on one measured fact: a student trained on soft targets reaches a given quality with substantially fewer tokens than one trained on hard labels.

Hard labels teach the answer. Teachers teach the shape of the answer space. That is worth 10–70× in model size — provided you can afford the teacher's forward pass on every training token.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): learning a language from a textbook answer key versus from a native speaker who also tells you “that's odd but legal.” The answer key is right exactly once; the speaker conveys a whole distribution over what is acceptable.

What the analogy gets right

Dense guidance beats sparse correction. A teacher encodes similarities between classes the labels do not contain — the canonical “dark knowledge” point: a teacher's distribution over digit classes resembles a hand-written 3 more than a 7 even when both are wrong.

Where it breaks

A speaker can be corrected; a teacher's errors are transmitted as if they were facts. Distillation has no ground-truth anchor unless you keep one, so a confidently wrong teacher produces a confidently wrong student. This is the central risk in reasoning distillation, and it is not hypothetical.

The four forms you will meet

FormSignalCost
Logit / KL distillationTeacher's full next-token distribution per positionTeacher forward pass on every training token
Sequence distillationTeacher-generated text (hard labels) on your corpusTeacher generation, no student gradient interaction
Reasoning distillationTeacher's chain-of-thought traces (often rejection-sampled for correctness)Many teacher samples per kept trace
On-policy distillationTeacher scores the student's own outputsTeacher forward on student rollouts each step

The cost ordering is the useful part: logit distillation needs a teacher pass per token (expensive, dense signal); sequence distillation needs the teacher once to write a dataset you can reuse forever; reasoning distillation has a rejection multiplier (you generate many traces, keep few).

3 · The mechanism #

&lagr; = (1−α) CE( y, pS )  +  α T² KL( pT(T) ‖ pS(T) ) p(T) = softmax(logits / T). The T² factor compensates for the fact that gradients of the soft-target term shrink as 1/T², so without it increasing T silently reduces the distillation weight. α balances hard labels (anchor to ground truth) against the teacher.

Worked: what temperature actually does to the signal

Take a teacher distribution t = (0.70, 0.10, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05) and a student s = (0.40, 0.20, 0.10, 0.10, 0.20). Both are given as probabilities for illustration; the point is how the KL and the entropy behave as T rises:

TemperatureKL(pT ‖ pS)Teacher entropyGradient scale (1/T²)
T = 10.21841.0098 nats1.0
T = 20.06301.4578 nats0.25
T = 40.01521.5751 nats0.0625

Two things happen at once. The KL magnitude falls by 14× from T = 1 to T = 4 — which is why the T² factor exists, since it restores the effective weight — and the teacher's entropy rises, i.e. its distribution becomes softer and more informative about the tail. That is the trade: soft targets carry more relative information about unlikely classes, but their absolute gradient contribution shrinks unless corrected.

Compare against the hard-label baseline: for the same target the cross-entropy against a one-hot is −ln 0.70 = 0.3567 nats with zero information about the other four classes. The teacher's distribution has 1.4578 nats of entropy at T = 2 — roughly 2.1 bits of structured information per token that a hard label does not carry.

Worked: what distillation costs in FLOPs

Per training token, a logit-distillation step needs a teacher forward pass (2 NT) and the student's forward+backward (6 NS):

costper token = 2 NT + 6 NS    vs    6 NS for plain training So distillation multiplies training cost by (1 + NT/(3NS)). For NT = 552B (8B active input side) and NS = 8B: (1 + 552/(3×8)) = 24×. For a 70B teacher and 7B student: (1 + 70/21) = 4.3×.
Teacher / studentSize ratioTraining cost multiplier
552B / 8B69×24×
284B / 13B21.8×8.3×

That is the honest cost. But note what it buys: an 8B student at 24× the cost of training an 8B from scratch is still far cheaper than training a 552B from scratch — and you already have the teacher. The comparison that matters is never “distillation versus free”, it is “distillation versus the run you were going to do anyway”.

Reasoning distillation: the multiplier is the rejection loop

Modern reasoning distillation state as of 2026-10 replaces logits with traces: sample many chains of thought from the teacher, keep only those whose final answer is verifiably correct, fine-tune the student on the traces. The economics are dominated by the keep rate:

teacher tokens generated = kept tokens / keep_rate     ∴  cost = (1/keep_rate) × generation cost If the teacher solves 25% of problems, you generate 4× the traces you use. That multiplier is usually more important than the model sizes involved, and it is the reason verifiable domains (math, code) distil far more cheaply than open-ended ones.

Worked: to produce 200,000 accepted traces at a 25% keep rate you must generate 800,000; at a 5% keep rate, 4,000,000. The second case is an 8× cost increase for the same dataset, so choosing a verifier whose keep rate is high is the single biggest lever in the pipeline.

Distillation is only auditable if the teacher is pinned: record teacher checkpoint hash, decoding config, temperature, rejection criteria and keep rate. A distilled model with an unpinned teacher is unreproducible by construction — and its failures are untraceable because you cannot re-derive the training data. This is the same discipline as your hash-chained ledger, applied to data lineage.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — derive the T² correction yourself

Why the T² factor exists is the most-asked question about distillation and the answer is one experiment. Measure how the soft-target gradient shrinks with T.

import numpy as np def softmax(z, T=1.0): z = np.asarray(z, float) / T; z = np.exp(z - z.max()); return z / z.sum() teacher = np.log(np.array([0.70, 0.10, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05])) student = np.log(np.array([0.40, 0.20, 0.10, 0.10, 0.20])) for T in (1.0, 2.0, 4.0): pt, ps = softmax(teacher, T), softmax(student, T) kl = np.sum(pt * np.log(pt / ps)) ent = -np.sum(pt * np.log(pt)) g = ps - pt # d KL / d logits print(f"T={T}: KL={kl:.4f} H_teacher={ent:.4f} |grad|={np.abs(g).sum():.5f} |grad|*T^2={np.abs(g).sum()*T*T:.5f}")

Measured: KL falls 0.2184 → 0.0630 → 0.0152 (14.4× from T = 1 to T = 4), teacher entropy rises 1.0098 → 1.4578 → 1.5751, and the raw gradient norm shrinks with T (0.600 → 0.3246 → 0.1561) while |grad|·T² moves the other way (0.600 → 1.298 → 2.497). The shrinkage is the 1/T² effect, and the T² multiplier in the loss is what restores the term's weight — this table is the measurement that justifies it. The measured |grad|·T² is not exactly constant because the softening of the distributions is the second, deliberate effect you are buying.

Experiment 2 — the physics of the cost multiplier

Distillation cost depends on the teacher/student ratio, not on absolute sizes. Compute it for any pairing before you commit to a plan.

python -c " pairs=[(552e9,8e9),(284e9,13e9),(70e9,7e9),(671e9,37e9),(7e9,1.5e9)] for T,S in pairs: m=1+T/(3*S) print(f'teacher {T/1e9:6.0f}B student {S/1e9:5.1f}B ratio {T/S:6.1f}x -> training cost {m:5.2f}x plain') print() for kr in (0.5,0.25,0.1,0.05): print(f'keep_rate {kr:.0%}: to get 200k traces must generate {int(200000/kr):,}') "

Cost multipliers: 24.0× (552B/8B), 8.28× (284B/13B), 4.33× (70B/7B), 7.05× (671B/37B), 2.56× (7B/1.5B). Rejection counts for 200,000 accepted traces: 50% keep → 400,000 generated; 25% → 800,000; 10% → 2,000,000; 5% → 4,000,000. The point: a teacher 5× larger costs 2.6× more training, while a teacher 69× larger costs 24× — superlinear in the ratio, so there is a sweet spot.

Experiment 3 — demonstrate soft-target learning on a tiny classifier

A 3-layer MLP on a synthetic 10-class problem with a small labelled set: compare hard-label training against distillation from a large teacher trained on much more data. This reproduces the central claim, and it gives you a harness to reuse.

import numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(41) def data(n): X = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 32)) # 10 classes from 10 random prototypes; some are confusable by construction P = rng.normal(0, 1, (10, 32)); P[3] = P[8] + rng.normal(0, 0.6, 32) logi = X @ P.T y = logi.argmax(1) return X, y, logi def mlp(X, hidden=64): return np.tanh(X @ rng.normal(0, 1/np.sqrt(32), (32, hidden))) Xl, yl, _ = data(500) # small labelled set Xt, yt, lt = data(50_000) # teacher's larger corpus print("small-set unique labels:", len(np.unique(yl))) # a real experiment: train student on hard labels vs on soft posteriors from a model # fitted on the 50k pool. Expect the soft-target student to generalise better. # (Full training loop omitted; the diagnostic to look for is val accuracy at equal epochs.)

The synthetic setup has two deliberately confusable classes (3 and 8), so a hard-label student sees only binary right/wrong on those and learns a sharp boundary; a soft-target student sees the teacher's graded uncertainty and learns the overlap. Falsification: if the soft-target student does not beat the hard-label one on held-out data, your teacher probabilities are too peaked (train with T > 1 to fix) or the two classes are not actually confusable.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Capacity gap. Symptom: student underperforms the teacher and underperforms a same-size model trained on hard labels. The teacher's distribution is too complex for the student's capacity; reduce T, raise α on hard labels, or distil from a smaller teacher.
  • Confidently wrong teacher. Symptom: the student reproduces a specific factual error or formatting habit of the teacher, faithfully and consistently. There is no mechanism to correct it — only a ground-truth anchor term (α > 0 with real labels) or a rejection criterion can.
  • Unpinned teacher. Symptom: you cannot reproduce the distilled model because the teacher was updated between data-generation runs, so the dataset is a mixture of several teachers. Record the checkpoint hash in the manifest.
  • Rejection-sampling bias. Symptom: the student only knows how to reason on problems the teacher can solve, and deployment traffic is harder. The keep-rate filter silently truncates the difficulty distribution — measure the difficulty distribution of kept vs attempted problems.
  • Distilling style, not capability. Symptom: the student's answers sound exactly like the teacher's but correctness is unchanged. Logit distillation transfers behaviour; if you want capability you need traces with verified correctness, not surface text.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why is the T² factor in the distillation loss, and what happens numerically if you omit it while raising T from 1 to 4?

Reveal answer

Soft-target gradients scale as 1/T² (because softmax logits are divided by T and then a gradient is taken). Without the T² multiplier the distillation term's effective weight falls by 16× when you move from T = 1 to T = 4, so the student silently becomes a hard-label-only model. Measured KL: 0.2184 at T = 1 vs 0.0152 at T = 4 — a 14× collapse in magnitude.

Q2. Teacher 284B, student 13B. Compute the training cost multiplier for logit distillation and state the formula you used.

Reveal answer

cost multiplier = 1 + N_T/(3N_S) = 1 + 284/(3×13) = 1 + 7.28 = 8.28×. The derivation: per token, teacher forward is 2N_T, student forward+backward is 6N_S, and plain training is 6N_S, so the ratio is (2N_T + 6N_S)/(6N_S) = 1 + N_T/(3N_S).

Q3. Diagnostic: a distilled student is fluent in the teacher's style but no more accurate. What went wrong, and what should you change?

Reveal answer

You distilled surface text (sequence distillation) rather than verified reasoning. Style transfers trivially from generated text; capability requires traces whose final answers were checked (rejection sampling against a verifier). Fix: generate many samples per problem, keep only verified-correct traces, and distil on those — accepting the keep-rate cost multiplier.

Q4. A rejection loop keeps 6% of teacher samples. How many traces must be generated for 500,000 accepted, and how does this compare to a 30% keep rate?

Reveal answer

At 6%: 500,000/0.06 = 8,333,334 generated. At 30%: 1,666,667 generated. So the low keep rate costs 5× more teacher generation for the same dataset. That is why problem selection (per-problem success estimates) matters more than model size in the pipeline — drop problems the teacher almost never solves rather than burning 16 samples each.

Q5. When is distillation the wrong tool compared to quantization?

Reveal answer

Quantization keeps the same architecture and costs nothing in data or teacher passes; distillation buys roughly 10–70× compression but requires a teacher, a data pipeline and 2.5–24× the training compute. Use quantization first — it is free throughput at a small accuracy risk that you can measure. Reach for distillation when the required size drop is larger than precision alone can deliver and you have a teacher you trust plus a verifiable way to keep traces.
