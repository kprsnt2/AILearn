L32 · DeepSeekMoE: Fine-Grained Experts + Shared Experts

Vanilla top-k MoE wastes capacity twice: a coarse expert either covers everything or nothing about a token, and every expert redundantly relearns grammar. Splitting experts finer multiplies the reachable combinations; adding shared always-on experts removes the redundancy.

~28 min difficulty ●●●●○ build: expert-split capacity model
fine-grained experts shared experts combinatorial capacity knowledge hybridity expert redundancy k/N ratio auxiliary-loss-free balancing

1 · Why it exists #

Two independent inefficiencies showed up in early sparse MoE training, and both were measured as loss you should have got.

Coarse experts waste the routing decision. With E = 8 experts and top-k = 2, there are only C(8,2) = 28 possible expert pairs a token can be assigned. That is a tiny menu. Every expert must therefore be a generalist: it has to be good at all the token types that might land on it, because the router cannot compose a specific mixture. Real token types are far more numerous than 28.

Redundant knowledge. Common structure — syntax, function words, basic reasoning patterns — is needed by essentially every token. In a vanilla MoE, every expert that sees many tokens learns it independently. You are buying the same knowledge E times over, and the specialists' capacity is partly spent on being generalists.

The fix, from DeepSeekMoE arXiv:2405.04434 and used in DeepSeek-V2 and V3 arXiv:2412.19437, is two structural moves: (1) fine-grained experts — many smaller experts with a larger k, and (2) shared experts — a small number of always-on experts that capture the common knowledge so the routed experts can specialise. Combined with auxiliary-loss-free balancing, this is the architecture family behind the 2026 DeepSeek line.

A MoE is not “E experts of the same size as a dense FFN.” The parameters that matter are how many distinct mixtures the router can express, and how much of each expert's capacity is spent on knowledge that every token already needs.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): mixing paint. Eight big cans of premixed colours with two-cans-per-token routing gives you 28 colours. Sixty-four small cans of pure-ish pigments with six-cans-per-token routing gives you C(64, 6) = 74,974,368 colours from roughly the same total volume of paint.

What the analogy gets right

Compositionality of the expert menu is combinatorial in how many you choose per token, not just in total capacity. Same paint volume, exponentially finer shades.

Where it breaks

Mixing paint is free; mixing experts is a weighted sum with fixed compute, so choosing k = 6 small experts costs the same FLOPs as choosing 2 big ones only if 6×small = 2×big in width. Also, and this is the reason shared experts exist: fine experts are worse at knowledge every token needs, because no single small expert sees enough tokens to learn it confidently.

The arithmetic of the split

The right quantity is the reachable mixture count, C(E, k), given a fixed total parameter budget:

Same parameter budget, same FLOPs per token, five orders of magnitude more expressive routing. That is the entire justification for fine-grained experts, and it costs nothing except a larger E (hence larger routing and all-to-all traffic).

3 · The mechanism #

y = ∑s∈S FFNs(x)  +  ∑i∈TopK(gi)  FFNi(x)  ,  gi = softmax(Wgx)i for i ∈ TopK only S = the small set of always-on shared experts (typically 1–2). The routed term is the standard top-k MoE. The shared term is a dense FFN that every token passes through, so common knowledge lives there once instead of in every routed expert.

Worked: split one coarse expert into four fine ones

Take dmodel = 4096 and a coarse expert of FFN width 8192. Its SwiGLU parameter count is 3 × d × w = 3 × 4096 × 8192 = 100,663,296 ≈ 0.1007B parameters. Now split it into 4 fine experts of width 2048: each is 3 × 4096 × 2048 = 25,165,824 ≈ 25.17M parameters, and four of them total 100,663,296 — exactly identical. Zero parameter cost.

Note the real trade-off, which is often glossed over: fine-grained experts do not give you more combinations for free. Splitting increases the menu but you must increase k to use it, and increasing k increases active parameters (and FLOPs) proportionally. What the split genuinely buys is that k experts of width w/4 can express a mixture that a fixed k×w/4 budget could not, because you are choosing which quarters, not just averaging. Whether that is worth it is an empirical question — the DeepSeekMoE finding is that at a fixed active budget, more granular experts that sum to the same width outperform fewer coarse ones.

The k/E ratio, stated plainly

For a fixed total routed parameter count Nrouted, the active fraction is exactly k/E. So:

Configurationk/EReading
E = 64, k = 60.094a token uses 9.4% of routed capacity
E = 160, k = 80.0505.0% — more total capacity, same or less active compute

Going from (64, 6) to (160, 8) at the same per-expert width multiplies total routed parameters by 160/64 = 2.5× and multiplies active routed parameters by 8/6 = 1.33× — so active per parameter falls from 9.4% to 5.0%. You buy 2.5× the knowledge for 1.33× the compute per token. That is the trade, and it is why 2026 frontier MoEs sit at 3–5% activation.

V4.1-Flash's asymmetry is the most interesting structural fact: the same model has two active-parameter counts because input processing and output generation are limited by different resources. It is the same principle as shared experts — do not pay for capacity you are not using in this phase — applied at the phase level instead of the expert level (L37).

Specify an MoE with five numbers or you cannot cost it: E, k, per-expert width, number of shared experts, and capacity factor. From those, Ntotal, Nactive, memory and per-token FLOPs are all deterministic. A spec sheet with only “total parameters” is a memory spec, not a model spec.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — mixture counts and why your intuition fails

Compute C(E, k) for the configurations people actually propose. The point is to see how badly sublinear human intuition is here, and at what E the combinatorial count stops being the binding constraint.

import math def comb(n, k): return math.comb(n, k) for E, k in [(8,2), (16,2), (64,6), (160,8), (256,8)]: c = comb(E, k) print(f"E={E:3d} k={k:2d} C(E,k)={c:,} active fraction k/E={k/E:.3f}")

Measured: E = 8,k = 2 → 28; E = 16,k = 2 → 120; E = 64,k = 6 → 74,974,368; E = 160,k = 8 → 8,917,061,687,820; E = 256,k = 8 → 409,663,695,276,000. Past E ≈ 160 the mixture count is astronomically larger than the number of distinct token types, so the binding constraint moves elsewhere — to per-expert batch size and to routing quality.

Experiment 2 — split a dense FFN into shared + routed under a fixed budget

The design calculation: given a target active FFN parameter count and a target total, solve for E, k and width. This is what you would actually do when sizing a model.

d = 4096 def expert_params(w): return 3 * d * w # dense baseline: one FFN of width 8192 print("dense w=8192 :", expert_params(8192)/1e6, "M params, all active") # fine-grained: 64 routed experts of width 1024, k=6, plus 2 shared of width 1024 w = 1024; E = 64; k = 6; ns = 2 tot = (E + ns) * expert_params(w) act = (k + ns) * expert_params(w) print(f"64r+2s w=1024: total={tot/1e9:.3f}B active={act/1e9:.3f}B ratio={act/tot:.3f}") # how many shared experts does it take to cover the dense baseline's active compute? for ns in (0, 1, 2, 4): act = (k + ns) * expert_params(w) print(f"ns={ns}: active={act/1e9:.3f}B ({act/expert_params(8192)/1e9:.2f}x a w=8192 dense FFN)")

Measured: dense w = 8192 is 100,663,296 ≈ 0.1007B params, all active. With 64 routed + 2 shared experts of width 1024, total is 0.830B and active is 0.1007B — an active/total ratio of 0.121, and an active count that is exactly 1.00× the dense baseline (0.101B vs 0.1007B). So the fine-grained design holds the dense model's compute fixed while growing memory 8.2× (0.830 / 0.1007). Each shared expert adds 12.58M active params; the “ns” rows show 0, 1, 2, 4 shared experts giving 0.75×, 0.88×, 1.00×, 1.25× the dense active count.

Experiment 3 — what redundant knowledge would cost without shared experts

Price the redundancy directly. If a fraction f of each routed expert's capacity is spent on knowledge every token needs, the model is paying f×E× for it. This is the number shared experts eliminate.

python -c " E, w, d = 64, 1024, 4096 per = 3*d*w for f in (0.05, 0.15, 0.30): redundant = f*E*per print(f'if {f:.0%} of each expert is common knowledge: {redundant/1e9:.2f}B params paid {E}x for the same thing') print('one shared expert of width 1024 =', per/1e6, 'M params, learned once') "

With width 1024 (12,582,912 params per expert) and 64 routed experts: at 5% redundancy you pay 40.3M parameters for duplicated knowledge; at 15%, 120.8M; at 30%, 241.6M. One shared expert of the same width is 12.58M params, learned once. The ratio is simply redundancy fraction × E: at 15% and E = 64 you spend 9.6 experts' worth of capacity on the same knowledge twice over. This is the quantitative case for shared experts, and it is why the DeepSeekMoE ablation isolates redundancy as a major loss term on its own.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Splitting without raising k. Symptom: you compare a fine-grained model to a coarse one at the same k and it loses — because you cut each expert's width by Enew/Eold without restoring the active budget. The comparison is invalid; always hold active parameters constant when evaluating granularity.
  • Too many shared experts. Symptom: the model behaves like a dense model of (k + |S|) experts and routed specialisation never develops; routed experts look interchangeable in the load histogram. Shared experts are a minority by design.
  • Routing overhead at large E. Symptom: throughput drops as E grows even though active FLOPs are constant — the router matmul (d_model × E), the sort over E scores and the all-to-all volume per token (k×d) grow with E and k. At E = 256 with k = 8 the communication volume is 4× that of k = 2.
  • Expert-parallel imbalance. Symptom: with E = 160 spread over 8 or 16 GPUs, one GPU holds experts that happen to be hot and becomes the straggler. Per-rank timing variance, not global average load, is the metric.
  • Reproducing from a paper's E and k without its width. Symptom: your parameter counts do not match the published total/active pair even though you used the same E and k. E and k alone do not determine size — width and the count of dense layers do, and papers differ on whether embeddings are counted.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. One coarse expert of width 8192 is split into four fine experts of width 2048. What is the parameter change, and what must you change about routing to exploit the split?

Reveal answer

Coarse: 3×4096×8192 = 100,663,296. Four fine: 4 × 3×4096×2048 = 100,663,296. Exactly zero parameter change. To exploit it you must raise k so a token can take more than one fine expert; with k = 1 there are 4 mixtures versus 1, but the expert each token sees is narrower, so the net effect is not automatically positive. The gain comes from choosing different subsets at a fixed total active width.

Q2. A model has 160 routed experts (width 2048, d = 4096), k = 8, plus 2 shared experts. Compute total routed parameters, active routed parameters, and the active fraction of routed capacity.

Reveal answer

Per expert: 3 × 4096 × 2048 = 25,165,824 ≈ 25.17M. Total routed: 160 × 25.17M = 4.027B. Active routed: 8 × 25.17M = 201.3M. Active fraction = 8/160 = 5.0%. Shared experts add 2 × 25.17M = 50.3M always-active. So the FFN sublayer of one layer is 4.03B of memory for 251.7M of work — a 16× gap at the layer level.

Q3. Diagnostic: your fine-grained MoE has a flat load histogram, healthy entropy, and lower quality than the coarse baseline at equal active FLOPs. Name two candidate causes.

Reveal answer

(a) The active budget was not held constant — narrower experts with the same k means less total active width, so you are comparing a smaller model. (b) Too few training tokens for the granularity: more experts means each sees fewer tokens, so specialisation cannot form; the fix is more data or fewer experts. A third possibility is routing overhead being paid in dropped tokens because capacity was computed for the old E.

Q4. Why do shared experts specifically help fine-grained MoE more than coarse MoE?

Reveal answer

Fine-grained experts are narrow, so each sees fewer tokens and has less capacity to spend on common knowledge; without a shared expert, the redundancy cost scales with E and the specialisation quality collapses because every small expert must still be a generalist. In a coarse MoE, each expert is large enough to absorb common knowledge cheaply, so the shared-expert gain is smaller. The two design moves are complementary, not independent.

Q5. Your spec says “284B total, 13B active.” List the three numbers you still need to reproduce its cost model, and what each decides.

Reveal answer

(1) Layer count and hidden size — needed for KV cache and activation memory; (2) KV-cache scheme and per-token cache bytes (MLA vs GQA vs token-wise compression) — usually the dominant term at long context (L33); (3) expert-parallel topology and interconnect bandwidth — decides whether all-to-all is affordable. Total/active alone tells you memory and per-token FLOPs but not concurrency, latency or throughput.
