L36 · Multi-Token Prediction: Training Signal and Speculation

A standard LM gets exactly one supervision signal per forward pass, at the last position of each token. Multi-token prediction makes the model defend its future predictions too — and the same extra heads become a speculative decoder you did not have to train separately.

MTP objective sequential vs parallel heads training signal density speculative decoding draft acceptance rate expected tokens per step planning in latent space

1 · Why it exists #

Next-token prediction has a subtle inefficiency that is invisible on a loss curve and expensive at scale: at each position the model produces a full vocabulary distribution, and that entire distribution is trained by a single cross-entropy term against a single next token. The forward pass computed an enormous amount of state and used almost all of it for one bit of supervision.

Worse, it teaches a local objective with no pressure toward global coherence. A model can be excellent at predicting the next token and still drift: it has never been asked whether its plan for the next few tokens was realisable. Errors compound because nothing in the objective penalises a locally-good, globally-bad continuation.

The second, more commercial failure is decoding speed. Autoregressive generation produces one token per forward pass, and at long context that pass is bandwidth-bound (L35), so most of the GPU sits idle while a whole cache is streamed to produce one token. Speculative decoding fixes this, but the standard form needs a separate draft model with matched tokenizer, aligned training data, and a second serving stack.

Multi-token prediction (MTP), used in DeepSeek-V3 arXiv:2412.19437, addresses both at once: extra prediction heads trained on tokens t+2, t+3… densify the training signal, and those same heads serve as the draft for speculative decoding at inference.

One head per output token is a choice, not a law. Adding heads buys (a) a denser training signal and (b) a free speculative draft — for a modest, computable increase in training compute and a small increase in parameters.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): a chess player who must not only make a move but declare the next two moves in advance. If they must commit, they choose plans that survive contact — the added commitment changes which move they pick, not just how they describe it.

What the analogy gets right

Extra commitment forces a representation that is predictive further ahead. A plan that is only locally good becomes costly, so the model prefers continuations with stable futures. That is the “planning in latent space” argument.

Where it breaks

A player who declares two moves and then abandons them is not penalised in real chess. In MTP the model is never forced to use its own draft; the extra heads are a training signal plus an optional decoder, and the main head stays the authority. So the cognitive benefit is a side effect of the auxiliary gradients, not an architectural constraint.

Two ways to place the heads

DesignStructureCostDraft quality
Parallel headsD independent heads all read the same final hidden state and predict t+1..t+DD extra small heads; cheap, fully parallelWeak at t+2 and beyond: the draft conditions cannot depend on the predicted t+1 actual token
Sequential headsEach extra head takes the previous head's hidden state plus the embedding of the token it just predictedD small transformer blocks + the causal chain at inferenceStronger: each head conditions on the actual drafted prefix, which is what speculation needs

DeepSeek-V3 uses the sequential form. The difference matters for speculation: a draft of t+2 is only useful if it is conditioned on the t+1 token that will actually be accepted, and only the sequential chain provides that at draft time.

3 · The mechanism #

&lagr; = ∑d=1D λd · CE( pθ(· | x≤t, drafts), xt+d ) d = 1 is the ordinary next-token loss; d = 2..D are the MTP losses, each with its own weight λd and its own head. D is typically small (2 or 3) because each extra head has diminishing returns and increases training compute.

Cost: parameters and training compute

For a model with dmodel = 7168, an extra MTP head is roughly a transformer block's worth of computation: attention projections and MLP are ≈ 4·d2 parameters for a GLU MLP of width 4d is 3·d·4d = 12d2, so order 101·d2 per head. With d = 7168, 4d2 = 2.06×108 = 0.206B parameters for a minimal block, and the LM head is shared with the main head (output embeddings tie).

The +10%-per-head figure is an estimate from block size, not a published number; treat it as the order of magnitude and measure the actual ratio on your own architecture. What matters is the shape: MTP cost is linear in D and small compared to the main model, which is why it is affordable at all.

Benefit 1: expected tokens per decoding step

If the model drafts a chain of D tokens and the main model verifies them, the number of tokens accepted per forward pass is the number of draft tokens that pass verification plus one from the main model. With independent per-token acceptance probability a, the expected accepted-token count for a chain of length D is

E[tokens/step] = 1 + a + a² + … + aD The 1 is the token the main model always contributes; each drafted token is accepted with probability a and must also have had all earlier tokens accepted, hence the powers. This is the standard speculative-decoding expectation under the assumption of a perfectly aligned draft distribution.

The margin on the third drafted token is small — going from D = 2 to D = 3 at a = 0.8 adds 0.512 tokens per step (2.952 − 2.440) but costs another head and another sequential verification. That is why D stays at 2 or 3 and why a is the number to optimise, not D. A draft with a = 0.9 outperforms a longer draft with a = 0.6 at every D.

Note the caveat hidden in that table: these are the gains if verification is free. Verification of D drafted tokens is one batched forward pass through the main model, which costs roughly the same as one ordinary forward pass when D is small, but is not actually free at long context because the cache for D positions must be written and read.

The three numbers that make an MTP claim auditable: D, the measured acceptance rate a per draft position (not just position 1), and the verification overhead in ms. Reporting a “2.4× speedup” without a is reporting a table lookup without its input.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — the acceptance-rate table, computed

The whole economics of speculative decoding in one loop. Run it before believing any speedup claim, including your own.

python -c " for a in (0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9,0.95): row=[sum(a**i for i in range(D+1)) for D in (1,2,3,4)] print(f'a={a:.2f}: ' + ' '.join(f'D={D}:{v:.3f}' for D,v in enumerate(row,1))) "

At a = 0.5, D = 4 gives 1.938 tokens/step — barely better than D = 1's 1.5 while costing four heads and four verifications. At a = 0.95, D = 2 gives 2.853 and D = 4 gives 4.524. The crossover where extra depth pays is a ≳ 0.7. Falsification: if your measured speedup exceeds E[tokens/step], the verifier is batching more than you think or the measurement is wrong.

Experiment 2 — 2-token prediction on a Markov toy

Confirm that an MTP objective really does change what the first prediction learns, not just add a second output. The toy is a 2-state Markov chain where predicting t+2 requires committing to the transition pattern.

import numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(36) # a corpus where every 'A' is followed by 'B' then 'C', every 'X' by 'Y' then 'Z' seq = [] for _ in range(2000): seq += [0, 1, 2] if rng.random() < 0.5 else [3, 4, 5] seq = np.array(seq) def bigram(tokens, order=1): m = {} for i in range(len(tokens) - order): ctx = tuple(tokens[i:i+order]); m.setdefault(ctx, np.zeros(6)) m[ctx][tokens[i+order]] += 1 return {k: v / v.sum() for k, v in m.items()} unigram = np.bincount(seq, minlength=6) / len(seq) bi = bigram(seq, 1) print("unigram entropy :", -np.sum(unigram*np.log2(unigram)).round(3)) for ctx, p in sorted(bi.items()): print(ctx, "->", np.round(p, 2), " H=", -np.sum(p[p>0]*np.log2(p[p>0])).round(3))

Unigram entropy is 2.585 bits. Conditional on a single token the next is deterministic (H = 0) for tokens 0, 1, 3 and 4; from token 2 and its partner 5 the next is a coin flip (H = 1.0 bit) — which is exactly the position where a 1-step model cannot tell which chain follows, while the token at t+2 is fully determined by the chain we are in. The MTP head on t+2 forces the model's representation of position t to encode which chain we are in — information the 1-step objective never needed. That is the measurable effect to look for: a rise in linear-probe accuracy for t+2 identity at hidden layer t.

Experiment 3 — when MTP loses money

Speculation pays only if the draft is cheap relative to the verifier. In an MoE, drafting with the full model is not cheap — quantify the condition under which the extra heads are a net loss.

def speedup(a, D, draft_cost_ratio): # one step: draft D tokens sequentially (D * ratio of a full pass), then verify in one pass tokens = sum(a**i for i in range(D+1)) cost = D * draft_cost_ratio + 1.0 return tokens / cost for ratio in (0.05, 0.2, 0.5): for D, a in [(1, 0.9), (2, 0.8), (2, 0.6)]: print(f"draft_ratio={ratio} D={D} a={a:.1f} speedup={speedup(a,D,ratio):.3f}x")

With a cheap draft (ratio 0.05) the D = 2, a = 0.8 configuration gives 2.44/1.10 = 2.218×. With a draft costing half a full pass the same configuration gives 2.44/2.00 = 1.220×; and at a = 0.6 with ratio 0.5 it collapses to 1.960/2.00 = 0.980× — a slowdown. The extra heads must be much cheaper than the main forward pass, which is why MTP heads are small and why long-context acceptance rates matter so much.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Draft acceptance measured only at position 1. Symptom: claimed speedup is 1.8× but users see 1.2×. Acceptance falls off sharply with chain depth; report a1, a2, a3 separately, because the benefit is the sum of their products.
  • Draft trained on pre-training data, verified on agent traces. Symptom: acceptance is good on prose and poor on JSON, code and tool-call markup — exactly the outputs an agent produces most of. Acceptance must be measured per workload, not in aggregate.
  • Verification overhead at long context. Symptom: speculation helps at short prompts and hurts past 32k, because the verifier must write D extra cache entries and read the whole cache for D positions. Measure net tokens/second, not acceptance rate.
  • MTP loss weight competing with the main objective. Symptom: the main head's loss is slightly worse than a matched baseline while the MTP loss looks great. λd too high; the extra heads are starving the primary signal.
  • Assuming MTP improves quality unconditionally. Symptom: a well-tuned baseline beats the MTP variant on downstream evals despite a better pre-training loss. The gains reported for MTP are relative to a specific baseline and data regime; re-validate on your own task rather than importing a multiplier.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. With acceptance a = 0.75 and D = 2, what is the expected number of tokens per decoding step, and what is it with D = 1?

Reveal answer

D = 2: 1 + 0.75 + 0.5625 = 2.3125 tokens/step. D = 1: 1.75. So the second draft adds 0.5625 tokens per step — a 32% increase over D = 1 for one extra head and one extra sequential draft pass. Whether that pays depends entirely on the draft cost ratio from Experiment 3.

Q2. A draft costs 15% of a full forward pass. With D = 2 and a = 0.85, compute the speedup including draft cost.

Reveal answer

E[tokens] = 1 + 0.85 + 0.7225 = 2.5725. Cost = 2 × 0.15 + 1.0 = 1.30 passes per step. Speedup = 2.5725/1.30 = 1.979×. Note that the naive “tokens per step” figure of 2.57 overstates it by 30% — this is the most common overclaim in speculative-decoding write-ups.

Q3. Diagnostic: acceptance rate is 0.9 on Wikitext and 0.55 on your agent's tool-call traces. What is happening and what do you do?

Reveal answer

The draft head was trained on a corpus whose distribution favours prose; tool-call traces are highly structured, low-entropy but with rare exact tokens (IDs, JSON keys, paths) where a draft is likely to guess wrong. The higher the structure the less helpful a probabilistic draft and the more helpful grammars/constrained decoding (L18). Fix: include agent traces in the MTP training mixture, or switch to constrained decoding for the structured segments.

Q4. Why does the sequential head design need the token it just drafted as input, while the parallel design does not, and why does the difference matter at verification time?

Reveal answer

Sequential heads condition on the drafted prefix, which is what the verifier will actually check against; parallel heads condition only on the original hidden state and predict t+2 without knowing the t+1 that will be accepted. At verification time the parallel draft for t+2 may be conditioned on the wrong prefix, so its acceptance rate is systematically lower for the second and third tokens — the exact positions where speculative gain is already thin.

Q5. Give the honest accounting of what MTP costs a pre-training run and what it buys.

Reveal answer

Costs: D extra small transformer blocks (≈0.2B params each at d = 7168 for a minimal block), roughly +10% training compute per head, and a slightly more complex data pipeline (needs targets at t+2..t+D). Buys: a denser training signal which is reported to improve pre-training efficiency, plus a draft for speculative decoding with no separate model. The training-efficiency gain is the harder claim to isolate — to prove it you need matched-compute baselines, which most write-ups do not provide.
