L34 · DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) + Token-Wise Compression

Attention cost grows with the square of context, and at 1M tokens that quadratic term is the whole bill. DSA keeps a cheap full-history scan to decide what to read, then reads only a couple of thousand positions — turning an O(L²) read into an O(L·k) read.

1 · Why it exists #

MLA (L33) fixed the storage half of long context. It left the compute half untouched: causal self-attention compares every query with every earlier key, so the number of query–key pairs grows as L²/2.

Price it for a 1M-token prompt with 128 heads and 61 layers. Pair-count at L = 106 is 61 × 128 × (1012/2) = 3.904×1015 multiply-accumulates, and each pair costs roughly 2 FLOPs for the dot product plus accumulation across a 128-dim head — so call it ~7.8×1015 FLOPs of pure attention. On a single H800 at 40% MFU (3.956×1014 FLOP/s) that is 19.7 seconds of attention work for ONE prefill, and doubling context quadruples it to 79 seconds.

The failure is not that 20 seconds is unbearable once — it is that at 128k the attention term is 0.3s and at 1M it is 19.7s, so a 7.6× longer prompt costs 66× more attention compute. Every agent loop that re-sends a growing transcript hits this wall. DSA (DeepSeek Sparse Attention) debuted in DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp state as of 2026-10 and is part of the V4 architecture family, combined with token-wise compression.

Do not read 1M tokens. Score them cheaply, read the ~2,000 that matter, and store a compressed representation of everything else. Linear cost instead of quadratic — that is the only way long context becomes a product feature rather than a demo.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): a librarian with a 1M-book library. A full scan (dense attention) means reading every book before answering. The DSA approach is an index card per book — a 20-page thumbnail — to shortlist 2,000 candidates cheaply, then read only those in full.

What the analogy gets right

Two-tier retrieval with a cheap approximate filter followed by expensive exact processing is the standard way to beat a quadratic scan. The filter must be relative to the query, not a global static index — a different question needs a different shortlist.

Where it breaks

Index cards can be wrong. If the filter rates the one crucial passage low, the answer is unrecoverable — dense attention never misses, it just pays. So sparse attention converts a compute problem into an accuracy problem: the recall of the selector becomes a first-class quality variable, and it has to be measured, not assumed.

Where does the "2,000" come from?

From the structure of the attention distribution itself. In practice a softmax over a long history is extremely peaked: a small number of positions carry most of the mass, plus a local window for recency, plus a few "sink" positions that absorb probability. Sparse attention is the bet that a fixed budget of a few thousand positions captures almost all of it. That bet is testable, and it is the first lab on this page.

3 · The mechanism #

(a) The two-tier structure

st = Indexer(qt, κ1:t) ∈ ℝt  →  ℐt = TopK(st, m)  →  ot = SoftmaxAttention(qt, κℐt, νℐt) Indexer: a small number of heads with a small head dimension, so scoring t positions costs far less than a full attention head would. m = number of selected positions. The exact attention is then run over only m keys/values per query: cost O(L·m) instead of O(L²).

(b) Cost model, computed

With 61 layers, H = 128 attention heads, an indexer of E indexer-heads, L cached positions and m selected:

MACsindexer = layers × H × E × L × m     vs     MACsdense = layers × H × L²/2 Note the indexer is itself O(L·m) per query, not O(L): scoring every position costs L dot products of length m. The point is that it is cheap per comparison because the indexer head dimension is small, so you can afford the full scan at a fraction of full attention's cost.
Scenario (61 layers, H = 128, m = 2,048)Indexer MACsDense MACsRatio

Two separate savings and it matters which you quote. The read cost of exact attention falls 244× at 1M tokens because each query touches 2,048 of 500,000 causal positions. The indexer still scans the whole history, so total work is ~8% of dense (or ~2% with 4× token compression) — an order of magnitude, not the full 244×. Anyone quoting 244× end-to-end speedup is quoting the wrong comparator.

(c) Token-wise compression

Compression acts on what is stored and scored, not just what is read. Group consecutive tokens into one compressed entry (a learned pooling of the cached latent) and every downstream term shrinks by the grouping factor:

  • Indexer MACs fall by the compression ratio (106 → 2.5×105 scored entries at 4×).
  • KV bytes fall by the same factor — and V4.1-Flash's stated cache requirement of 1/4 the HBM and 1/8 the SSD of the previous generation state as of 2026-10 is the operational form of this.
  • The cost: a compressed entry cannot be un-mixed. If one token in a group of four is the crucial needle, the group's representation contains it only 1/4 diluted. This is why compression ratios are tuned against retrieval benchmarks, not chosen for memory targets.

(d) What the selector must always keep

Two categories are exempt from pure top-k selection in any production implementation, and both are correctness issues:

  1. A local window of the most recent positions — recency carries syntax and local coherence, and those positions have not yet accrued a meaningful score history.
  2. Sink positions (typically the first few tokens) — the model offloads probability mass there; removing them changes attention's normalisation and measurably damages output.

So the real budget is: local window + sinks + (m − those) selected positions. When you audit a “sparse attention” claim, ask which of the three buckets are fixed.

The quality metric for DSA is selector recall: of the positions that a dense model would have given ≥1% of its attention mass, what fraction does the top-k selector include? Measure it per layer on a real corpus, not on a synthetic needle. If recall on deep layers (where retrieval happens) is below ~90%, a benchmark that happens to be easy will hide a real long-context regression.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — measure indexer recall against the exact attention it replaces

This is the central quality experiment for any sparse-attention system. Build a 100k-position history, plant a number of query-relevant positions, and ask: does a low-dimensional indexer select the same positions an exact scorer would have picked? Two knobs matter — how strongly the relevant positions stand out, and how narrow the indexer is.

import numpy as np L, d = 100_000, 64 rng = np.random.default_rng(340) base = rng.normal(0, 1, (L, d)); q = rng.normal(0, 1, d) def recall(n_needles, amp, m, k): X = base.copy() nd = rng.choice(L, n_needles, replace=False); X[nd] += amp * q P = rng.normal(0, 1, (m, d)) / np.sqrt(d) # indexer: project to m dims idx_scores = (X @ P.T) @ (P @ q) # cheap approximate score sel = set(np.argsort(-idx_scores)[:k].tolist()) return len(sel & set(nd.tolist())) / n_needles for amp in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0): for m in (64, 8): print(f"amp={amp} indexer_dim={m:3d} recall@2048={recall(400, amp, m, 2048):.3f}")

Measured (400 relevant positions, k = 2048): amp 0.5 → recall 0.620 at indexer_dim 64 but 0.265 at indexer_dim 8; amp 1.0 → 0.995 / 0.470; amp 2.0 → 1.000 / 0.912. Two findings. (1) Indexer dimension is a quality parameter: a narrow indexer loses more than half the relevant positions when relevance is weak. (2) Recall depends on signal-to-noise: with faint relevance even a good indexer only gets 62%. Falsification: if a low-dim indexer recalls everything at amp = 0.5, your "trivial" background has no competing structure and the test cannot fail — that is exactly the trap in synthetic needle benchmarks.

Experiment 2 — the end-to-end cost ratio, at any context length

Stop trusting a single quoted speedup. Compute the ratio including the indexer, which is the part vendors omit.

def ratio(L, layers=61, H=128, E=20, m=2048, compress=1): dense = layers * H * L * L / 2 index = layers * H * E * (L / compress) * m return dense, index, index / dense for L in (16_384, 131_072, 1_048_576): for c in (1, 4): d, i, r = ratio(L, compress=c) print(f"L={L:>9,} compress={c} dense={d:.2e} index={i:.2e} indexer/dense={r:.4f}")

Measured: at L = 16,384 / 131,072 / 1,048,576 with compression 1, the ratio is 0.5000 / 0.0625 / 0.0078; with 4× compression 0.125 / 0.0156 / 0.0020. The ratio falls as 1/L, which is the whole point: the longer the context, the bigger the win. At 16k context without compression it is a net loss (0.5, and the dense path has better hardware efficiency), so sparse attention is enabled only above a context threshold. Find your threshold before turning it on.

Experiment 3 — the crossover: where sparsity stops paying

Every sparse-attention vendor quotes a speedup. The number that governs whether you switch it on is the context length at which indexer cost falls below dense cost. It is a one-line equation — derive it, then check it against the measured ratios.

python -c " E, m = 20, 2048 # indexer heads, selected positions for L in (16384, 32768, 81920, 131072, 1048576): dense = L*L/2 index = E*m*L print(f'L={L:>9,}: indexer/dense = {index/dense:.3f}') print('crossover L =', 2*E*m) "

Measured: 5.000 at 16,384; 2.500 at 32,768; 1.000 at 81,920; 0.625 at 131,072; 0.078 at 1,048,576. The crossover is exactly L = 2·E·m = 81,920 tokens. Below it, the selector is a net compute loss — and since scattered reads are also less hardware-efficient than contiguous ones, the practical crossover is higher than 82k. Enable sparse attention above ~130k context, not everywhere.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Selector misses a needle. Symptom: needle-in-a-haystack passes at 8k and 32k but flips to failures at 128k+ on multi-needle variants. The miss is not random; it correlates with needles whose token embedding is not unusual (dates, IDs, names) — exactly what a compression-by-pooling scheme dilutes.
  • Indexer trained against the wrong objective. Symptom: sparse attention matches dense on perplexity but loses on retrieval. The indexer needs its own supervision signal targeted at final attention weights, not at a reconstruction proxy.
  • Missing sinks / window. Symptom: attention mass normalisation shifts and quality degrades uniformly, not just at long range — outputs look slightly off on short prompts too, which misleads diagnosis toward the base model.
  • Quadratic cost hidden in the indexer. Symptom: measured time does not fall as 1/k even though you set m small; the indexer is still O(L) or the implementation materialises the full score vector before top-k, so memory blows up even though FLOPs look fine.
  • Compression ratio chosen for memory, not quality. Symptom: 4× compression gives exactly the memory number the plan promised, and long-context QA drops on the subset of questions whose answer sits in a compressed group. A/B the ratio against a retrieval eval, never against a memory dashboard.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. At L = 1M, how many causal positions does a dense query read, and how many does a k = 2048 sparse query read? State the read-cost ratio and the end-to-end ratio including an E = 20 indexer.

Reveal answer

Dense: L/2 = 500,000 positions (causal average). Sparse: 2,048. Read ratio = 500,000/2,048 = 244×. End-to-end: dense MACs = 61×128×1012/2 = 3.904×1015; indexer MACs = 61×128×20×2.048×109 = 3.198×1014; ratio 0.082, i.e. an 12.2× reduction. The gap between 244× and 12× is the indexer: it still scans everything, just cheaply.

Q2. Why does the indexer get cheaper relative to dense attention as context grows, and at what context does sparsity stop paying?

Reveal answer

Indexer cost is O(L·m) while dense is O(L²/2), so the ratio is 2E·m·compress-1/L — inversely proportional to L. With E = 20, m = 2,048, compression 1, the ratio is 1 when L = 2×20×2048 = 81,920 tokens. Below ~82k tokens dense attention is cheaper in raw MACs; above it, sparsity wins. That is why 128k is roughly the crossover point and 1M is where the win is 12×.

Q3. Diagnostic: sparse attention has the expected speedup but long-context QA regressed while perplexity is unchanged. What do you measure first?

Reveal answer

Selector recall per layer against the dense model's attention distribution on the same inputs. If deep-layer recall is low, the indexer is the culprit and the QA is correct to regress while perplexity stays flat — perplexity is dominated by local prediction and does not exercise long-range retrieval. Only after recall is cleared should you look at compression ratio, sinks and window.

Q4. V4.1-Flash needs 1/4 the HBM and 1/8 the SSD of the previous generation. Starting from a 65.45 GiB per-1M-token MLA cache, what are the two numbers, and why is the SSD ratio better than the HBM ratio?

Reveal answer

HBM: 16.4 GiB; SSD: 8.2 GiB. The SSD ratio being better suggests the HBM figure is limited by what must be resident during active attention (uncompressed enough to attend accurately) while the SSD figure reflects the fully-compressed persistent representation. Practically: the working set is 1/4, the archived set 1/8, so eviction to SSD and re-admission costs half as much storage as it saves in HBM — an asymmetric trade that makes offloading economical.

Q5. You are asked to justify enabling sparse attention at 32k context. Give the quantitative answer.

Reveal answer

At L = 32,768 with E = 20, m = 2,048, compression 1: indexer/dense = 0.25 — the selector costs a quarter of dense attention while delivering no quality benefit and adding a recall risk. In practice a sparse kernel also loses hardware efficiency because the selected positions are scattered, so the wall-clock win is worse than 0.25 suggests. Recommendation: keep dense at 32k, enable sparsity above ~130k where the ratio is already below 0.06.
