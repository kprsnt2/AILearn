L26 · Kaplan Scaling Laws: Loss Is a Power Law

A $30M pretraining run that turns out to be three points short of the target is not a research failure, it is a forecasting failure — and forecasting was possible for $50k. This page gives you the equation that lets you predict loss from compute before you spend it.

~28 min difficulty ●●●○○ build: fit your own tiny scaling law
power law compute frontier C = 6ND N_opt ∝ Ca sublinear returns extrapolation risk isoFLOP slices

1 · Why it exists #

Pretraining is the only part of the LLM pipeline where the unit of failure is a rounding error in the budget. The largest runs cost tens of millions of dollars and lock 10,000+ GPUs for months. You get one shot per budget cycle. Before 2020 the decision — how big, how much data, how long — was folklore passed between labs.

The forcing failure: every lab that trained a large model had to answer “is the next 10× worth it?” with no evidence. Teams would pick a size because a competitor shipped that size. When the run finished above target, there was no way to know whether the failure was architectural, data-related, or simply under-allocated compute — and the money was already gone.

Kaplan et al., Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models arXiv:2001.08361, replaced folklore with a measurable law: cross-entropy loss on held-out text falls as a power law in model size, dataset size, and compute, over more than six orders of magnitude, and the law fitted on models small enough to train in a day predicted models that cost a thousand times more.

Loss is a smooth, predictable function of how much you spend. So “will this run hit the target?” is an arithmetic question answerable with 1é-2 scale models, not a gamble you discover the answer to at month three.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy: loss is a land value that drops as you move away from the city, and each order of magnitude of compute moves you one fixed fraction of the remaining distance closer. Not one fixed number of kilometres — a fraction. That distinction is the whole page: diminishing absolute returns, constant relative returns.

Distance to the irreducible floor shrinks by a constant factor per decade of compute. So going from 1022 to 1023 FLOPs (10× the compute) buys the same multiplicative improvement as going from 1025 to 1026 — but in absolute loss points, the early decade buys far more than the late one.

What the analogy gets right

Constant relative return per decade of spend. Adding a fixed multiplicative budget buys a predictable multiplicative loss improvement, which is why forecasting works at all.

Where it breaks

The floor is not zero and is unknown; the exponent changes when you change architecture, data mixture or tokenizer. A scaling law is only valid inside the family you fitted — swap in a new architecture (MoE, MLA, a different data mix) and the coefficients are dead.

The one number everyone forgets: C = 6ND

Training compute for a dense transformer is approximately 6 × (parameters) × (training tokens). The 6 is 2 FLOPs forward + 4 FLOPs backward per parameter per token — the standard multiplier, and the sentence “about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token” is the single most reused fact in this track. Backward is 2× forward because each linear layer needs a gradient with respect to its input and its weights.

3 · The mechanism #

Kaplan's three laws, in the form the paper fits them (N = non-embedding parameters, D = tokens, C = FLOPs):

L(N) = (Nc / N)αN    L(D) = (Dc / D)αD    L(C) = (Cc / C)αC L is nats-per-token; Nc, Dc, Cc are fitted constants; each is a straight line on a log-log plot. Kaplan reports αN≈0.076, αD≈0.095, αC≈0.050 in the paper's own fit.

Because α is small, the predictions are shockingly insensitive to the constant and shockingly sensitive to the exponent. Take the paper's reported Cc ≈ 2.3×108 and αC = 0.05, and predict the loss at 1025 FLOPs:

# predict loss at C = 1e25 FLOPs from Kaplan's reported fit constants C_c, alpha = 2.3e8, 0.05 for C in (1e21, 1e23, 1e25): L = (C_c / C) ** alpha print(f"C={C:.0e} L={L:.4f} nats/token ({L/0.6931:.3f} bits/token)") # C=1e+21 L=0.2334 nats/token (0.337 bits/token) # C=1e+23 L=0.1854 nats/token (0.267 bits/token) # C=1e+25 L=0.1473 nats/token (0.212 bits/token)

Read the spacing: from 1021 to 1025 FLOPs — a 10,000× increase in spend — loss falls from 0.2334 to 0.1473, a 37% relative cut but only 0.086 nats absolute. Every 100× of compute multiplies loss by 100-0.05 = 0.7943, i.e. it closes 1 − 0.7943 = 20.6% of the distance to zero; a 1000× bump multiplies loss by 1000-0.05 = 0.7079 and closes 29.2%. Note the fit constants are from the paper's reported fit and give the right shape; absolute nats depend on the paper's “nats/token” convention.

How you actually fit one

You do not read coefficients out of a paper and trust them — they are stale the week the architecture changes. You fit them on your own family. The procedure:

  1. Train 6–10 small models spanning at least 2 decades of compute (e.g. 2M to 200M parameters) on the same data, tokenizer and LR schedule shape.
  2. Record loss at a fixed token-per-parameter ratio so you are not confounding N and D.
  3. Fit L = (Nc/N)^aN by least squares in log-log space — the fit is linear there, so `numpy.polyfit` on log N vs log L is exactly the right tool.
  4. Validate: hold out the largest model, predict it from the smaller ones, and report the error. That error is your forecast uncertainty.

A scaling-law forecast with no held-out validation is a rumour. The audit artifact is: n runs, the fitted (Cc, α), the held-out model's predicted vs actual loss, and the resulting error in loss points. Save the checkpoints and seeds — if you can't retrain the 8th point you can't prove the law was fitted, not tuned.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — fit a power law to synthetic ground truth and recover the exponent

You need to know whether your fitter is honest before you point it at $50k of training. Here the truth is known: α = 0.076. If your recovery is off by more than ~10%, the fitter or the data is wrong.

import numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(26) N = np.logspace(6.3, 9.0, 9) # 2M .. 1B params Nc, aN = 8.8e13, 0.076 L_true = (Nc / N) ** aN L_obs = L_true * (1 + rng.normal(0, 0.004, N.size)) # 0.4% measurement noise slope, icpt = np.polyfit(np.log(N), np.log(L_obs), 1) print(f"recovered alpha_N = {-slope:.4f} (truth 0.076)") print(f"implied N_c = {np.exp(icpt/slope):.3e}") # held-out extrapolation: fit on the 8 smallest, predict the 9th s2, i2 = np.polyfit(np.log(N[:-1]), np.log(L_obs[:-1]), 1) pred = np.exp(i2 + s2 * np.log(N[-1])) print(f"holdout N={N[-1]:.3e} predicted {pred:.4f} actual {L_obs[-1]:.4f} err {abs(pred-L_obs[-1]):.5f}")

Recovered α comes out at ~0.0745 (vs truth 0.076 — within the ~0.003 you get from 0.4% noise), and holdout error is ~0.004 nats. What would falsify it: a recovered exponent materially different from 0.076, or holdout error growing with model size — that second symptom means the law is not a power law in your family and extrapolation is unsafe.

Experiment 2 — the “how much does 10× buy” calculator

The question your VP asks. Answer it with two logarithms, not a slide deck.

python -c " for a in (0.076, 0.05): print(f'alpha={a}: each 10x compute multiplies loss by {10**-a:.4f}, ' f'closes {100*(1-10**-a):.2f}% of remaining distance, ' f'1000x closes {100*(1-1000**-a):.2f}%') "

For α = 0.05: each 10× buys 10.87% of the remaining distance and a 1000× budget bump buys only 29.21%. If someone proposes a 10× run to fix a quality complaint, this printout is either the rebuttal or the justification — in numbers.

Experiment 3 — find the break: when does the law stop holding?

Power laws break at both ends. At the small end the irreducible loss floor bends the curve; at the large end data repetition does. Fit including an offset term and see whether the exponent moves.

import numpy as np N = np.logspace(6, 10, 12) L = 1.69 + (8.8e13 / N) ** 0.35 # floor + power law for floor in (0.0, 1.0, 1.69): s, _ = np.polyfit(np.log(N), np.log(L - floor), 1) print(f"assumed floor {floor:.2f} -> fitted exponent {-s:.3f}") # floor 0.00 -> 0.344 # floor 1.00 -> 0.347 # floor 1.69 -> 0.350 ← recovers the truth

With the correct 1.69 floor you recover 0.350 exactly; with floor 0 you get 0.344. Small here because the range spans 106–1010 params; narrow the range to 106–108 and the bias grows. That is the trap in L29 (emergence): omit the floor and a smooth curve looks like it has a kink.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Architecture drift. You fit on dense GQA models, then ship an MoE with a different active-parameter count. Symptom: every 5B+-scale prediction is 0.05–0.2 nats optimistic, and the miss has the same sign every time.
  • Tokenizer drift. Loss is nats-per-token. A new tokenizer with a bigger vocab makes loss per token go DOWN for the same quality because each token carries more bytes. Symptom: predicted vs actual disagree after a tokenizer upgrade while bits-per-byte (lesson 16) stays flat — you "improved" by changing the unit.
  • Data contamination of the frontier. Small models memorise a small corpus, so their perplexity is flattered. Symptom: the fitted exponent is too small (headroom looks huge), then the big run lands worse than predicted. Check exact-match rates on a canary set before trusting small-model numbers.
  • Single-seed fitting. Seed-to-seed variance at small scale (often several milli-nats) exceeds the per-decade effect you are trying to measure. Symptom: adding one small run changes your forecast by more than the forecast's own confidence interval.
  • Extrapolating past the data wall. With a fixed corpus of D tokens, loss law in N silently changes slope past N ≈ D/20 because you are now repeating data (lesson 28). Symptom: the largest run underperforms only when its tokens-per-parameter ratio exceeds the range you fitted.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why is the backward pass assumed to cost 2× the forward pass, and what breaks in C = 6ND if that were wrong?

Reveal answer

Per linear layer, forward does one matmul per token. Backward needs dL/dx (for the previous layer) and dL/dW (for the update) — two matmuls of the same shape — i.e. 2 × the forward matmul cost. Per parameter per token: forward 2 FLOPs, backward 4, total 6. If backward were 1× you would get C = 4ND and every budget estimate would be 1.5× too high.

Q2. With αC = 0.05, a 10× compute increase multiplies loss by what factor, and closes what fraction of the remaining distance to zero?

Reveal answer

Multiplier = 10-0.05 = 0.8913. So loss falls ~10.9%, meaning 10.9% of the distance from the current loss to zero is closed — but if there is a floor L∞, you have closed 10.9% of the distance to the floor only if you divide by (L - L∞); the fractional closure of the distance to the floor is still exactly 1 − 10-α. This is why the same 10× feels huge at 1020 FLOPs and invisible at 1027.

Q3. Diagnostic: your 9 small-model fits show an excellent straight line (R² > 0.999) but the 10th, larger model lands 0.08 nats worse than predicted. Name two candidate causes and the single experiment that separates them.

Reveal answer

(a) data repetition — the big run exceeded the unique-token budget so it is loss-limited by duplicated data; (b) a data-wall or tokenizer mismatch; (c) small-model contamination flattering the fit. The separating experiment: re-read the 10th run's loss restricted to the first epoch's tokens, and check unique-token count per parameter. If first-epoch loss matches the prediction and full-run loss is worse, it is repetition. If both are worse, it is contamination or an architecture difference.

Q4. 6 × 300×109 × 9×1011 = ? Express in ZFLOPs and say how many H800-GPU-days that is at a sustained 350 TFLOP/s per GPU.

Reveal answer

6 × 3×1011 × 9×1011 = 1.62×1024 FLOPs = 1620 ZFLOPs. At 3.5×1014 FLOP/s, GPU-seconds = 1.62×1024 / 3.5×1014 = 4.63×109 s = 1.286×106 GPU-hours = 53,571 GPU-days. At 1000 GPUs that is ~54 days of perfect utilisation, before any failure, checkpoint stall or queueing overhead.

Q5. Why is it correct to say a scaling law is a forecast and not a theory, and what audit artifact proves you have one?

Reveal answer

A theory explains why the exponent takes that value; a forecast only predicts the next point. Nobody has a derivation of α from first principles — it is a fitted regularity. So the only defensible claim is the forecast, and the only proof is out-of-sample: N-1 runs fitted, 1 run held out, predicted vs actual loss reported. Anything else is curve-fitting one straight line through points you already paid for.
