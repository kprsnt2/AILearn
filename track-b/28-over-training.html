L28 · Over-training, Inference-Aware Scaling, and the “Small but Long” Era

Chinchilla is only optimal if inference is free and data is infinite. Neither holds. Once a token costs money to serve, the cheapest model to run is deliberately over-trained — and that is the whole reason 2026 frontier models are small, hungry and fast instead of huge.

1 · Why it exists #

Lesson 27 left us with a contradiction. Chinchilla says: for compute C, the optimal parameters are N ≈ C0.45. Apply it honestly to a modern budget and you get requirements no corpus can meet — 1023 FLOPs wants 833B parameters and 16.7 trillion tokens; 1025 FLOPs wants 83 trillion parameters. There is no 16.7T-token high-quality corpus for most domains, and never has been.

The second contradiction is the one that actually reorganised the industry. Chinchilla optimises training loss per FLOP of training. A deployed model serves billions of tokens afterwards. Training spend is paid once; serving spend is paid per query, forever. A model that is 2× cheaper to serve and 0.05 nats worse can be dramatically better business.

So the failure that killed naive Chinchilla-following was: labs built compute-optimal-sized models, could not find the data to train them, and simultaneously watched serving bills dominate the total cost of ownership. The response — now standard — is to pick a model size far below the compute-optimal point and train it on far more tokens than 20×: 100×, 500×, 2000×. That is over-training.

Spend extra training FLOPs once to make every future token cheaper. The right size is whatever minimises training cost + inference cost over the model's lifetime, and for anything with real traffic that number is far smaller and far hungrier than Chinchilla's.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): how many delivery vans to buy. A big van carries more per trip but costs more to run — per kilometre, forever. A small van costs less to buy, more per trip in fuel if you overfill it. If your total distance driven is huge, you buy the small van and drive it very hard.

Precisely: training cost scales with N·D (both parameters and tokens); inference cost per token scales with Nactive only. So for a lifetime token count Q,

Total FLOPs ≈ 6 N D  +  2 N&thinspace;Q 6ND is the training bill, paid once. 2NQ is inference: 2 FLOPs per parameter per generated (and processed) token, paid Q times. There is no D in the second term — that is the entire asymmetry. The model you trained harder costs nothing extra to serve.

What the analogy gets right

Fixed capital cost vs marginal running cost, and the decision depends on volume. Over-training is buying a cheaper-to-run vehicle.

Where it breaks

Vans have a maximum load. Models do not keep improving indefinitely with more tokens: past some ratio the loss curve flattens hard and you are burning FLOPs for milli-nats. Also, over-training is only affordable when the data exists — if it does not, you are repeating, and repetition is not free.

The break-even thought

Every training FLOP you spend on D must be repaid by inference savings over Q tokens. The break-even Q, comparing a model trained with ratio r to one trained with ratio 20, is a divide. Below, the number that matters in every serving decision.

Qbreak-even = 6 N D / (2 N) = 3 D = 3 r N For a 30B model trained at r = 200 (D = 6T): Q = 3 × 6×1012 = 1.8×1013 tokens. If that model will serve fewer than 18 trillion tokens in its life, the extra training was not repaid by inference savings on its own account — you did it for quality or latency, not cost.

3 · The mechanism #

Two independent constraints bind at once, and the modern design point is the intersection.

Constraint 1 — the data wall

You have U unique tokens. The cheapest valid plan sets D ≤ U (one epoch). The compute-optimal N needs Dopt = C/6 once C is fixed, so once Dopt > U you cannot be compute-optimal. You either spend less compute (train a smaller model) or repeat data (which delivers less than a new token). Effectively, over-training means re-allocating a fixed token budget across fewer parameters.

Constraint 2 — the serving bill

Prefill cost per token is 2Nactive FLOPs, decode is 2Nactive per token too. So cost per query for a prompt of p tokens and a response of o tokens is 2Nactive(p + o). That is linear in active parameters and completely independent of how many tokens you trained on. A model trained at r = 2000 costs the same to serve as one trained at r = 20 with the same N. The extra training is pure sunk cost that bought quality.

Read the last two rows together. The 30B at r = 200 has 1.8× the training cost of the 7B at r = 2000 (1.08×1024 vs 5.88×1023) but its break-even Q is less than half (1.8×1013 vs 4.2×1013) — because each served token costs 4.3× more (30B/7B). At high volume the big model never repays itself; at low volume the big model is the better buy because quality per FLOP is better and you will not serve enough tokens to care.

Worked dollar example

Price H800-class compute at $2/GPU-hour and assume 40% MFU on 989 TFLOP/s dense bf16, i.e. 989×1012 × 0.40 = 3.956×1014 FLOP/s per GPU. Then:

  • 7B at r = 200: C = 5.88×1022 FLOPs → 5.88×1022/3.956×1014 = 1.486×108 GPU-s = 41,287 GPU-h → $82,575.
  • 70B at r = 20 (Chinchilla-optimal size): C = 5.88×1023 → 412,875 GPU-h → $825,750.
  • Serving 1M queries of 1,000 prompt + 300 output tokens on a 13B-active model: 2×13×109×1,300×106 = 3.38×1019 FLOPs → 24 GPU-h. Every million queries.

That last line is the whole argument. The $825k training run is a one-time event. Twenty-four GPU-hours per million queries, at $2/h, is $48/M queries — and at a billion queries a year that is $48,000/year that scales linearly forever, while the training bill does not.

state as of 2026-10 This is exactly the shape of the 2026 DeepSeek line. DeepSeek-V4-Flash is 284B total / 13B active and V4-Pro is 1.6T total / 49B active — a 3.8× gap in serving cost per token between the two tiers of the same family. V4.1-Flash goes further: 552B MoE with 8B active for input and 16B for output, and its KV cache needs 1/4 the HBM and 1/8 the SSD of the previous generation. All three design choices — sparse activation, asymmetric input/output width, compressed cache — are inference-cost decisions, not training-cost decisions.

The auditable artifact for this lesson is a single table per candidate model: (Nactive, D, C, training $, tokens/query, expected Q per year, break-even Q, months-to-repay). If months-to-repay is longer than the model's replacement window, over-training it was a vanity metric. That is a go/no-go gate, not an opinion.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — break-even tokens for any (N, r) pair

The one number that tells you whether over-training pays at your traffic level. Copy, change the numbers to your own deployment.

def breakeven(N, r): D = N * r train_flops = 6 * N * D infer_per_tok = 2 * N return train_flops / infer_per_tok # = 3rN tokens for N, r in [(7e9, 200), (7e9, 2000), (30e9, 200), (552e9, 16)]: q = breakeven(N, r) print(f"N={N/1e9:6.1f}B r={r:5d} D={N*r/1e12:6.2f}T break-even Q={q:.2e} tokens")

7B/200 → 4.2×1012; 7B/2000 → 4.2×1013; 30B/200 → 1.8×1013; 552B/16 → 2.65×1013. Note the last one: even a 552B model with a very modest 16 tokens/param needs 26.5 trillion served tokens to repay just its training — which is why the MoE trick (analysing 16B active, not 552B) is what makes it servable at all.

Experiment 2 — total cost of ownership as a function of model size

The optimisation Chinchilla does not do: sweep N, hold total compute-as-quality proxy roughly fixed, and add the serving term. Watch the optimum move left as Q grows.

import numpy as np E, A, B, al, be = 1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28 def loss(N, D): return E + A/N**al + B/D**be PRICE_H = 2.0; FLOPS = 989e12 * 0.40 # $/GPU-h, FLOP/s per GPU def dollars(N, D, Q): return (6*N*D + 2*N*Q) / FLOPS / 3600 * PRICE_H for Q in (1e11, 1e13, 1e15): best = None for N in np.logspace(9, 11.5, 120): for r in (20, 50, 100, 200, 400): D = N * r cost = dollars(N, D, Q); l = loss(N, D) if l > 1.90: continue # quality gate: must reach L <= 1.90 if best is None or cost < best[0]: best = (cost, N, r, D, l) print(f"Q={Q:.0e} cheapest plan: N={best[1]/1e9:5.1f}B r={best[2]:4d} D={best[3]/1e12:5.2f}T " f"L={best[4]:.4f} total=${best[0]:,.0f}")

As Q grows by four orders of magnitude, the cheapest plan that reaches the quality gate should shrink in N and jump in r. That is the over-training effect reproduced from first principles. Falsification: if the optimum does not move with Q, your inference term is missing from dollars().

Experiment 3 — what a 1.6T-total model costs to serve if it were dense

Quantifies the MoE argument before lesson 30 makes it formally.

python -c " for N,tag in ((1.6e12,'dense 1.6T'),(49e9,'V4-Pro active'),(13e9,'V4-Flash active'),(16e9,'V4.1-Flash out-active')): for q in (1_000_000,): f=2*N*1300*q print(f'{tag:22s} N={N:.3g} 1M queries(1k in/300 out) = {f:.2e} FLOPs = {f/(989e12*0.4)/3600:,.0f} GPU-h = \${f/(989e12*0.4)/3600*2:,.0f}') "

A dense 1.6T model would need 2,921 GPU-hours per million queries; the 49B-active MoE version needs 89.5, and the 13B-active Flash version 23.7. Same order of total parameter count on the box, 33–123× different serving bill. That is the economic reason sparse architecture won.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Over-training into repetition. You wanted r = 2000 but only have 1/5 the unique tokens. Symptom: loss falls faster than the scaling law predicts through epoch 1, then almost not at all, and validation loss on fresh text plateaus while training loss keeps dropping. The measurable tell is a growing train/val gap with no perplexity gain on held-out data.
  • Over-training a model nobody will call. Symptom: months-to-repay longer than the model's replacement cycle. You over-trained for a service that gets a few thousand queries a day, so the training premium is never recovered and the quality was available cheaper from a bigger, lazier model.
  • Under-training a hot endpoint. Symptom: serving cost dominates the P&L while the training budget sat unused. This is the pre-2022 mistake repeating in reverse: you shipped a huge model at r = 20 because the paper said so, to an endpoint with 1015 lifetime tokens.
  • Comparing across tokenizers. Symptom: “our over-trained model is worse” when per-token loss says so but per-byte loss (lesson 16) says better — the tokenizer changed how many tokens a document costs, and the serving math is per token.
  • Forgetting that over-training changes the LR schedule. Symptom: the run that was scaled from 200B to 2T tokens underperforms quietly because the schedule (lesson 14) was not stretched, so the model spent most of its life decaying a too-high LR. Loss is 0.02–0.05 nats off with no other symptom, and only a re-run settles it.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why does the inference term in total cost have no D, and what does that make over-training conceptually?

Reveal answer

Inference cost is 2 FLOPs per active parameter per token, and only the parameters are touched — the training corpus is gone by serving time. So a model trained on 200× its parameter count in tokens costs exactly the same to serve as one trained on Chinchilla's 20×. Over-training is therefore a one-time capital payment to buy quality with no recurring cost, which is precisely why it dominates when lifetime volume is large.

Q2. A 30B model trained at r = 500. Compute D, training FLOPs, GPU-hours at 40% MFU on 989 TFLOP/s, and the break-even serving volume.

Reveal answer

D = 30×109 × 500 = 1.5×1013 tokens. C = 6ND = 6×3×1010×1.5×1013 = 2.7×1024 FLOPs. GPU-hours = 2.7×1024/(3.956×1014 × 3600) = 1.895×108/… let us be careful: GPU-seconds = 2.7e24/3.956e14 = 6.8268e9 s, GPU-hours = 6.8268e9/3600 = 1,896,330 GPU-h, about $3.79M at $2/h. Break-even Q = 3rN = 3×500×3×1010 = 4.5×1013 tokens. Below that lifetime volume the extra training is not repaid by inference savings.

Q3. Diagnostic: your heavily over-trained 8B model matches the quality of a 70B model, but the finance team says serving cost went up. What is the likely explanation?

Reveal answer

Serving cost went up means more FLOPs per token somewhere, which cannot come from training data. Candidates: (a) the 8B model has a much larger KV cache per token (more layers/heads/heads-of-KV), so concurrency dropped and you are batching far worse; (b) you are running it at 8k context with long prompts, so prefill dominates and the smaller model's cheaper weights do not help; (c) the quality match only holds with long chain-of-thought, so output tokens per query tripled (lesson 60). The check: per-query FLOPs broken into prefill and decode, before and after.

Q4. Why is “over-training” affordable in 2026 but was not in 2020, apart from the compute itself?

Reveal answer

Mostly because of what made D economically obtainable and what made Nactive affordable: MoE decouples parameter count from activation, so the serving term stays small enough that the training premium is easy to repay; and data became the bottleneck, which forces the reallocation. Also, without MoE, a compute-optimal very large dense model has an astronomical serving bill, which pushes the optimum to small-and-long even at typical volumes.

Q5. V4.1-Flash has 8B active for input and 16B for output. Give the one-line cost consequence of that asymmetry.

Reveal answer

Prefill (input) and decode (output) are charged differently and with different arithmetic intensities, so the model spends half the compute per prompt token that it spends per generated token. For a workload dominated by long prompts and short answers — RAG, classification, long-document QA — that is a 2× cut on the dominant term. It is deliberately asymmetric because the two phases have different cost curves (see L37).
