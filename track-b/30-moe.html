L30 · Mixture of Experts: Sparse Activation

A dense model makes you pay twice for capacity: once in training FLOPs and again on every token you ever serve. Sparse activation breaks the link — total parameters buy knowledge, active parameters buy your bill — at the price of memory, bandwidth and routing fragility.

~30 min difficulty ●●●●○ build: toy MoE router with load stats
sparse activation top-k router expert FFN total vs active params all-to-all comms expert parallelism capacity factor

1 · Why it exists #

Two lessons converged on a wall. L26 says loss falls as a power law in parameters. L28 says serving cost is linear in parameters. Put them together and a dense model forces a bad trade: to get the loss of a 1.6T model you must pay 1.6T-parameter prices on every single generated token, forever.

Price it. At 16-bit weights, 1.6T parameters is 1.6×1012 × 2 bytes = 3.2×1012 bytes ≈ 2.91 TiB just to hold the weights. You cannot fit that in one node; you must shard across a cluster, and every token then requires reading all of it or communicating across all of it. Generating one token on such a dense model means moving ~3 TB through HBM interconnects. The physics, not the budget, says no.

The escape: a transformer's FFN layers are the parameter bulk, and neighbouring tokens do not need the same knowledge. If you split the FFN into E parallel experts and route each token to only the top k of them, the model can hold Ntotal parameters while spending only Nactive ≈ Ntotal·k/E FLOPs and bytes per token. That is the whole idea. Switch Transformer arXiv:2101.03961 and GShard arXiv:2006.16668 are the canonical early references; every 2026 frontier model uses some variant.

Sparse activation separates the two costs you were forced to bundle: knowledge scales with total parameters, your bill scales with active parameters. A model can be 20–35× larger in memory than the compute it spends per token — that ratio is the design knob.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): a hospital with specialists. Every patient (token) is triaged by a generalist (the router) and sent to two specialists. The hospital must staff and house all specialists (memory = total parameters), but on any given day only the ones who see patients do work (compute = active parameters).

The break in that analogy: in a hospital, patients are routed over hours and can queue; here, routing decisions are made inside the forward pass and must be packed into dense matrix multiplies to run efficiently on GPUs. That packing is where the real engineering lives, and it is why the effective speedup is never k/E of the parameter count.

What the analogy gets right

Capacity decoupled from throughput; the generalist's decision quality is a first-class concern (a bad router means the right specialist never sees the patient).

Where it breaks

A hospital can hire more specialists cheaply; an MoE with E = 256 pays a memory bill of 256 experts whether or not they are used, plus an all-to-all network hop per MoE layer to move tokens to wherever the experts live. And specialists can starve — the hospital has no union rules, so 90% of patients can end up at two doctors (route collapse, L31).

Where the parameters actually are

Take a transformer with dmodel = 4096 and dense FFN width 14336. Each dense FFN layer is 3 × 4096 × 14336 ≈ 0.176B parameters (three matrices: gate, up, down). Attention at d = 4096 with 32 heads is roughly 4 × d2 ≈ 0.067B. So the FFN is ~2.6× the size of attention and it is the obvious place to add capacity. Every real MoE sparsifies the FFN and leaves attention dense.

3 · The mechanism #

router:  s = softmax(Wg x)  →  &TopK;(s, k)  →  y = ∑i ∈ top-k (si / ∑j ∈ top-k sj) · FFNi(x) Wg: d_model × E. The gate weights on the selected experts are renormalised to sum to 1 so the output scale does not depend on k. Nactive = attention + k/E × FFN capacity; Ntotal = everything on disk.
# minimal MoE FFN layer. numpy-free: this runs anywhere. import random, math def softmax(z): m = max(z); e = [math.exp(v - m) for v in z]; s = sum(e) return [v / s for v in e] E, K, D = 8, 2, 16 random.seed(30) Wg = [[random.gauss(0, 0.5) for _ in range(D)] for _ in range(E)] def route(x): s = softmax([sum(wi * xi for wi, xi in zip(w, x)) for w in Wg]) top = sorted(range(E), key=lambda i: -s[i])[:K] z = sum(s[i] for i in top) return [(i, s[i] / z) for i in top] loads = [0] * E for t in range(1000): x = [random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(D)] for i, _ in route(x): loads[i] += 1 print("loads:", loads) print("max/min ratio:", max(loads) / max(1, min(loads))) print("ideal per expert:", 1000 * K / E)

Three costs this pseudocode hides, all of which decide whether an MoE is actually faster than a dense model of the same active size:

  1. Memory. All E experts must be resident. At 16-bit, a 1.6T-total model is 1.6×1012 × 2 bytes = 3.2×1012 B = 2.91 TiB. Even at 8-bit it is 1.46 TiB. You are forced into expert parallelism across nodes whether you want it or not.
  2. All-to-all. Each MoE layer moves every token's hidden vector to the node that holds its experts and back. With batch B tokens, dmodel = 7168, k = 6, 16-bit: each token sends k vectors of d values out, and receives the same volume back — B × 7168 × 6 × 2 bytes × 2 = B × 172,032 bytes per layer. At B = 4096 that is 0.705 GB per MoE layer; at B = 32,768 it is 5.64 GB per layer. Across 61 layers: 43.0 GB and 343.9 GB respectively, per batch. That traffic, not FLOPs, is what you optimise.
  3. Padding / capacity. To run as a dense matmul each expert gets a fixed slot count: capacity = (tokens × k / E) × capacity_factor. Tokens beyond capacity are dropped or passed through the residual unmodified — a silent quality leak when routing is imbalanced.

Every one of them sits at 3–5% sparsity. Note what that means for serving: V4-Pro holds 1.6T parameters of memory but pays only 49B parameters of arithmetic per token — a 32.7× gap between what the cluster must store and what a token costs. V4.1-Flash's split (8B for input, 16B for output) is a further refinement: the two serving phases get different active widths because they are limited by different resources (L37).

Two numbers or the claim is unusable. Any MoE spec sheet that says only “1.6T parameters” is marketing: the serving bill is set by 49B and the memory requirement by 1.6T, and those are 32.7× apart. Record both, plus k, E and capacity factor, or you cannot reproduce the cost model.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — does a random router balance? (the answer is yes, and that is the trap)

Untrained router weights already produce near-uniform load. This matters because it means load imbalance in a real run is learned, not incidental — which is why it needs a mechanism (L31), not a hope.

python -c " import random, math def softmax(z): m=max(z); e=[math.exp(v-m) for v in z]; s=sum(e); return [v/s for v in e] E,K,D=64,6,32 random.seed(30) Wg=[[random.gauss(0,0.4) for _ in range(D)] for _ in range(E)] loads=[0]*E for t in range(20000): x=[random.gauss(0,1) for _ in range(D)] s=softmax([sum(a*b for a,b in zip(w,x)) for w in Wg]) for i in sorted(range(E), key=lambda i:-s[i])[:K]: loads[i]+=1 ideal=20000*K/E print('ideal per expert', ideal, 'max', max(loads), 'min', min(loads)) print('max/ideal %.3f min/ideal %.3f' % (max(loads)/ideal, min(loads)/ideal)) "

Ideal is 20,000×6/64 = 1,875 per expert. With random weights the spread is small — roughly within ±10% of ideal — because no expert has been trained to specialise. Falsification: if you see collapse (one expert taking >50% of tokens) with random weights, your softmax or top-k is wrong.

Experiment 2 — compute the memory and comms bill for a shape you pick

The two lines that decide feasibility. Set B, d, k, total parameters and get tebibytes and gigabytes.

def moe_bill(N_total, B, d_model, k, layers, bits=16): w_bytes = N_total * bits / 8 per_layer_a2a = B * d_model * k * 2 * bits / 8 # out and back return w_bytes / 1024**4, per_layer_a2a * layers / 1e9 for N, B in [(284e9, 4096), (1.6e12, 4096), (1.6e12, 32768)]: tib, gb = moe_bill(N, B, 7168, 6, 61) print(f"N={N/1e12:4.2f}T B={B:6d} weights={tib:7.2f} TiB all-to-all={gb:8.2f} GB/token-batch")

284B at B = 4096: 0.517 TiB of weights, 43.0 GB of all-to-all per batch. 1.6T at B = 4096: 2.91 TiB and also 43.0 GB. At B = 32,768 the all-to-all is 343.9 GB per batch — larger than most interconnects can move in less than seconds. That is the bandwidth wall, and it is why MoE serving is a networking problem as much as a GPU one.

Experiment 3 — measure the real speedup of sparsity (it is not 1/sparsity)

A thought-experiment you can check against any benchmark: compare dense-layer FLOPs with MoE-layer FLOPs plus comms. The point is to see the gap between the theoretical FLOPs saving and the achieved saving.

python -c " Ntot, Nact = 1.6e12, 49e9 flop_ratio = Ntot/Nact # achieved ratio also has routing + a2a + padding overhead; assume 35% of dense-savings survive for eff in (1.0, 0.5, 0.35, 0.2): print(f'if {eff:.0%} of the FLOPs saving survives overhead: effective dense-equivalent size = {Nact/eff/1e9:.0f}B') print('raw ratio', round(flop_ratio,1)) "

The honest reading: an MoE's “effective dense size” for a given throughput is Nactive/eff, and if only 35% of the saving survives you get the throughput of a 140B dense model. Still a 11× win over dense-at-1.6T, but nowhere near 32.7×. Any vendor quoting the raw ratio is quoting the ceiling.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Route collapse. Symptom: a handful of experts receive most tokens, the others receive almost none and their weights barely change; validation loss plateaus above the dense baseline. Detect with a load histogram per layer, per step.
  • Expert starvation at small batch. Symptom: throughput collapses at low concurrency. With E = 256 and k = 8, you need B ≥ 128 tokens just to average 4 tokens per expert; a single-user request (a batch of one prompt) may use an expert exactly once, so the matmul is a vector→matrix and the GPU idles. Symptom: latency per token is fine at batch 1 but the FLOP utilisation is a few percent.
  • Capacity overflow drops. Symptom: loss is worse than predicted and gets worse with longer sequences; the drop counter for over-capacity tokens is non-zero on some layers. Silent because nothing errors.
  • All-to-all saturation. Symptom: scaling efficiency falls when you add nodes; per-step time grows super-linearly with node count and the network counters are saturated while GPU utilisation drops. The fix is usually a smaller k or expert groups (L42).
  • Load-balancing loss fights the LM objective. Symptom: an auxiliary loss weight that is too high makes the router nearly uniform, so no expert specialises and quality drops even as balance looks perfect (L31).

7 · Self-check #

Q1. A model has 552B total / 16B active. What is the sparsity ratio, the bf16 weight footprint in GiB, and the ratio of memory to serving cost?

Reveal answer

Sparsity = 16/552 = 2.9%. Weights = 552×109 × 2 B = 1.104×1012 B / 10243 = 1,028.2 GiB (1.004 TiB). Memory-to-serving ratio = 34.5×. So this model needs over a terabyte of accelerator memory but costs about 1/34.5 of that in per-token arithmetic.

Q2. d_model = 4096, dense ffn = 14336, E = 64, top-k = 2. What are the FFN params per layer and per token, and what is the parameter ratio?

Reveal answer

Per expert: 3 × 4096 × 14336 = 176,160,768 ≈ 0.176B. Per layer total: 64 × 0.176 = 11.27B. Per token active: 2 × 0.176 = 0.352B. Ratio = 11.27/0.352 = 32× at this single layer — note the whole-model ratio is lower (e.g. 4.6%) because attention and embeddings are dense.

Q3. Diagnostic: quality is excellent, but the serving cluster is network-bound at batch 32k while GPUs idle at 30%. Name the two most likely causes and the measurement that separates them.

Reveal answer

(a) all-to-all traffic per MoE layer is saturating the fabric — measure bytes moved per token per layer against the NVLink/InfiniBand peak; (b) routing is unbalanced so some expert-parallel ranks finish late and everyone waits (straggler effect) — measure per-rank compute time per layer and the variance across ranks. If per-rank times are similar but total time exceeds the sum, it is bandwidth. If one rank takes far longer, it is imbalance.

Q4. During decode, an MoE is bottlenecked by weight fetch. If you quantize only the experts to 8-bit, what happens to (a) memory footprint, (b) decode bytes per token, (c) whether the KV cache budget improves?

Reveal answer

(a) the expert portion halves — for a 552B-total model, 552×109×1 B = 514 GiB instead of 1,028 GiB. (b) decode fetches only the active experts, so bytes per token roughly halve too and decode gets close to 2× faster on a bandwidth-bound kernel. (c) not directly — the KV cache size is set by layers, heads and context, not by expert weight precision; but the memory you freed can now hold KV, so concurrency improves. Quantizing weights and quantizing the KV cache are separate levers (L40).

Q5. Your router loses 3% of tokens to capacity drops at layer 12. Is that acceptable, and how do you decide?

Reveal answer

Decide with the drop's effect on the objective, not the percentage: dropped tokens skip the MoE sublayer and pass the residual straight through, so they lose one of many layer contributions. Measure validation loss with capacity_factor raised to 2.0 versus 1.0; if loss is unchanged, the drops are irrelevant. If loss is better at 2.0, you are trading quality for throughput and you should know the exchange rate explicitly. Never accept a drop counter you have not priced.
