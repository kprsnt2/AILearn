L29 · Emergence, and Why It Is Partly a Metric Artifact

A capability that is 0% at 10B parameters and 60% at 100B looks like a phase transition, and labs have built safety policy on that picture. Much of it is your metric throwing a step function onto a smooth curve — and knowing which part is real changes how you forecast and how you gate.

emergent abilities metric discontinuity exact match vs token-level smooth underlying loss threshold effects all-or-nothing scoring capability forecasting

1 · Why it exists #

Between 2022 and 2023 the field observed a pattern that looked like physics: benchmark accuracy on some tasks sat at near-zero for small models, then rose steeply past a size threshold. Papers catalogued dozens of “emergent abilities” and the framing entered policy: capabilities appear suddenly, so you cannot predict them from small-model results, so you must evaluate every scale.

That framing has a concrete production cost. If capability curves are genuinely discontinuous, every scaling decision requires a full ladder of large runs before you know what you get, and forecasting is impossible. But there was a suspicious detail: the discontinuity usually appeared in exact-match and all-or-nothing metrics, and usually vanished in token-level log-likelihood metrics. Same models, same checkpoints, different scoring rule.

The failure to avoid: treating a metric's threshold as a property of the model, and therefore either (a) believing a capability is impossibly far away when it is 0.03 nats from arriving, or (b) shipping a model because it crossed a numeric threshold on a task that was nearly working already and quietly breaks on the long tail.

Capability almost always improves smoothly in log-loss. state as of 2026-10 What jumps is your scoring rule, because a multiple-choice or exact-match metric converts a continuous probability into a step. Predict capability with per-token loss; report it with the metric your business actually pays for.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy (labelled): a dam in a river. Water level rises smoothly; the downstream riverbed goes from dry to flowing the moment the level crosses the lip. Nothing discontinuous happened in the water — the discontinuity is in the geometry of the boundary.

The precise version: exact-match accuracy is P(all tokens correct). If each of k answer tokens is correct with probability p, then accuracy = pk. That is a smooth function of p, but it has a power-law steepness in log p: at p = 0.5 and k = 10, accuracy is 0.00098; at p = 0.9, it is 0.349. Small changes in per-token quality near the top produce enormous changes in all-or-nothing accuracy. Meanwhile the log-loss curve is a near-straight line.

What the analogy gets right

The underlying variable is continuous and predictable; the observed jump is produced by a threshold in how you measure. Forecast on the continuous variable.

Where it breaks

Some capabilities really are threshold-shaped in the model, not the metric: a task that requires composing k independent operations has a natural k-way conjunction, and skills like multi-step tool use may not decompose linearly. So: check the metric’s shape before concluding, but do not conclude “it’s all the metric.”

The decisive experiment (and what it shows)

For each scale, measure both: (1) exact-match accuracy, (2) token-level accuracy or bits-per-byte on the same items. If the underlying metric is smooth while exact match steps, you are looking at a metric artifact. If the underlying metric also steps, you have a genuine threshold and you should treat the capability as unpredictable from smaller scales.

3 · The mechanism #

AccEM ≈ pk   and   ∂ ln Acc / ∂ ln p = k p = per-answer-token probability of being right, k = tokens in the answer. The elasticity of exact-match accuracy to per-token quality is exactly k — so long answers (k = 50) amplify a 2% per-token change into a ~2.7× change in pass rate, and short answers barely move.

That single line is the whole mechanism, and it also predicts where emergence will be reported: in benchmarks with long answers or many independent sub-parts, at low absolute accuracy, on all-or-nothing metrics. Let us price it:

Where the threshold is genuinely real

Two mechanisms produce real discontinuity:

  1. Conjunction over independent subskills. A task needing k independent skills, each with success probability s, succeeds with sk. If the skills arrive at different scales, the product is zero until the last one arrives — a true step, driven by “the weakest link.”
  2. Greedy decoding commitment. If the model must emit a token before it has enough evidence, and the first token choices are unrecoverable, small probability differences can flip an entire trajectory. Sampled decoding and chain-of-thought (which defers the commitment) both reduce this.

The audit question is not “did accuracy jump?” but “did the jump survive a metric change?” Report each capability with at minimum three numbers: exact-match, token-level accuracy, and bits-per-byte; at each scale. A capability claim backed by one metric on one ladder of runs is unfalsifiable in practice. This is exactly the seam as your hash-chained ledger: the artifact must be re-derivable under a different measurement, or it is a story.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — manufacture an “emergent ability” out of a smooth curve

Generate a smooth per-token quality curve against scale, then score it with exact match. You will produce a textbook emergence plot from a model that never had a discontinuity. If your plot does not reproduce, the metric transform is not the one being tested.

import numpy as np scale = np.logspace(8, 11, 10) # 0.1B .. 100B params # SMOOTH per-token quality: logistic in log scale, no threshold anywhere p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(np.log10(scale) - 9.4) / 0.35)) print("N(B) p(per-token) EM_k=4 EM_k=10 EM_k=30") for s, pi in zip(scale, p): print(f"{s/1e9:6.2f} {pi:12.4f} {pi**4:9.4f} {pi**10:9.4f} {pi**30:9.4f}")

The p column is a smooth sigmoid and shows no transition. The EM_k=30 column stays ~0 for the first five scales and then climbs to a large value — a shape colloquially called emergence, generated with no discontinuity in the model. Falsification: if the per-token column also steps, then this particular toy has a real threshold and the metric is not the culprit — a useful result, not a failed lab.

Experiment 2 — the elasticities, in one table

The number a forecaster needs: how much does the reported benchmark move per unit of real improvement?

python -c " for k in (1,4,10,30): for p in (0.80,0.90): r=(0.90**k)/(0.80**k) print(f'k={k:2d} p 0.80->0.90: EM {0.80**k:.4f} -> {0.90**k:.4f} ratio {r:.2f}x') "

For k = 1 the ratio is 1.125×; for k = 30 it is (1.125)30 = 34.4×. Same underlying improvement, 30× different apparent magnitude. Quote this whenever someone reads a benchmark jump as a capability jump.

Experiment 3 — simulate a real conjunction

Build a task with five independent subskills arriving at staggered scales. Show that conjunction produces a genuine step even though each subskill is smooth. This is the case where emergence is not a metric artifact.

import numpy as np xs = np.logspace(8, 11, 10) centres = [8.6, 9.0, 9.4, 9.8, 10.1] # each skill arrives at its own scale skills = [(1 / (1 + np.exp(-(np.log10(xs) - c) / 0.3))) for c in centres] task = np.prod(np.array(skills), axis=0) for x, t in zip(xs, task): print(f"N={x/1e9:7.2f}B conjunction success={t:.4f}")

Success stays near zero long after the first three skills are available, then rises sharply once the fifth arrives. That step is real, and it is the one to plan for: it means capability is gated by the weakest subskill, so a diagnostic that isolates subskills predicts far better than end-to-end accuracy.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Forecasting off exact match. Symptom: your model-size projections say a capability is 5 years away; the next model shows it working. The token-level metric had been climbing all along and you plotted the wrong series.
  • Dismissing a real threshold. Symptom: you raise a model 10× and a multi-step agent task still fails at step 4 — the conjunction over skills means adding overall quality does nothing until the specific missing subskill is fixed. Tell: end-to-end accuracy is flat while per-subskill accuracy is already high.
  • Small-benchmark noise read as emergence. Symptom: the “emergent” point moves when you change the random seed or the prompt template. At n = 200 items, a 5-point accuracy swing is about ±1.5 percentage points of standard error; anything smaller is noise wearing a trend line.
  • Contamination manufactures the knee. Symptom: the jump appears on the one benchmark you did not canary-check, and per-item analysis shows high memorisation on exactly the items that jumped (see L74).
  • Metric swap mid-ladder. Symptom: an apparent step at the scale where the eval harness changed (e.g. switched from log-likelihood to generative scoring, or added a parser). Version-lock the scorer with the model.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. A benchmark answer is 8 tokens and per-token accuracy goes from 0.85 to 0.95. What happens to exact-match accuracy, and how elastic is it?

Reveal answer

EM = p8. Before: 0.858 = 0.2725; after: 0.958 = 0.6634. So a 11.8% relative improvement in per-token quality produced a 143% increase in pass rate. Elasticity = k = 8: roughly an 8× multiplier on the relative change. This is why long-answer benchmarks “emerge” and single-token benchmarks do not.

Q2. You have three scales (7B, 30B, 70B) showing 0%, 4%, 61% on a task. Name the two measurements that decide whether this is a real threshold, and what each outcome implies.

Reveal answer

(1) Token-level accuracy or bits-per-byte on the same items, per scale. (2) Per-subskill accuracy broken out by subskill. If token-level is smooth and rising throughout — metric artifact, forecast on the log-loss line. If token-level is also flat then rising sharply — real threshold. If the subskill breakdown shows one skill stuck at chance until 70B while others are fine — a conjunction over a missing subskill, and raising the scale is the expensive way to fix it.

Q3. Diagnostic: your benchmark shows a jump precisely at the run where you changed the eval harness. What do you do first?

Reveal answer

Re-score the previous checkpoints with the new harness (and the new ones with the old harness). If the jump disappears or moves, it was a scorer change. This is a two-hour job that prevents a five-month wrong conclusion, and it is the same discipline as re-running a hash chain against two implementations before trusting a ledger.

Q4. A task requires 4 independent skills each succeeding 0.9 of the time. What is task success, and what is it if one skill is 0.6?

Reveal answer

0.94 = 0.6561. With one skill at 0.6: 0.6 × 0.93 = 0.4374, a 33% drop from improving everything else. The weakest subskill dominates: a model at 0.99 on three skills and 0.5 on the fourth scores 0.485 — worse than a model at 0.85 everywhere (0.522). Diagnostic implication: per-subskill evals beat end-to-end benchmarks for finding what to fix.

Q5. Why does sampled decoding (or a chain of thought) tend to move a threshold down rather than raise the maximum?

Reveal answer

Both defer commitment: a chain of thought puts the answer token later, after more evidence has been conditioned in, and sampling explores alternative token choices instead of locking the greedy path. So tasks that failed because the model committed to a wrong early token now succeed — the threshold (where success becomes reliable) drops. The maximum accuracy on tasks where the model already had the evidence is roughly unchanged. That is why “emergence” is partly a property of how you decode, not just how big the model is (L60–L61).
