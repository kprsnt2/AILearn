L71 · Agentic RL: credit assignment over long-horizon trajectories

One terminal reward over forty tool calls, spread equally across every action: that is the default and it is wrong. This page swaps credit estimators on the same trajectory and shows exactly which step each one can and cannot see.

~27 min difficulty ●●●●○ build: multi-step credit assignment sim
credit assignment trajectory advantage discount horizon value-based advantage step-verified reward sparse success first-wrong step episode budget

1 · Why it exists #

A single-turn reasoning model gets a reward at the end of 500 tokens. An agent gets a reward at the end of perhaps forty tool calls, and the failure this lesson exists to address is that the same scalar credit is applied to every action in that trajectory. In GRPO the advantage of a whole trajectory is one number, spread over every token of every call. A step that saved the task and a step that recovered from a blunder get identical credit.

Worse, the arithmetic of long horizons amplifies it. If each step has probability p of being the right move, a trajectory of n steps succeeds with roughly pn (lesson 60's reliability law, in the agent setting). At p = 0.95 and n = 40, success is about 13%. The reward signal is therefore sparse at the trajectory level and abundant at the step level — the exact inverse of what the optimiser needs.

Agentic RL is the credit-assignment problem from lesson 59 applied to trajectories that are long, partly tool-determined and partly stochastic. Every practical method — step-level verifiers, process rewards, discounted returns, hierarchical credit — is an attempt to convert one terminal scalar into per-step signal without inventing a reward that lies.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: a trajectory is a sequence of (observation, action, reward) with the only real reward at the end. Credit assignment asks: for each action, how much of the final outcome was caused by it? Three estimators with different assumptions:

EstimatorPer-step signalAssumptionFails when
Trajectory advantage (GRPO default)the same scalar for every stepAll steps are equally responsibleAny long trajectory: good and bad steps get identical credit
Discounted returnγt · R, so early steps count lessEarlier actions matter lessOften false — the first plan is the most consequential action in the whole episode
Value-based advantageγV(st+1) − V(st)A value model that generalises across statesThe value model is wrong exactly where the agent has never been
Step-verified rewarda checkable result per step (did the file change, did the test pass)You can write per-step checksChecks are missing or the task's value is in the whole, not the parts

What "long horizon" gets right

It explains why agent runs need hundreds or thousands of episodes per prompt and why the pass rate is the bottleneck: a 13% success rate with G = 8 samples leaves most groups with zero successes, which is the zero-variance failure of lesson 56 in its most expensive form.

Where it breaks

Agents are not Markov: the same tool call means different things depending on what was learned earlier in the episode. A state value function over the transcript is a massive extrapolation, and its errors are correlated with the policy's own unusual states.

3 · The mechanism #

Write the credit estimator so it can be swapped and compared, which is the only way to know whether it is earning its complexity:

Âttraj = R   |   Âtdisc = γtR   |   Âtvalue = γV(st+1) − V(st)   |   Âtstep = rtverified + λ·R R is the episode outcome (0/1 or graded). r_t is any per-step verified signal. λ keeps an episode-level term so step rewards cannot fully decouple from the task.

The lab builds a toy agent — a three-stage task with a recoverable failure — and compares how each estimator allocates credit. The point is not which wins on the toy; it is to see the trajectory estimator hand identical credit to a decisive first action and a wasted one.

Experiment 2 — the sparse-success cliff

Trajectory success is pn, and a group is only useful if at least one of its G samples succeeds. This script tabulates both, plus the effective discount horizon.

At p = 0.95 and n = 40, trajectory success is 0.129 and only 67% of G=8 groups contain a success; at n = 80 it is 0.017 and 12.5%. That is the quantitative reason agent RL needs high per-step reliability before it starts — and why a discount of 0.9, with an effective horizon of 10 steps, is measuring something much shorter than the episode you are training.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Credit smearing. Symptom: training reward and final success both rise slowly, and error analysis shows the policy repeating a bad middle step that is never penalised; the trajectory estimator cannot isolate it.
  • Sparse success. Symptom: most groups have no successful episode; zero-variance fraction above 50% and cost per useful gradient explodes.
  • Reward from tool side effects. Symptom: the agent learns to trigger state changes the verifier checks rather than to complete the task; check that the verifier asserts the end state you intended, not an intermediate one.
  • Episode budget gaming. Symptom: agents that terminate early with partial work score as well as complete ones because the verifier is a prefix check.
  • Environment flakiness. Symptom: identical seeds give different outcomes; reward noise from the environment is indistinguishable from learning noise. Seed everything.
  • Discount misuse. Symptom: with γ = 0.9 over 40 steps the effective horizon is about 10, so the agent optimises the last ten actions and ignores the plan that determined everything.

Log per episode: steps, outcome, which step first went wrong (human or checker), and the credit each step received. A table of "first-wrong step" against "credit sign" shows immediately whether your estimator can see the failure at all — and that table is the evidence a reviewer should ask for.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why does a discount factor of 0.9 over a 40-step episode effectively blind the optimiser to the plan?

Reveal answer

The weight on step t is γt, so the effective horizon is about 1/(1−γ) = 10 steps: step 30 carries 0.930≈ 0.04 of the credit of step 0, and the first few decisions — which determine the whole trajectory — receive almost none of thesignal. Discounting is a modelling choice that assumes rewards arrive soon; in agents they often arrive only at the end, and theassumption is exactly backwards.

Q2. Quantitative: 40-step episodes, per-step success probability 0.95, G=8 samples per task. What fraction of groups contain at least one success, and what does that imply?

Reveal answer

Trajectory success ≈ 0.9540 ≈ 0.129. P(at least one of 8 succeeds) = 1 − (1−0.129)8 ≈ 0.67. Soabout a third of groups contain no success at all and contribute no gradient. The implication is that agent RL needs either higherper-step reliability (better SFT before RL), shorter horizons, or more samples — and that the cost per useful gradient risesquickly as horizon grows.

Q3. Name two per-step signals you can verify cheaply in an agent that writes code to disk, and one you cannot.

Reveal answer

Verifiable cheaply: (1) the file exists and parses as the expected language; (2) the targeted test passes after the edit.Not verifiable cheaply: whether the design is the one a maintainer would want — that is a judgement, so it belongs to alearned critic with all the correlated-error problems of lesson 68.

Q4. Diagnostic: training reward climbs, final success is flat, and the episode length grows. What is the likely estimator defect and the first check?

Reveal answer

The estimator rewards intermediate activity — step checks that pass regardless of the final state, or a reward that pays for toolcalls. First check: recompute the reward with the final-state term only and compare trajectories. If the ranking changes, theintermediate terms dominate and the optimiser is maximising them.

Q5. Why is 'first-wrong-step' analysis more informative than final accuracy for an agent?

Reveal answer

Final accuracy is one bit per episode; the first-wrong step localises where the policy fails, which is the information needed todecide between a plan defect (early), an execution defect (middle), and a verification defect (late). Training changes that targetone category are invisible in aggregate accuracy until they dominate.
