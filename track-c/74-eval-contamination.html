L74 · Contamination, benchmark gaming, and honestly-measured gains

A benchmark score is a measurement of generalisation until six percent of the questions are in the training data. This page builds the detector, reports the clean-slice delta beside the headline, and names the derived-contamination source synthetic pipelines keep creating.

~25 min difficulty ●●○○○ build: contamination detector
exact hash normalised hash n-gram overlap derived contamination clean slice leak rate threshold freezing probe leakage

1 · Why it exists #

A model's benchmark score jumps eight points after fine-tuning, and six of those points are because the benchmark's questions were in the fine-tuning data. The failure is not a lie; it is an evaluation that measures recall of the test set while the report reads it as capability. It is the single most common way post-training results become non-reproducible and non-comparable.

Contamination has three sources and they need different detectors:

  • Direct overlap: the eval item, verbatim, is in training data.
  • Near-duplicate overlap: the item is paraphrased, re-templated, or translated.
  • Derived contamination: not the item, but its solution — a trace generated from the answer, a synthetic task built from it, a critique that quotes it. This is the one that synthetic-data pipelines create without noticing (lesson 66 and 67).

Contamination is an audit problem with a cheap instrument: hash and diff. Every training file, every synthetic pool, every trace log against every eval item — exact, then near-duplicate. A pipeline that does not run that diff before reporting a number is producing an anecdote.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: the eval is a measurement of generalisation, and contamination converts it into a measurement of fit. Two quantities are worth separating:

reported Δ = Δreal + Δcontaminated Print the second term. A 4-point gain on a benchmark where 6% of items are in the training data is a claim about that 6%, not about the benchmark.
DetectorCatchesCost / limitation
Exact hash of itemVerbatim leakageTrivial; misses everything else
Normalised hash (case, whitespace, punctuation, numbers templated)Formatting variants, re-templated itemsCheap; still misses paraphrase
n-gram overlapParaphrase, partial copies, quoted solutionsTunable; threshold must be chosen per task length, and false positives are common on short items
Embedding similaritySemantic paraphrase, translated itemsNeeds a model; threshold is arbitrary, so calibrate it on known-clean pairs
Answer-only probesDerived contamination: was the solution in the data?Requires answers to be exact-matched and unique

What "measurement vs fit" gets right

It gives you a number to subtract, and it makes the mitigation obvious: hold out a clean slice and report it separately.

Where it breaks

Near-duplicate detection with a threshold can be gamed to hide or inflate leakage depending on the threshold chosen. Fix the threshold before the run and keep it in the run card; a post-hoc threshold is a tuning knob on your own honesty.

3 · The mechanism #

Two numbers per eval, reported together:

leak rate = |eval items with a match in training| / |eval items|
clean-slice Δ = improvement on eval items with NO match The clean-slice delta is the honest number. If the full-set delta is much larger, the difference is the contamination component.

Rules that make the number trustworthy:

  • Hash at ingestion. Every training file, synthetic pool and trace log gets its items hashed when it is written, not when someone gets suspicious.
  • Keep a clean slice sealed. A held-out portion of the eval that is checked against every data source and never used for anything else — and diffed again after each data addition.
  • Freeze the detector version. The detector is part of the eval; its threshold and normalisation go in the run card.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Reported gain that inverts on a clean slice. Symptom: full eval +6, clean slice −1. The entire gain was leakage; report the clean slice.
  • Derived contamination from synthetic pools. Symptom: a synthetic task generator seeded from eval answers produces near-copies; detect by diffing generated text against the answer strings, not just the questions.
  • Threshold arbitrariness. Symptom: the leak rate changes by an order of magnitude when the n-gram threshold moves; the threshold was not frozen before the run.
  • Probe leakage. Symptom: capability probes improve when the stage did not target them; same detector, different target.
  • Eval regeneration without re-check. Symptom: a new eval version has different contamination; the detector is not part of the eval release.
  • Contamination through distillation. Symptom: a student trained on teacher traces that were generated on eval items — the item is not in the data, its solution is.

Every eval report carries: leak rate, detector version and threshold hash, clean-slice delta, and the date of the last diff against all data sources. Four fields, all cheap, and together they turn a score into evidence.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Eval has 500 items, 30 of which appear verbatim in training data. Full-set accuracy is 0.72; clean-slice accuracy is 0.64; base model is 0.65. What is the honest improvement?

Reveal answer

Clean-slice delta = 0.64 − 0.65 = −0.01 — no improvement, within noise. The full-set number 0.72 is inflated by the 30 leakeditems. Leak rate = 6%. The report should say: no measurable improvement on uncontaminated items, plus 6% leakage in thebenchmark. That is a very different sentence from up-seven-points.

Q2. Why is derived contamination harder to detect than direct overlap?

Reveal answer

Because the eval item itself is absent — only material derived from it is present: a generated solution, a paraphrase, acritique quoting it, or a synthetic task seeded from its answer. Exact hashes find nothing. Detection requires matchinganswers/solutions and near-duplicate text, and understanding the generation provenance of every synthetic record.

Q3. Quantitative: an eval item is 40 tokens. What n-gram window would you use for overlap detection, and what is the risk?

Reveal answer

A window of about 8-12 tokens (roughly 20-30% of the item) balances sensitivity against false positives: a shared 10-tokenspan is unlikely by chance in prose but common in a template. The risk: short items produce many spurious matches with boilerplatephrasing such as What-is-the-value-of, so the threshold must be calibrated on known-clean pairs and frozen before the run.

Q4. Diagnostic: your synthetic data generator is seeded from task templates that also generated the eval. What specific artefact do you hash?

Reveal answer

Both the rendered question text AND the target answer/solution. Template-based generators frequently produce identical answersacross paraphrased questions, so hashing questions alone misses the case where the eval's answer is in the training data under adifferent question.

Q5. Why must the clean slice be sealed rather than merely unused?

Reveal answer

Because not-used-for-training is an intention, and data pipelines silently include files. Sealing means a hash-based checkruns after every data addition, so the claim is verified rather than asserted. It also stops the normal erosion where someone usesthe clean slice to tune a checkpoint just once.
