L60 · Chain of thought: why intermediate tokens buy serial compute

A transformer has fixed depth, so a one-token answer has a fixed number of serial steps no matter how hard the problem. Reasoning tokens buy more steps — at a measurable price, with a measurable reliability law.

serial depth reasoning tokens reliability law self-consistency faithfulness truncation majority vote error propagation

1 · Why it exists #

The failure chain-of-thought (arXiv:2201.11903) was invented to fix is not "the model is dumb". It is the model runs out of serial computation. Ask for the answer in one token and a transformer must have finished every step of the computation by the time it emits that token. Stacks of layers give it depth, but depth is width-parallel, not serially reusable: the model cannot loop back and apply the same operation to its own intermediate result. Multi-step arithmetic, tracking a state through a puzzle, or checking a candidate answer all require reusing the same computation along a sequence, and one forward pass does not provide that.

The observable symptom, pre-CoT: accuracy collapses on problems that require more intermediate steps, and it collapses in a specific pattern — performance becomes a function of the number of steps, not of the surface difficulty of the question. Two problems with identical wording but different chain lengths separate wildly. That is the signature of a compute bottleneck, not a knowledge gap.

A reasoning token is a program cycle. Every token the model writes is a full forward pass through the network, so its output becomes visible input for the next pass. Chain-of-thought converts a fixed-depth circuit into a variable-depth computation, paid for in tokens. That is the entire mechanism, and it explains every property: why it helps most on long chains, why it costs what it costs, and why the intermediate tokens can be wrong while the answer is right.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: the model has a hidden state ht. Without CoT the answer is read out from h at one position after fixed depth L of computation. With CoT, each emitted reasoning token becomes part of the input, and the next token is computed with that token in context — so the effective serial depth grows with the number of tokens written.

without CoT: y = fL(x)   |   with CoT: y = gL( x, z1, …, zk ),   zi+1 = gL(x, z1..i) Serial depth grows with k, the number of reasoning tokens. Total FLOPs grow linearly in k; attention cost grows as k² without a cache trick.

What "program cycles" gets right

It predicts the scaling: more tokens, more serial steps. It predicts the failure mode of a too-small budget: truncated reasoning means a truncated program, and the answer is read from a partial state. It also predicts why self-consistency works — sampling several programs and taking the majority is a cheap ensemble over the computation.

Where it breaks

Unlike real program cycles, the intermediate state is natural language, not a machine-checked register. The model can write a plausible-looking step that is wrong, and the next step reads that error as if it were true. CoT is not a proof system; it is a scratchpad with no type checker, which is exactly why process verification (lesson 59) exists.

3 · The mechanism #

The scaling claim, made testable. If CoT works because it buys serial compute, then for problems whose solution requires n dependent steps, accuracy should be limited by the number of tokens available — not by the number of parameters, which is fixed.

acc(n) ≈ P( all n steps correct ) ~= pn  for per-step reliability p This is the empirical law you can measure on your own model: find the per-step reliability implied by accuracy versus chain length, and watch where it stops being about p.

Two derived facts worth checking against any model you use:

  • A single wrong step is fatal unless the model catches it. With p = 0.95 per step, a 10-step chain succeeds about 60% of the time (0.9510 ≈ 0.60). This is why long chains in published reasoning models come with explicit self-checking behaviours — checking is a step that repairs p.
  • Faithfulness is not required for usefulness. The written trace need not be the actual computation. What matters is that the emitted tokens carry enough information for the next forward pass to continue the computation. This distinction matters for audits: you cannot read the trace as a proof of the answer (see L75).

The lab below simulates both regimes with a declared generator: a chain of n dependent steps, per-step reliability p, and a budget b of reasoning tokens. It measures accuracy as a function of budget and of n, and also runs self-consistency at k samples.

# L60 Lab 1: the reliability law. Why CoT works, and how fast a chain decays. # No simulation: p^n and its inverse are exact. import math P = 0.95 # per-step reliability of a single reasoning step TOKENS_PER_STEP = 60 print(f"per-step reliability p = {P}") print(f"{'chain length n':>15}{'p^n':>10}{'(1-p)/n elasticity':>22}") for n in (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 10, 15, 20, 30): acc = P ** n # d(ln acc)/d(ln p) = n -> a 1% relative gain in p gives about n% relative gain in acc print(f"{n:>15}{acc:>10.3f}{n:>22}") print("\nthe same table as a budget: to keep 50% success, how reliable must a step be?") for n in (5, 10, 20, 40): need = 0.5 ** (1 / n) print(f" n={n:>3} steps -> p must be {need:.4f}") print("\ncost model: accuracy per 1000 reasoning tokens, at 60 tokens per step") for n in (5, 10, 20): tok = n * TOKENS_PER_STEP print(f" n={n:>3} tokens={tok:>4} acc={P**n:.3f} acc per 1k tokens={P**n / (tok / 1000):.3f}")

Accuracy per 1k tokens falls by roughly 8x from the 5-step to the 20-step case, so longer chains must buy more than linear accuracy to be worth their cost. Falsifier: if your model's accuracy is flat in chain length, the chain is not doing the computation.

Experiment 2 — self-consistency only pays when errors are uncorrelated

k chains, majority vote, with two error structures: failures concentrated on one modal wrong answer, and failures spread across different wrong answers.

# L60 Lab 3: self-consistency, measured. Majority vote over k chains. # The per-step reliability p is set by the task; the chain length n decides how much # signal there is left to vote on. Pure stdlib. import random from collections import Counter rng = random.Random(1) ITEMS, N_ITEMS = 300, 6000 ERR_CONCENTRATED = 0.85 # when a chain fails, how often it agrees with the SAME wrong answer def vote(k, p, n, err_conc): votes = [] for _ in range(k): ok = rng.random() < p ** n if ok: votes.append("RIGHT") else: votes.append("WRONG_A" if rng.random() < err_conc else f"WRONG_{rng.randrange(2, 6)}") top = Counter(votes).most_common(2) if len(top) > 1 and top[0][1] == top[1][1]: return False # tie -> treat as failure return top[0][0] == "RIGHT" print(f"{'p':>5}{'n':>4}{'k':>4}{'majority-vote accuracy':>24} (single chain = first column)") for p, n in ((0.95, 5), (0.95, 10), (0.90, 10), (0.85, 10)): print(f"\n errors concentrated: {ERR_CONCENTRATED:.0%} of failures agree on one wrong answer") for k in (1, 3, 5, 9, 15): ok = sum(vote(k, p, n, ERR_CONCENTRATED) for _ in range(N_ITEMS)) / N_ITEMS print(f"{p:>5.2f}{n:>4}{k:>4}{ok:>24.3f}") print(" errors uncorrelated: each failure is a different wrong answer") for k in (1, 3, 5, 9, 15): ok = sum(vote(k, p, n, 1 / 5) for _ in range(N_ITEMS)) / N_ITEMS print(f"{p:>5.2f}{n:>4}{k:>4}{ok:>24.3f}")

This is the sharpest result on the page. At p = 0.95, n = 10, majority-vote accuracy climbs from 0.60 to 0.93 with k = 9 when errors are uncorrelated — and only to 0.75 when 85% of failures agree on the same wrong answer. Worse, at p = 0.90 the vote actively HURTS (0.35 down to 0.20): the majority is wrong more often than one sample. Self-consistency is an ensemble, and ensembles require independent errors.

Experiment 3 — measure serial depth on a real model

Give one model the same underlying problem twice: once as a direct question, once as a chain with k extra dependent sub-steps appended. If CoT is buying serial compute, accuracy should fall with k in the CoT condition only.

for k in [0, 1, 2, 4, 8]: prompt_direct = base_problem(k) # same numbers, no reasoning asked for prompt_cot = base_problem(k) + " Think step by step." run(prompt_direct, reasoning_budget=0) # record accuracy run(prompt_cot, reasoning_budget=4096) # record accuracy, tokens, truncations # also record: reasoning tokens used, stop-reason (natural vs budget), answered-vs-truncated

Expected shape: the CoT curve sits above the direct curve and the gap widens with k, until the reasoning budget" " truncates and the gap collapses. Record truncations as a separate column — a drop caused by truncation is not a reasoning" " failure, and reporting it as one is how models get blamed for harness bugs.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Truncated chains. Symptom: reasoning stops mid-step and accuracy falls on exactly the items with the longest correct solutions. The budget is too small; the model's "answer" was read from a partial program.
  • Unfaithful traces. Symptom: the reasoning text contradicts the chosen answer, or the answer is constant across different traces. This is not a bug you can fix by better prompting; it is a property of the training objective, and it means the trace cannot be used as evidence.
  • Verbosity without steps. Symptom: token count rises but accuracy does not — the model generates restatements rather than intermediate results. Measure accuracy per 1k reasoning tokens, not per call.
  • Backwards-length mismatch. Symptom: training used full or long traces, inference truncates, and the harness reports a drop blamed on the model. Check the token budget before checking the weights.
  • Format brittleness. Symptom: accuracy swings hugely on a trivial change in the trigger phrase, because the style of the chain was learned as a surface pattern during SFT. Report accuracy across at least two phrasings of the reasoning instruction.
  • Error propagation. Symptom: a visibly wrong early step is carried forward faithfully to a wrong answer, with the model never revisiting it. The fix is not more tokens; it is checking (L59) or self-consistency (lab 2).

7 · Self-check #

Q1. With per-step reliability 0.95 and 10 dependent steps, what is the success probability, and how much does raising p to 0.99 buy?

Reveal answer

0.9510 ≈ 0.599. At 0.99: 0.9910 ≈ 0.904. A 4-point per-step improvement becomes a 30-pointend-to-end improvement. This super-linear sensitivity is why check steps and self-verification are worth their token cost.

Q2. Why does chain-of-thought help a model whose parameters are frozen?

Reveal answer

Because the reasoning tokens become input, so the effective computation is reused: each new token is a fresh pass throughthe same weights but with the previous results present. The model is not getting smarter; it is amortising a fixed circuit overmore serial steps. The cost is linear in tokens (or quadratic without KV-cache tricks) for depth that the architecture cannototherwise achieve.

Q3. Quantitative: model A answers at 3,000 reasoning tokens with 71% accuracy; model B at 1,000 tokens with 68%. Which is better per token, and what else must you report?

Reveal answer

B gains 0.68 per 1k tokens against A's 0.237 — roughly 3x the accuracy per token on this task. But you must alsoreport accuracy per unit cost (tokens differ in price), the accuracy on the hardest chain-length bucket, and the truncationrate. A high efficiency on easy items with collapse on long chains is not a better model, it is a cheaper one for the easy half.

Q4. Diagnostic: a colleague shows a trace where the final answer is correct but step 3 contains a wrong intermediate value. Is the model wrong?

Reveal answer

Not necessarily. The written trace is a sequence of tokens that carried computation, not a certified proof. A wrong-lookingintermediate can be compensated later, and the model may have been running a different computation than the one it described.This is the faithfulness problem: the trace is evidence of what was written, not of how the answer was computed.For auditing, verify the answer independently; never accept the trace as a proof.

Q5. When is CoT the wrong tool?

Reveal answer

When the task has a short computation but a large retrieval or classification surface: there is nothing serial to compute,so reasoning tokens buy only latency and a larger surface for format errors. Also when the output must be constrained (strictJSON) and the reasoning budget competes with output budget, and when the unit economics are tight enough that the tokens arethe dominant cost with no accuracy gain on your eval.
