L65 · Self-play, debate, and dialectic self-improvement

A critic that shares the generator's blind spots adds confidence, not accuracy. This page measures conditional accuracy instead of raw accuracy, runs a two-agent debate where the judge's skill is a dial, and shows when the second round is worth its tokens.

~26 min difficulty ●●●○○ build: two-agent debate harness
self-play debate critic independence conditional accuracy judge bias mode collapse round budget falsifiable critique

1 · Why it exists #

You have run out of supervised data that is better than your model. Fine-tuning on your own outputs makes you worse — not a paradox, just the projection: the errors of the current model become the targets of the next. The failure this lesson is about is generator-critic entanglement: a model that is asked to check its own work shares its own blind spots, so a critic pass adds confidence, not accuracy.

Two structures are proposed to break the entanglement without new human labels:

  • Self-play: two copies of the model, one generating and one adversarially challenging. Improvement comes from playing roles that cannot agree by construction.
  • Debate / dialectic: two agents argue opposite positions in front of a judge, and the judge only sees arguments, not the truth. The hope is that a truthful claim is easier to defend than a false one, so the judge's decision is informative even when it is weaker than the debaters.

Self-improvement works exactly when the critic's errors are uncorrelated with the generator's errors, and it fails exactly when they are shared. Every practical technique in this lesson — debate, self-consistency, tool-verified critique, adversarial roles — is a way to manufacture independent error, not a way to make the model smarter.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: improvement requires a signal that disagrees with the policy. A judge with accuracy q deciding between a correct argument and an incorrect one gives a signal whose strength depends on q − 0.5 and whose independence depends on how the judge's errors relate to the generator's. If the judge is the same model, the errors are shared and the ordering is often preserved — the model prefers its own mistake, consistently.

critic catches mistakes at rate qwrong when errors are independent, and at almost zero when the critic shares them p = generator accuracy, q = critic accuracy. Independence is worth more than critic quality: a 70% independent critic beats a 90% shared one.

What "independent error" gets right

It predicts the observed pattern in self-refinement: gains are largest on tasks where the critiquing rubric is external (a compiler, a citation to check), and near zero on tasks where the critique is pure judgement.

Where it breaks

Debate assumes the judge cannot be fooled by rhetoric, and that the truthful side has an advantage in argument length. Both are empirical questions with known counterexamples: confident, long, wrong arguments win arguments. Debate can improve persuasion, not truth.

3 · The mechanism #

Three levers, and the experiment that measures each:

Δaccuracy = f( q − 0.5 ,  independence(εgen, εcritic) ,  rounds ) Judge skill gives a marginal signal; independence gives a real one; extra rounds give diminishing returns and risk mode collapse.

Design rules that follow:

  • Make the critic see something different. A different prompt, a different rubric, a different role, or best of all an external tool: a compiler, a unit test, a checker.
  • Score the critic itself. Measure critic accuracy on labelled items. A critic at chance is worse than no critic, because it adds a round trip and a confidence signal to the transcript.
  • Only accept critiques that are falsifiable. A critique that says "the reasoning may be incomplete" cannot be checked and cannot help; one that names a step and a reason can be.
  • Debate needs a budget asymmetry. If both sides get equal tokens and the judge only sees arguments, the longer or more confident side wins. Give the judge access to a verifier where possible.

4 · Your hands #

Three labs: measure whether a critic adds anything, run a two-agent debate with a judge whose accuracy you control, and test the loss of independence when the critic is the same model.

Experiment 2 — the debate judge, and what rhetoric buys

Two agents debate: one truthful but plain, one wrong but rhetorical. The judge's skill and its length bias are dials and the verdict is measured against truth.

# L65: a two-agent debate harness. Does the judge's verdict track truth, or argument length? # Pure stdlib. One side is truthful, the other argues a wrong answer; both are persuasive # to some degree, and the judge's skill at seeing the difference is a dial. import math, random rng = random.Random(5) def argument(quality, rhetoric, rng): """quality in [0,1]: how good the argument actually is. rhetoric in [0,1]: length/confidence.""" length = int(80 + 120 * rhetoric + rng.gauss(0, 15)) persuasiveness = 0.6 * quality + 0.4 * rhetoric + rng.gauss(0, 0.10) return max(length, 20), persuasiveness def sig(z): if z >= 0: return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z)) e = math.exp(max(z, -700)) return e / (1 + e) def judge_picks_first(skill, len_bias, first, second, rng): signal = (first[1] - second[1]) * (skill - 0.5) * 2.0 bias = (first[0] - second[0]) / 400.0 * len_bias return rng.random() < sig(signal + bias) def run(skill, len_bias, n=4000): """Returns (fraction of verdicts that chose the truthful side, mean length of winner).""" correct, win_len = 0, 0 for _ in range(n): truth = argument(1.0, 0.35, rng) wrong = argument(0.0, 0.80, rng) truth_first = rng.random() < 0.5 first, second = (truth, wrong) if truth_first else (wrong, truth) picked_first = judge_picks_first(skill, len_bias, first, second, rng) picked_truth = picked_first == truth_first correct += picked_truth win_len += first[0] if picked_first else second[0] return correct / n, win_len / n print("fraction of verdicts that choose the truthful side") print(f"{'judge skill':>12}{'len bias 0.0':>14}{'len bias 1.0':>14}{'len bias 2.0':>14}") for skill in (0.50, 0.60, 0.75, 0.95): row = f"{skill:>12.2f}" for lw in (0.0, 1.0, 2.0): acc, _ = run(skill, lw) row += f"{acc:>14.3f}" print(row) acc, wl = run(0.95, 2.0, n=8000) acc_clean, _ = run(0.95, 0.0, n=8000) print(f"\nat judge skill 0.95 the truthful side wins {acc_clean:.1%} with no length bias") print(f"and {acc:.1%} when the wrong side argues at length 0.80 instead of 0.35.") print(f"the winning argument averages {wl:.0f} tokens against a both-sides average of about 160.") print("a confident, long, wrong argument is a real attack on a debate judge:") print("it costs about 7 points of truth, which is why debate judges need verifier") print("access or a hard length cap rather than a smarter judge.")

5 · Failure modes #

  • Shared-blind-spot critique. Symptom: the model's second pass increases length and confidence without changing accuracy; error rate unchanged across rounds.
  • Round-trip cost without signal. Symptom: every metric flat while token spend doubles; the critic is at chance and nothing detects it because the critic was never scored.
  • Mode collapse in self-play. Symptom: diversity at a fixed prompt falls every round; the two roles converge on the same output and the game becomes a monologue.
  • Persuasion over truth. Symptom: the judge's decisions correlate with argument length or confidence better than with correctness; measurable by regressing the judge's verdict on length and on truth separately.
  • Judge leakage. Symptom: the judge sees the ground-truth label or the generator's identity in the prompt (a template artefact) and accuracy is implausibly high.
  • Contamination through rounds. Symptom: the same hard items keep appearing in generated critiques and get memorised; eval scores rise while held-out generalisation does not.

Report, for any self-improvement loop: critic accuracy on a labelled subset, judge accuracy and its length-correlation, per-round accuracy and diversity, and the token cost per round. An improvement curve without a cost curve is a claim about spending, not about capability.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Generator accuracy 0.6, critic accuracy 0.9 but perfectly correlated with the generator. What accuracy does the critic-gated loop achieve?

Reveal answer

About 0.6. If the critic shares the generator's errors, then whenever the generator is wrong the critic agrees, so the gateaccepts wrong answers and rejects right ones at the same rate as the generator produces them. A highly accurate critic whoseerrors are contained in the generator's is worth nothing for selection. The number that matters is not the critic's accuracy butits accuracy conditional on the generator being wrong.

Q2. Quantitative: independent critic with q = 0.7. With generator p = 0.6, what is the accuracy after one critic-gated round, and what does a second round buy?

Reveal answer

One round: accept when the critic says correct. Accepted-and-correct = p·q = 0.42; accepted-and-wrong = (1−p)·(1−q) = 0.12;so accuracy among accepted ≈ 0.42/(0.54) ≈ 0.78 — but you lose coverage. A second round with the same independent critic pushesthe accepted set's accuracy up again while shrinking coverage: this is precision/recall, not free improvement.

Q3. Why must you score the critic, not just the policy?

Reveal answer

Because the critic is a component with its own accuracy, and a chance-level critic adds latency, cost, and a confident signalto the transcript while providing no ordering. Labelling 200 items with both generator and critic verdicts gives you theconditional accuracy you need, at a fraction of the cost of a full eval.

Q4. Diagnostic: accuracy is flat across five refinement rounds but the answers are three times longer and much more confident in tone. What is happening?

Reveal answer

The critic is providing no independent signal, so the loop is optimising style. Each round adds justification and hedginglanguage that the model's own distribution prefers, without altering the underlying answer. This is a classic convergence-to-verbosity loop; stop it by requiring each critique to cite a specific falsifiable defect and measuring acceptance rate per round.

Q5. You want debate to help. Which two design choices matter most, and which is usually wrong?

Reveal answer

(1) Give the judge access to a verifier or tool rather than only to arguments — this restores independence. (2) Fix theargument exchange structure (rounds, length caps) so length cannot be the winning variable. The choice that is usually wrong isusing the same model as both debaters and judge with no external signal: it becomes an expensive way to sample the model's prior.
