TRACK C · POST-TRAINING & REASONING

L66 · Distilling reasoning: rejection sampling and trace recycling

The reasoning model you can afford to train is not the one you can afford to serve. Rejection sampling turns a teacher's successes into a student's ceiling — and its yield, not its accuracy, decides whether the pipeline is economic.

~26 min difficulty ●●●○○ build: rejection-sampling loop
rejection sampling yield teacher-student verifier filter zero-yield band trace recycling offline policy improvement provenance

1 · Why it exists #

The best reasoning model you can afford to train is almost never the one you can afford to serve. And the obvious fix — train the small model with RL directly — often fails: small models explore less usefully and their reasoning traces are short and shallow, so there is little to reinforce. The failure this lesson exists to fix is capability sitting in a teacher you cannot deploy.

Reasoning distillation is the published answer. Generate a large pool of traces from the teacher, keep only the ones that pass a verifier, and run ordinary SFT on the student. No reward model, no RL loop, no critic — the verifier is used as a filter rather than as a reward. DeepSeek-R1 (arXiv:2501.12948) reports this stage as part of its pipeline, and it is the reason consumer-scale models shipped with visible reasoning traces at all.

Rejection sampling is a search procedure disguised as a data pipeline. You are looking for outputs that clear a bar the teacher sometimes fails to clear. The yield — the fraction of sampled traces that pass — is the quantity that determines whether the whole approach is economic, and it is a number you can measure before spending on training.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: SFT on filtered teacher outputs is offline policy improvement. The teacher is a policy; the verifier is a filter; the student imitates the filtered distribution. The student's ceiling is the teacher's filtered capability, not the teacher's average behaviour — which is why filtering can make a student beat its teacher's average score.

yield = Eprompt [ 1 − (1 − pteacher)k ]   |   student ceiling ≈ teacher accuracy under the filter p is the teacher's pass rate on a prompt, k the samples per prompt. With k samples, the expected number of passing traces per prompt is 1 − (1−p)^k.

What "offline policy improvement" gets right

It explains why filtering beats imitation: you are not fitting the teacher's noise, you are fitting its successes. And it predicts the main failure: the student inherits the hole in the teacher's coverage — prompts where the teacher never passed produce no data at all.

Where it breaks

RL can explore past the teacher; SFT cannot. A distilled student is bounded by what the teacher produced, while an RL-trained student can in principle discover a method the teacher never used. Distillation buys a cheaper deployment, not a better policy.

3 · The mechanism #

The pipeline, in the order that decides whether it is economic:

yieldband = passes / samples   →   cost per usable trace = (k · trace cost) / passes Compute this per difficulty band before training. A band with yield below a few percent is paying for traces you discard, and its prompts will have no data at all.

Track three numbers per band: yield, mean trace length of accepted traces, and the fraction of prompts with zero accepted traces. Then run SFT only on the prompts that produced data, and add an eval specifically for the prompts that did not.

4 · Your hands #

Three labs: simulate the rejection-sampling loop and its economics, measure the ceiling a student can inherit, and decide how many samples per prompt you need before the yield is acceptable.

Experiment 2 — the ceiling the student inherits

Do not measure student capability against the teacher's average. Measure it against the teacher's filtered accuracy and against the teacher's accuracy on the prompts that produced data.

teacher_acc_all = 0.41 # every prompt in the eval teacher_acc_covered = 0.58 # only prompts where at least one trace passed student_acc_all = 0.39 student_acc_covered = 0.55 # the honest comparison is the middle column, and the gap that matters is # student_acc_all - student_acc on ZERO-yield prompts (measured separately)

A student at 0.55 on covered prompts looks like a 14-point gain against the teacher's 0.41 average, and looks like a 3-point loss against the teacher's 0.58 filtered performance. Both statements are true and only one is useful for planning. Report the zero-yield band as its own line, always.

Rejection-sampled data is generated, filtered, and mixed into an SFT stage. This is the minimum record per trace.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Zero-yield bands. Symptom: whole categories of prompts have no accepted traces, so the student is untrained on them; accuracy on those bands equals the base model's.
  • Verifier-filtered artefact. Symptom: student reasoning contains syntax the verifier likes rather than the method; test by scoring student traces with an independent grader.
  • Length mismatch. Symptom: student traces are much shorter than teacher traces because most long ones were truncated; accuracy collapses on long-chain items.
  • Format drift. Symptom: mixed languages or style artefacts inside traces; they are copied perfectly by the student because SFT has no preference.
  • Silent distribution shift. Symptom: student excels on the training prompts and fails on paraphrases; the prompt pool was narrow.
  • Teacher overfit. Symptom: student reproduces the teacher's specific errors on items where the verifier is weak. Filter strength is only as good as the verifier's coverage (lesson 63).

Keep the sampling record: for every accepted trace, the teacher id, sampler settings, verifier version and hash, and the acceptance rule. Rejection-sampled data is the most provenance-critical data in the pipeline — it is generated, filtered, and mixed into a stage where contamination checks (lesson 74) need to know exactly what came from where.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Teacher pass rate 0.4 on a band, k = 5 samples per prompt. What is the yield, and what does it cost per usable trace?

Reveal answer

Yield = 1 − 0.65 = 1 − 0.0778 = 0.922. But the number of accepted traces per prompt isk·p = 2.0, so if you cap at one accepted trace per prompt you accept about 1.0 of the 2 available and the marginalcost per usable trace is roughly half a prompt's k-sample cost. The distinction matters: yield measures whether you have dataat all, cost per usable trace measures whether it is worth generating.

Q2. Why can a distilled student beat its teacher's average accuracy, even though it never exceeds the teacher on any prompt?

Reveal answer

Because the student is trained on the teacher's successes. The teacher's average includes the prompts it fails on,and the student learns from the accepted traces only — effectively an offline policy improvement over the teacher's samplingdistribution. It cannot beat the teacher on prompts where the teacher never passes, which is exactly why zero-yield bands needtheir own eval.

Q3. Quantitative: traces cost 1,200 tokens on average, sampling costs nothing per token today, and training costs are fixed. Yield is 8% on a band. What is the argument against using that band?

Reveal answer

8% yield means about 12.5 samples per accepted trace. If the trace pool is capped, that band consumes the majority of thesampling budget for a minority of the data. The decision is not never but cap it: take the accepted traces you get, but donot scale sampling there, and record the zero-yield prompts for a future RL stage that can improve the teacher first.

Q4. Diagnostic: distilled student passes the eval but fails on paraphrases of the same questions. What is the likely cause and the check?

Reveal answer

The prompt pool was too narrow relative to the eval's semantic range. Check: sample the accepted traces by prompt template andmeasure how many distinct templates exist; then run an eval slice of paraphrases. If the drop is large, the data pipeline needsprompt augmentation (paraphrase the prompt, not the trace) before more sampling.

Q5. What is one thing RL training can do that rejection-sampling distillation cannot, and why does that matter for pipeline order?

Reveal answer

RL can explore outside the teacher's output distribution and discover methods the teacher never produced. Distillation isbounded by the teacher's coverage. So the pipeline order matters: run RL to push capability, then distil to deploy it. If youdistil first and RL second, the RL stage starts from a student that cannot produce the novel traces it would need to explore.
