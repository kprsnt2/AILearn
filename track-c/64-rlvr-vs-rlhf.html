L64 · RLVR vs RLHF: what each signal can and cannot teach

RLVR teaches what a program can check; RLHF teaches what a person can compare. Choosing one for the other's job does not give you a weaker version of the other — it gives you a policy optimised to exploit the gap.

~25 min difficulty ●●○○○ build: capability-boundary table
RLVR RLHF verifier coverage learned reward reward mixture scale normalisation over-refusal projection gap

1 · Why it exists #

Teams routinely ask for the wrong signal and pay for it. "Make the model more helpful" with a rubric-trained judge, when the actual failure is arithmetic. "Make it safer" with an RLVR loop over harmful-prompt refusals, when the actual failure is that refusals are over-triggered on safe clinical text. Both fail for the same reason: the reward you can build is not the reward the task requires, and choosing between RLVR and RLHF is choosing which mismatch you can live with.

The two signals differ in a way that is structural, not stylistic:

  • RLVR (verifiable reward) is exact within its coverage. If the checker accepts it, the answer is right for the checkable part of the task. Outside its coverage it is silent — the reward has nothing to say about clarity, safety, or style.
  • RLHF (learned preference reward) is broad but approximate. It can rank anything a person can rank, and it is systematically wrong wherever human preference is a proxy for something you did not define.

RLVR teaches capabilities that can be checked — computation, correctness, format, tool use. RLHF teaches conventions and tradeoffs that can be compared — tone, safety, helpfulness, length. Asking either to do the other's job does not produce a mediocre version of the other; it produces a policy optimised to exploit the mismatch.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: each signal is a projection of "quality" onto a different subspace. RLVR projects onto verifiable correctness; the kernel of that projection is "everything no program checks". RLHF projects onto human-preferred surface; the kernel is "everything humans cannot see or would not agree on".

Novel reasoning routes no demonstration showsYes (given reward variance)LimitedRLVR explores; preference data only ranks what was generated

What "projection" gets right

It explains behaviour you will observe reproducibly: RLVR runs produce models that are better at checking, worse at tone; RLHF runs produce models that are better at tone, occasionally worse at factuality because fluency correlates with preference.

Where it breaks

The subspaces are not orthogonal. A correct answer that is unparseable scores zero under RLVR and poorly under RLHF, and both are right. Real pipelines mix the two signals, and the mixture weights are a product decision with its own hacking surface.

3 · The mechanism #

Mixed reward, with the weight as the only knob:

R(y) = λ · V(y)  +  (1 − λ) · RMφ(y) V is a verifier (0/1 or partial), RM is a learned preference reward. λ = weights how much of the objective is checkable.

Two facts about that mixture are worth knowing before touching λ:

  • The variance comes from the verifier, the bias from the reward model. Increasing λ reduces reward-hacking surface and increases variance (and zero-variance groups). The right λ is the largest value that still leaves enough variance for the group baseline.
  • The two components must be on comparable scales. A verifier in {0,1} mixed at λ = 0.5 with a reward model in [−8, +8] is a reward model with a rounding term; the mixture arithmetic is silently decided by scale, not by λ. Normalise both before mixing.

The lab below constructs the capability boundary empirically: a task family where the checkable part is correctness and the uncheckable part is concision. It optimises a policy under each reward and prints what each signal teaches.

# L64: what each signal teaches. Two rewards, one task family, same optimiser. # Task: pick a response with two properties -- (correct?, concise?). Pure stdlib. import math # Four behaviours: (correct, concise) -> the observable outcome and the reward each signal pays. BEHAVIOURS = { "terse_correct": dict(correct=0.35, concise=1.0), "verbose_correct": dict(correct=0.75, concise=0.0), "terse_wrong": dict(correct=0.02, concise=1.0), "verbose_wrong": dict(correct=0.20, concise=0.0), } NAMES = list(BEHAVIOURS) def verifier(b): """Program-checkable part only: is the final answer right?""" return BEHAVIOURS[b]["correct"] def reward_model(b): """What a rater prefers: mostly correctness, but concision is visible and gets credit.""" return 0.70 * BEHAVIOURS[b]["correct"] + 0.30 * BEHAVIOURS[b]["concise"] def softmax(z): m = max(z) e = [math.exp(v - m) for v in z] s = sum(e) return [v / s for v in e] def optimise(reward_fn, steps=400, lr=2.0): """Direct expected-reward ascent: pi moves to put mass on the highest-reward behaviours.""" logits = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0] r = [reward_fn(b) for b in NAMES] for _ in range(steps): p = softmax(logits) g = [p[i] * (r[i] - sum(pi * ri for pi, ri in zip(p, r))) for i in range(4)] logits = [zi + lr * gi for zi, gi in zip(logits, g)] return softmax(logits) print(f"{'behaviour':<17}{'verifier':>10}{'RM':>7}") for b in NAMES: print(f"{b:<17}{verifier(b):>10.2f}{reward_model(b):>7.2f}") for label, fn in (("RLVR (verifier only)", verifier), ("RLHF (learned RM)", reward_model)): p = optimise(fn) exp_correct = sum(pi * BEHAVIOURS[b]["correct"] for b, pi in zip(NAMES, p)) exp_concise = sum(pi * BEHAVIOURS[b]["concise"] for b, pi in zip(NAMES, p)) top = max(zip(p, NAMES))[1] print(f"\n{label}") print(f" learned policy -> {top} (P={max(p):.3f})") print(f" expected correctness {exp_correct:.3f} expected concision {exp_concise:.3f}") # scale trap: the mixture weight is meaningless unless the two scales match print("\nmixing at lambda=0.5 without normalising:") for b in NAMES: mixed = 0.5 * verifier(b) + 0.5 * (reward_model(b) * 10 - 5) # RM expressed on a -5..+5 scale print(f" {b:<17} mixed={mixed:+.3f}")

4 · Your hands #

Three labs: watch RLVR teach correctness and ignore form, watch RLHF teach form and pay for it in correctness, and compute a mixed reward with the scales fixed.

Experiment 1 — same task, two signals, two different models

Four behaviours judged on two properties: is the answer right, and is it concise. The verifier sees only correctness; the reward model pays 70% for correctness and 30% for what a rater can see. Both policies are optimised exactly.

Experiment 3 — the outside-coverage eval

Every stage needs an eval for what its signal cannot see. This is the audit artefact that turns "we improved the model" into a claim with a boundary.

stage: rlvr-arithmetic signal : exact-match verifier, 0/1 INSIDE coverage : final numeric answer, unit correctness OUTSIDE coverage : explanation quality, refusal behaviour, format compliance, latency outside evals run : refusal_rate_on_benign (n=500), format_compliance (n=300), mean_tokens gate : inside +≥ 5 pts AND every outside eval within tolerance

Fill this in before the run. If you cannot name the outside set, the stage will optimise into it and the report will" " show only the inside number. Suspiciously uniform gains across unrelated metrics are usually a signal that the outside eval is" " not measuring anything.

5 · Failure modes #

  • RLVR on a non-verifiable goal. Symptom: the verifier's coverage is a narrow slice, and the model degrades everywhere else — e.g. an exact-match reward that teaches terse answers with no explanation.
  • RLHF on a verifiable goal. Symptom: reviewers approve answers that are subtly wrong; the model learns fluent confidence, and factuality on a held-out graded set regresses while preference accuracy improves.
  • Scale mismatch in a mixture. Symptom: the mixture behaves identically at λ = 0.1 and 0.9; one component dominates because its units are 16x larger.
  • Verifier coverage drift. Symptom: the same checker covers fewer task variants after the task set is extended, so the reward silently becomes partly constant — zero-variance groups climb.
  • Annotator proxy learning. Symptom: RLHF run with rubric-based pairs teaches formatting rules nobody intended, because rubric compliance is easier to see than correctness.
  • Cross-stage asking. Symptom: an RLHF stage is used to repair a capability regression caused by an RLVR stage, without removing the RLVR reward — the two objectives fight and the checkpoint oscillates across runs.

For every post-training stage, write down which capabilities are inside the signal's coverage and which are outside, and add an eval for the outside set. A stage without an outside-coverage eval is a stage that will optimise into the gap and report victory.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why does RLVR explore reasoning routes that RLHF cannot, even with the same policy and budget?

Reveal answer

RLHF's signal comes from comparisons between generated outputs, so it can only rank what the policy already produces; if abetter route to the answer is never sampled, no preference can express it. RLVR scores any output by running a program, soa novel route that reaches a correct answer is rewarded when it finally appears, and the variance it creates keeps thesearch alive (lesson 56).

Q2. Quantitative: a verifier in {0,1} is mixed at λ = 0.5 with a reward model in [−8, +8]. What is the verifier's effective weight, and how do you fix it?

Reveal answer

The reward model contributes up to ±8 points while the verifier contributes 0.5, so the verifier's effective influence isunder a tenth of the range: λ = 0.5 is a lie. Fix by normalising both to a common scale before mixing — e.g. z-score thereward model within a batch and keep the verifier as 0/1, then mix with a weight that actually reflects the intent.

Q3. You want better refusals on harmful prompts. Which signal, and what is the coverage gap you must add an eval for?

Reveal answer

RLHF (or a rubric judge): the class boundary is a judgement, and a verifier covers only the harmful prompts you enumerated. Thecoverage gap is everything not in your list, and the specific risk is over-refusal on benign-but-scary-sounding prompts (clinical,security research, fiction). So the outside-coverage eval must include a large benign set with false-refusal rates, and that evalmust be reported next to the refusal rate on the harmful set.

Q4. Diagnostic: after a stage, exact-match accuracy is up 12 points and human ratings of helpfulness are down. Which signal was used, and what does the drop tell you?

Reveal answer

An RLVR-style signal (exact match). The drop is the projection gap: exact match is silent about everything a rater valuesbeyond correctness, so the policy co-adapted to the narrow checkable form, likely becoming terse or dropping explanations. This isnot a bug; it is the expected reduction. The fix is a mixture or a second stage, and the audit lesson is that the outside-coverageeval should have caught it before shipping.

Q5. When should a pipeline use both signals in the same stage rather than in sequence?

Reveal answer

When the two objectives interact strongly enough that optimising one degrades the other — e.g. terse answers are easier toverify but less helpful. In sequence, the second stage partly undoes the first and you land on a compromise neither signal endorses.In one stage with a normalised mixture, the policy sees the trade off directly. The cost is a second hacking surface, so bothcomponents need their own adversarial suite.
