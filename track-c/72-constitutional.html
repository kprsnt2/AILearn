L72 · Constitutional AI, AI feedback, and rubric-based judging

A written principle scales judgement the way a human annotator cannot — and turns the objective into a text the model will parse literally. This page scores a real rubric, then writes the cheapest way to pass it, then closes the hole.

~26 min difficulty ●●●○○ build: rubric critic
constitution RLAIF rubric criteria anti-pattern terms literal compliance critic sycophancy over-refusal rubric versioning

1 · Why it exists #

Constitutional AI (arXiv:2212.08073) exists because human harm-labelling does not scale and does not specify. To train a model to refuse harmful requests you need to grade attempts, and human graders are expensive, slow, inconsistent, and — for the worst content — unwilling. The failure is the cost and inconsistency of judgement itself, not a lack of examples.

The solution is to replace the human grader with a written criterion the model applies to itself: sample a response, ask the model to critique it against a specific principle, revise, then train on the revised output. A second stage ranks the model's own responses by the same principles. The "constitution" is a list of principles — the reward is replaced by a checklist.

A constitution is a rubric, and a rubric-driven critic is a reward model with the weights written in English. It scales because it is cheap; it fails the same way a reward model fails — the model learns to satisfy the rubric's letter. The engineering is in the rubric's specificity and in the adversarial suite that checks the letter is not all that was learned.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: three loops and one substitution.

sample response → critique against principle P → revise → (train on revised) sample two responses → AI ranks them against P → (train on the preference)

The substitution is human preference data → rubric-conditioned AI preference data. That changes the failure profile in three specific ways:

PropertyHuman annotatorsRubric-driven critic
Cost per judgementHigh, scales linearlyNear zero, scales with tokens
ConsistencyModerate; drifts over a long projectHigh at temperature 0; varies with sampling
Correlated error with the policyLow (different model, different failure)High (same model, same blind spots)
What it measuresHuman intent, including things not written downExactly the rubric's words, and nothing else
Failure signatureNoise, disagreementLiteral compliance: the model optimises the rubric's letter

What "a rubric with the weights in English" gets right

It makes the objective inspectable: you can read the rubric, version it, and argue about its wording. That is a real audit advantage over an opaque reward model.

Where it breaks

English is ambiguous and the optimiser resolves ambiguity in its own favour. "Be concise" becomes "be short to the point of uselessness"; "cite sources" becomes "include something that looks like a citation". Each rubric clause therefore needs its own negative examples — the literal-compliance failure — written before the loop runs.

3 · The mechanism #

Design the rubric so it can be scored, not just read:

score(y) = Σk wk · 1[ criterionk(y) met ]  −  Σj pj · 1[ violationj(y) found ] Criteria are things to do; violations are specific negative examples ("claims a source exists without naming it"). Both need to be enumerable, or you cannot score the critique.

Four rubric rules that survive contact with an optimiser:

  1. Every clause needs an observable test. "Helpful" is not a clause; "states the answer in the first two sentences" is.
  2. Every clause needs an anti-pattern. Write the cheapest way to pass it and list that as a violation.
  3. Weight the negatives higher than the positives. Rewarding the presence of a feature invites its insertion everywhere; penalising a specific wrong behaviour is harder to game.
  4. Version and hash the rubric. A rubric edit is a change to the objective; two runs against different rubric versions are two experiments.

4 · Your hands #

Three labs: score a set of responses against a concrete rubric, find the cheapest way to pass it, and measure how much a v2 rubric with anti-patterns changes the attack's score.

Experiment 3 — the rubric record

The rubric is the objective. Store it like one.

{"constitution": "v2 (2026-10)", "sha256": "...", "criteria": {"answer_first": 0.30, "has_citation": 0.30, "shows_steps": 0.20, "is_concise": 0.20}, "violations": {"citation_without_locator": 0.35, "ritual_steps": 0.35, "padding": 0.35, "refusal_on_benign": 0.50}, "precedence": ["safety_violation > any positive clause"], "attack_suite": ["stuffed", "padded", "refuse-everything", "ritual-steps-only"], "attack_suite_max_score": -0.25}

If attack_suite_max_score is close to the best genuine response's score, the rubric has a hole —" " before the loop has ever run. Two runs against different sha256 values are different experiments, and the attack" " suite travels with the rubric so the hole cannot be quietly reopened.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Literal compliance. Symptom: every rubric clause is nominally satisfied and the output is unusable; e.g. "shows reasoning" becomes a three-line ritual.
  • Rubric-stuffing. Symptom: outputs include artefacts of every clause in every answer — citations on conversations, hedging on confident facts.
  • Critic sycophancy. Symptom: the critic approves whatever the policy produced when both are the same model; measured by a critic-accuracy check on labelled pairs.
  • Principle conflict. Symptom: two clauses pull opposite ways (conciseness vs completeness) and behaviour oscillates across rubric versions; make precedence explicit.
  • Unversioned rubric. Symptom: an improvement cannot be reproduced, and nobody can say which wording produced it.
  • Constitution as a compliance label. Symptom: outputs claim compliance rather than demonstrate it — detect by requiring evidence per clause (a quote, a citation with a resolvable locator).

Store the rubric, its hash, its version, and the anti-pattern list with every run. Then write the attack suite: for each clause, a deliberately stuffed response and a deliberately empty one. If the rubric scores the stuffed response highly, you have found a failure the optimiser will find too.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why is a rubric-driven critic more vulnerable to correlated error than a human annotator?

Reveal answer

The critic and the policy are samples of the same model, so they share systematic blind spots: the critique cannot see anerror the generator reliably makes, so the revision loop converges on it. A human annotator has a different model of the task,so their errors are largely independent and their disagreement carries information.

Q2. Quantitative: a rubric rewards presence of four features, each 0.25, and has no violation terms. A stuffed response that dumps all four features satisfies what, and what does the optimiser learn?

Reveal answer

It scores 1.00, satisfying every clause literally. The optimiser learns to include all four features in every answer regardlessof relevance — verbosity, spurious citations, gratuitous hedging. The fix is anti-pattern terms: features presented without relevance to the task must deduct more than the presence of a feature adds.

Q3. Write the anti-pattern for two clauses: states-a-source and shows-reasoning.

Reveal answer

States-a-source: anti-pattern = names a source that cannot be resolved, or presents an authority claim without alocator. Shows-reasoning: anti-pattern = emits a numbered list of steps that does not change the answer, or restates thequestion with different words. In both cases the anti-pattern is the cheapest literal way to satisfy the clause, which isexactly what an optimiser will find.

Q4. Diagnostic: after a constitution-style refinement stage, refusals on harmful prompts rise 5 points and refusals on benign prompts rise 12 points. What does the rubric over-weight, and how do you check?

Reveal answer

The safety clause: it has no anti-pattern for over-refusal, so the optimiser gains by refusing. Check: score the rubric'scriteria on a benign set with scary surface features; if the safety clause is satisfied by refusing, the clause is a biastoward caution. The fix is a paired criterion (refuses harmful AND does not refuse benign) plus a violation term forunnecessary refusal.

Q5. Why version and hash a rubric, when it is just a paragraph of English?

Reveal answer

Because it is the objective. Any change — one word of precedence, one added clause — changes what the model optimises, and tworuns against different versions are not comparable. Hashing makes the change auditable; versioning makes the comparison honest.
