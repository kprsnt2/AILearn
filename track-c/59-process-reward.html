L59 · Process rewards and step-level verification

Outcome reward cannot tell a 90%-correct trace from nonsense, so it pushes on both equally. Process reward can assign credit to the step that failed — and can also teach rhetorical form instead of reasoning. This page does the credit arithmetic and then attacks the verifier.

outcome reward process reward potential-based shaping step-level labels credit assignment PRM MCTS-derived targets step definition

1 · Why it exists #

Outcome-only reward has a specific, structural blind spot: credit assignment over the steps of a trajectory with a single scalar at the end. Four reasoning steps, one of which is wrong, give the same reward as a lucky guess that happens to land on the right answer. The estimator cannot tell them apart, so the policy gradient pushes on all four steps equally — leaving promising prefixes that later fail with no reward, and forcing promising prefixes that later succeed with equal weight.

The failure this lesson is about is subtler than "sparse reward is hard". It is that outcome reward is systematically wrong about credit, which has two named consequences:

  • Wasted prefixes. A reasoning trace that is 90% correct and fails at the last step scores 0, exactly like a trace that was nonsense from the first token. The gradient pushes both down.
  • Learned bad habits that succeed. If a shortcut occasionally reaches the right answer, outcome reward reinforces the shortcut at every step that preceded it. Process reward can object at the step where the shortcut appeared; outcome reward cannot see it.

Outcome reward answers "was the answer right?". Process reward answers "was each step warranted by the steps before it?". Only the second can assign credit, but only the first is verifiable from a program's output. That asymmetry — process reward must be learned or machine-checked, outcome reward can be computed — is the whole tradeoff.

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: a reward is a function on a trajectory; a process reward defines a potential on states, and credit is the difference of potentials along the path.

What "denser signal is better" gets right

Credit assignment is a variance problem. A per-step potential cuts the variance of the advantage estimate by orders of magnitude on long traces, and that is measurable in the lab below.

Where it breaks

Dense signal can be wrong in a way sparse signal cannot be. An outcome is checkable; a step judgement is an opinion, and the policy will optimise the opinion. A process reward that prefers "looks like careful logic" teaches rhetorical form, not reasoning.

3 · The mechanism #

The potential-based formulation is the one to use when you can compute a per-step value, because it is the only shaping that is guaranteed not to change the optimal policy:

r′(st, at) = r(st, at) − Vref(st) + γVref(st+1) Vref is any potential; the discounted sum telescopes, so total shaping over a full trajectory equals −V(s0), which does not depend on the policy.

Prerequisite for the lab: a "step" must be defined. In a reasoning trace a step is a sentence or a line; in an agent trajectory it is a tool call plus its observation. Choose it explicitly, because the reward's resolution is exactly the step size.

4 · Your hands #

Three labs. The first shows the credit-assignment arithmetic on a declared sequence of steps so there is nothing to simulate; the second runs a 12-line PRM; the third runs the adversarial test that you should write before trusting any step scorer.

Experiment 1 — localise the error with potentials

Five declared steps, one of which is wrong. The potentials are success rates of continuing from that point; the outcome reward is a single 0 at the end.

Experiment 2 — does the PRM track logic or confidence?

Ten traces described by two features: logic validity and confident-sounding wording. Fit a PRM on each feature set and compare its probability of correctness.

# L59 Lab 2 + adversarial test: is a step verifier a proxy for correctness, or for confidence? # Pure stdlib. Logistic regression on step-level features. import math # Each trace: logic quality (fraction of valid steps), flourish (confident/careful wording), correct? TRACES = [ (0.95, 0.20, True), (0.90, 0.30, True), (0.85, 0.10, True), (0.75, 0.90, False), # confident-sounding AND wrong -- the adversarial case (0.70, 0.95, False), (0.40, 0.20, False), (0.35, 0.10, False), (0.60, 0.50, True), (0.55, 0.60, False), (0.80, 0.40, True), ] def fit(feats, labels, ridge=1e-2, steps=4000, lr=0.5): w = [0.0] * len(feats[0]) for _ in range(steps): g = [0.0] * len(w) for f, y in zip(feats, labels): z = sum(wi * fi for wi, fi in zip(w, f)) p = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z)) for i in range(len(w)): g[i] += (y - p) * f[i] for i in range(len(w)): w[i] += lr * (g[i] / len(feats) - ridge * w[i]) return w def s(w, f): z = sum(wi * fi for wi, fi in zip(w, f)) return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z)) y = [1.0 if t[2] else 0.0 for t in TRACES] w_logic = fit([(t[0],) for t in TRACES], y) w_full = fit([(t[0], t[1]) for t in TRACES], y) print("PRM(logic) : weights =", [round(x, 3) for x in w_logic]) print("PRM(logic + flourish): weights =", [round(x, 3) for x in w_full], "<- flourish learned as a NEGATIVE predictor") print(f"\n{'trace':<34}{'PRM(logic)':>12}{'PRM(both)':>11}") for lg, fl, ok in TRACES: print(f"logic={lg:.2f} flourish={fl:.2f} {'right' if ok else 'WRONG':<6}" f"{s(w_logic, (lg,)):>12.3f}{s(w_full, (lg, fl)):>11.3f}") a = (0.75, 0.90) # confident and wrong b = (0.85, 0.10) # plain and right print(f"\nadversarial pair: confident-WRONG={s(w_full, a):.3f} vs plain-RIGHT={s(w_full, b):.3f}") print("rank correlation between PRM(both) and correctness, over the 10 traces:") sa = sorted(range(len(TRACES)), key=lambda i: s(w_full, TRACES[i][:2])) sb = sorted(range(len(TRACES)), key=lambda i: y[i]) import statistics ra = [0] * 10 rb = [0] * 10 for rank, i in enumerate(sa): ra[i] = rank for rank, i in enumerate(sb): rb[i] = rank print(" Spearman-style rank agreement:", round(statistics.correlation(ra, rb), 3))

Experiment 3 — the adversarial perturbation test

Three perturbations that should not change the score if the scorer measures the answer. Anything that raises the score is a path an optimiser will find.

# L59 Lab 3: the adversarial test that belongs in CI for any step scorer. # Any perturbation that RAISES the score is a path the optimiser will find. Pure stdlib. GOOD_TRACE = [ "s0 restate: 45 minutes at 60 km/h", "s1 45 min = 0.75 h", "s2 d = v * t = 60 * 0.75", "s3 d = 45 km", ] def toy_scorer(trace): """Stand-in for a real PRM: rewards arithmetic-looking steps and a numeric finish.""" score = 0.0 for step in trace: if any(tok in step for tok in ("=", "*", "+")): score += 0.25 if step.strip()[-1].isdigit(): score += 0.10 return round(score, 2) def pad(trace): # plausible-looking extra steps the answer does not need return trace + ["s4 check: 45 min = 0.75 h (again)", "s5 check: d = 60 * 0.75 = 45"] def shuffle(trace): return [trace[0]] + trace[2:][::-1] + [trace[1]] def restyle(trace): return [s.replace("=", "is equal to").replace("*", "times") for s in trace] base = toy_scorer(GOOD_TRACE) print(f"{'variant':<32}{'score':>7}{'delta':>8} verdict") print(f"{'original trace':<32}{base:>7.2f}{0.0:>+8.2f} reference") for name, fn in (("padded with redundant checks", pad), ("steps reordered", shuffle), ("arithmetic restyled as prose", restyle)): v = toy_scorer(fn(GOOD_TRACE)) print(f"{name:<32}{v:>7.2f}{v - base:>+8.2f} {'EXPLOITABLE' if v > base else 'robust'}") print("\nthe padded trace contains the same answer plus two redundant checks, and scores higher.") print("a real RL run will discover exactly that, at scale, without being told to.")
variant score delta verdict original trace 0.85 +0.00 reference padded with redundant checks 1.45 +0.60 EXPLOITABLE steps reordered 0.85 +0.00 robust arithmetic restyled as prose 0.10 -0.75 robust the padded trace contains the same answer plus two redundant checks, and scores higher. a real RL run will discover exactly that, at scale, without being told to.

Padding with redundant checks raises the score by 0.60 while the answer is unchanged. That is the review question in one line: what happens to the score when the answer is held fixed? Run this against your real PRM before RL ever touches it.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Step-boundary mismatch. Symptom: the PRM scores at different granularity from what RL optimises; tricks emerge that add plausible-looking steps without changing the answer. The step definition must be identical in the PRM and the RL environment.
  • Flattered prefixes. Symptom: a PRM gives high step scores to visibly wrong reasoning because the wording is confident. This is a reward model at step level with all of lesson 58's failure modes plus a smaller data set.
  • Non-potential shaping. Symptom: with an arbitrary per-step bonus, the optimal policy becomes one that farms the bonus — e.g. producing more steps. Test: does optimal behaviour change when you turn shaping off? If yes, it was not potential-based.
  • Step-label ambiguity. Symptom: annotators cannot agree whether a step is "correct" when the intermediate result is right for the wrong reason. Store the rubric's step definition and measure agreement at step level, not answer level.
  • Cost blow-up. Symptom: PRM training data costs grow with trace length squared (every prefix needs a label), and you silently end up with far less data per token than you planned.
  • Verifier gaming at inference. Symptom: best-of-n with a PRM steers toward whatever the PRM likes, not what is right — detectable by comparing best-of-n-with-PRM against best-of-n-with-outcome-grader on the same samples.

For every process reward, report three numbers: step-level agreement with a held-out human sample, the correlation between PRM score and final-answer correctness on fresh traces, and the best-of-n gap versus the outcome grader. If the PRM's ranking of solutions disagrees with the grader's ranking, the PRM will eventually be optimised into that disagreement.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Four steps, only the last is wrong, outcome reward is 0. Under a potential-based shaper with V = [0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.1] and γ = 1, what reward does each step receive before the terminal outcome?

Reveal answer

Shaped reward for step t is γV(st+1) − V(st): step 0 gets 0.8 − 0.9 = −0.1; step 1 gets0.7 − 0.8 = −0.1; step 2 gets 0.1 − 0.7 = −0.6; step 3 gets V(terminal) − 0.1. The collapse is localised at thestep where the potential drops — that is the entire point of potential-based credit assignment.

Q2. Why is potential-based shaping provably policy-invariant while a per-step bonus is not?

Reveal answer

Because the shaped returns telescope: Σt (γV(st+1) − V(st)) = −V(s0) for a fullepisode, which is constant with respect to the policy, so the ranking of policies is unchanged. An arbitrary bonus does nottelescope, so a policy can increase its return by generating more bonused steps, regardless of the answer.

Q3. Quantitative: outcome reward gives advantage ±1 on a trace of 40 scored steps. What is the per-step advantage magnitude, and what does a PRM with per-step accuracy 0.85 change?

Reveal answer

If the same ±1 advantage is spread over 40 steps uniformly, each step sees a credit of about ±0.025 — but thevariance is what matters, and it is now 40 correlated noisy terms instead of one. The PRM replaces that with 40 independentestimates at 85% accuracy: the signal per step is better, but it is wrong 15% of the time on exactly the steps thepolicy will learn from, and those errors are systematic (confident-sounding wrong steps), so they are optimised against.

Q4. Diagnostic: best-of-n reranked by a PRM does worse than best-of-n reranked by the outcome grader. What is the first thing you check?

Reveal answer

Whether the PRM is scoring a different quantity: granularity mismatch (steps defined differently), optimistic bias(it likes confident wording), or simply that its step labels came from a different task family. Measure rank correlationbetween PRM score and grader score on the same candidate set; anything below about 0.5 means the PRM is not a proxyfor the thing you care about.

Q5. When is outcome reward the correct choice despite the wasted prefixes?

Reveal answer

When the outcome grader is exact and cheap and the step-level judgement is not: a coding task with unit tests has aperfect outcome signal, and a learned process reward adds an attack surface without adding verifiable information. Thegeneral rule is that process reward pays when the outcome signal is either sparse per unit cost (long agent trajectories)or noisy (open-ended quality).
