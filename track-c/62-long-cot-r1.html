L62 · Long CoT and R1-Zero: RL that grows reasoning

Before RL, how long a model thinks is copied from its demonstrations. R1-Zero is the demonstration that a verifiable reward plus a group baseline can teach a model to spend more time — and to waste it. This page runs that mechanism on a toy verifier and instruments the thing most reports leave out.

1 · Why it exists #

Before RL, reasoning length is whatever your SFT data demonstrated. If your demonstrations are 500 tokens, the model reasons for about 500 tokens — including on problems that need 5,000. The failure is not that the model cannot reason further; it is that nobody ever trained it to decide how long to think. Length is an imitation artefact, copied from the dataset, not a response to the problem.

DeepSeek-R1-Zero (arXiv:2501.12948) is the experiment that shows this is trainable. Start from a base model, apply RL with verifiable rewards and no SFT warm-up, and group-relative advantage (GRPO, arXiv:2402.03300). The published trajectory shows reasoning length and accuracy rising together over training, and behaviours appearing that were never demonstrated — re-checking, restating a constraint before proceeding. Nothing taught the model to write longer; the reward only cared about correctness.

Under a verifiable reward and a group baseline, thinking longer is an action the optimiser can discover. Length becomes a learned policy conditioned on difficulty rather than a property copied from demonstrations. That is the whole claim — and the reason the follow-up work had to solve the problems it created: unreadable traces, mixed languages, and length that grows for reasons other than difficulty.

state as of 2026-10 — current frontier practice is not R1-Zero verbatim. A cold-start SFT stage, a reasoning-RL stage with verifiable rewards, and rejection-sampling distillation are the published shape; and the current generation of models exposes an explicit thinking budget rather than letting length grow without bound (lesson 69).

2 · The mental model #

The mental model: the policy is choosing how many thoughts to have and what to have them about, in the same action space as the answer tokens. With a group baseline, a longer attempt that succeeds gets positive advantage on every one of its tokens, including the "let me reconsider" token that was uniquely available in the longer trace. Anything that plausibly precedes success gets reinforced, and "reconsider" often does precede success.

What "length is learnable" gets right

It predicts the honest R1-Zero observation: no length curriculum, no length reward, length rises anyway. It also predicts which prompts drive it — the ones where the group has variance, i.e. solvable-but-not-yet-solved.

Where it breaks

Unconstrained, the same mechanism rewards length that is not thinking: padding, repetition, restating the question, and switching languages mid-trace. Length growth and usefulness are correlated only while the verifier is the binding constraint. The moment the reward saturates, length keeps growing for its own sake.

3 · The mechanism #

Three quantities get tracked together in this literature, and the interesting one is the third:

accuracy (pass@1)  ↑    mean reasoning length  ↑    accuracy per 1k reasoning tokens  ↑? If the third quantity is flat or falling while the first two rise, part of the "reasoning gain" is length, not thinking. Always plot it.

The lab below does what the papers cannot do in a paragraph: it runs a small RL loop with a deterministic verifier, no length term, and measures whether length rises. The environment has three behaviours:

  • SHORT — jumps straight to the answer, ~40 tokens, 40% correct;
  • CHECK — one verification pass, ~140 tokens, 70% correct;
  • LONG — several passes, ~400 tokens, 78% correct.

No reward mentions length. Watch which direction the policy drifts, how quickly, and whether the third printed quantity keeps up.

# L62: the policy RL settles on depends on WHICH items still carry a variance signal. # Zero-variance groups give no gradient, so prompts the behaviour always gets right # (or always wrong) are invisible to the optimiser. Pure stdlib. import math, random G, GROUPS, STEPS, LR = 4, 60, 40, 0.30 NAMES = ["short", "check", "long"] TOKENS = [40, 140, 400] BASE = [0.25, 0.50, 0.55] # correctness on a medium item HARD = {"easy": 1.00, "medium": 0.22, "hard": 0.04} MIX = {"easy": 0.5, "medium": 0.3, "hard": 0.2} def acc_of(b, d): return BASE[b] ** (HARD[d] / 0.30) def softmax(z): m = max(z) e = [math.exp(v - m) for v in z] s = sum(e) return [v / s for v in e] rng = random.Random(7) logits = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0] print(f"{'step':>5}{'P(short)':>10}{'P(check)':>10}{'P(long)':>9}" f"{'E[tokens]':>11}{'hard acc':>10}") for step in range(STEPS + 1): grad = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0] for _ in range(GROUPS): d = rng.choices(list(MIX), weights=list(MIX.values()))[0] p = softmax(logits) samples = rng.choices(range(3), weights=p, k=G) rewards = [1.0 if rng.random() < acc_of(b, d) else 0.0 for b in samples] mu = sum(rewards) / G sd = (sum((r - mu) ** 2 for r in rewards) / G) ** 0.5 if sd < 1e-8: continue # zero-variance group: contributes nothing for b, r in zip(samples, rewards): a = (r - mu) / sd for j in range(3): grad[j] -= a * ((1.0 if j == b else 0.0) - p[j]) / G logits = [z + LR * g for z, g in zip(logits, grad)] if step % 8 == 0: p = softmax(logits) et = sum(pi * t for pi, t in zip(p, TOKENS)) hard_acc = sum(pi * acc_of(b, "hard") for b, pi in enumerate(p)) print(f"{step:>5}{p[0]:>10.3f}{p[1]:>10.3f}{p[2]:>9.3f}{et:>11.1f}{hard_acc:>10.3f}") print("\nper-behaviour accuracy by difficulty, and the final policy:") p = softmax(logits) for b in range(3): row = " ".join(f"{d}:{acc_of(b, d):.2f}" for d in MIX) print(f" {NAMES[b]:<6} {row} final P={p[b]:.3f}")

4 · Your hands #

Three labs: run the R1-Zero mechanism, watch length grow past the point of usefulness when the verifier saturates, and reproduce the "efficiency per token" metric that separates real reasoning gains from verbosity.

Experiment 1 — no length reward anywhere, and the policy still moves

Three behaviours, a 0/1 verifier, group-relative advantage, forty gradient updates. Nothing in the reward mentions length. The script prints the policy, the expected token cost, and the hard-item accuracy.

Experiment 2 — find where length can actually grow

Change two constants so that 'short' is nearly useless and easy prompts are rare. Re-run.

# in the snippet above BASE = [0.05, 0.50, 0.55] MIX = {"easy": 0.1, "medium": 0.3, "hard": 0.6}

With no prompt the short path can get right, the only variance left lives on items where extra passes pay, and the policy moves toward 'check'. That is the R1-Zero regime: a correctness-only reward plus a prompt mix concentrated in the band where the extra steps earn their tokens. Falsifier: if the policy still collapses to short, your prompt mix is not doing what you think.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Length inflation without gain. Symptom: mean tokens up 2-3x, accuracy flat or up 1 point. Check accuracy per 1k reasoning tokens; if it falls, you trained verbosity.
  • Verifier saturation. Symptom: proxy reward plateaus while length keeps growing and zero-variance group fraction climbs (lesson 56). The reward has run out of resolution and length is now the only free variable.
  • Unreadable traces. Symptom: language mixing or repetition in the chain. This was a well-known R1-Zero artefact and the reason published pipelines add a format rule to the reward.
  • Budget truncation at inference. Symptom: pass@1 drops on the longest items only, because the trained policy is happy to think for more tokens than the serving budget allows. Train and serve must share a token budget, or the policy must be trained to respect one.
  • Distillation of the inflation. Symptom: a smaller student trained on the long traces learns length but not the checks, because long traces are truncated or summarised when they hit the token limit.
  • Reward-shape leakage. Symptom: the model writes the answer format early and reasons after it, because the format-check component of the reward was satisfied first. Component order in a shaped reward is a curriculum.

Report four curves from any long-CoT RL run: accuracy, mean reasoning length, accuracy per 1k reasoning tokens, and the zero-variance group fraction. The third curve is the one that tells you whether the run learned to think or learned to type. A run that only reports the first two is the single most common way this literature gets misread.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Why does length grow in R1-Zero-style training even though no reward term mentions length?

Reveal answer

Because longer traces that succeed receive positive advantage on more tokens, and the tokens unique to the longer trace(rechecking, restating) are often the ones that made the success possible. The optimiser is not rewarded for length; it isrewarded for the behaviours that length makes room for, and length rises as a side effect of those behaviours beingreinforced. Once the verifier saturates, length becomes the only unconstrained variable and keeps rising without gain.

Q2. Quantitative: run A is 71% at 1,900 tokens; run B is 68% at 900 tokens. Same model, same eval, same grader. Which result would you ship and what single curve decides it?

Reveal answer

B: 0.68/1.9 = 0.374 accuracy per 1k tokens versus A's 0.237 — but the curve that decides is accuracy per 1k tokens asa function of difficulty band. If B collapses on the hard band and A does not, they are not substitutes; the answer becomesrouting. Neither should be shipped on the headline number alone.

Q3. Your run shows mean length rising 25% over training with accuracy +1 point on a 300-item eval. Is that a gain?

Reveal answer

No. The eval's standard error at a 50% ceiling with 300 items is about √(0.25/300) ≈ 2.9 points, so a 1-pointgain is well inside one SE. The 25% token increase is real and costly; the accuracy gain is indistinguishable from noise.This is the case where the correct action is to stop the run and re-examine the verifier, not to declare victory.

Q4. Diagnostic: the model starts producing correct answers, then continues reasoning for another 800 tokens. Name the mechanism and the two reward-design fixes.

Reveal answer

Mechanism: the reward is shaped with a format/exactness component that is satisfied as soon as the answer string appears,while the length penalty or stop condition is either absent or weaker; the policy exploits the order of reward components.Fixes: (1) require the final answer to be the terminal content (an explicit end marker with a strict parse), and (2) add adecisive stop condition to the reward (extra content after the answer invalidates the reward), rather than a fraction of alength penalty.

Q5. Which part of the R1-Zero result is reproducible with a tiny model and which is not?

Reveal answer

Reproducible: that length and accuracy can rise together under a verifiable reward and a group baseline with no lengthcurriculum, and that zero-variance groups dominate as the model saturates. Not reproducible: the absolute capability gain.
