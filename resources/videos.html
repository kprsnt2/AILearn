Videos & Courses — 25 things to watch with a keyboard open

Video is the only medium that shows you the debugging — the wrong shape, the loss that spikes, the fix. It is also the easiest medium to consume passively for six hours and retain nothing. Every entry here names the AILearn lesson it feeds, so you watch it in the week it is relevant and not before.

25 entries 5 groups channels & course pages only links verified 2026-10
from scratch paper walkthrough GPU systems serving short courses prerequisite-aware

Link policy. No guessed video IDs. Every entry links a channel, a playlist index, a course page, or a repository — the stable artifact — and names the series inside it. Every link here returned HTTP 200 at build time (Oct 2026). The “commitment” figure is the honest total, not the runtime of one episode: you will pause, type, and re-run.

1 · Foundations #

The mathematically honest versions — gradients, backprop, attention — taught by people who can derive them on camera. Watch these once, properly, early; everything later is a variation. Six entries.

3Blue1Brown — Neural Network series

~2 h series, 2–3 eveningsfeeds L03 · L04

What you get: the visual intuition for what a gradient is — the four-episode neural-network series builds backprop as a chain of local nudges with animations you cannot reproduce in text. The later attention episodes are the best available pre-reading for attention before you see the matrix form.

Prerequisite: none. Watch before L03, then re-watch the backprop episode after you have written your own autodiff — the second viewing is the one that lands.

MIT 6.S191 — Introduction to Deep Learning

~1 weekend of lectures + labsfeeds Track A broadly

What you get: a compact, annually refreshed tour of the whole field with runnable labs — the fastest legitimate way to fill gaps in depth without a semester. The lecture on sequence modelling is a good first pass at attention before L06.

Prerequisite: you can already write Python; that is all it assumes.

Stanford CS224N — NLP with Deep Learning · YouTube

~20 h lectures + problem setsfeeds L01 · L06 · L08

What you get: the academic-grade treatment of tokenization, embeddings, self-attention, and transformer architecture, with assignments that force you to implement them. Use the lectures selectively — the ones on attention and subword tokenization — rather than watching the whole course.

Prerequisite: linear algebra and PyTorch. Watch the attention lecture immediately before L06; skip the sections on legacy recurrent models unless you need them for a codebase you maintain.

Stanford CS231N — Convolutional Neural Networks

~15 h selectivefeeds L47

What you get: backprop and optimisation taught with the discipline of having to implement them, plus the visual-model foundations you need to reason about how an image actually becomes tokens in a multimodal model. Watch the backprop/optimisation lectures even if you never touch a conv net.

Prerequisite: L03. Read the patchification material in L47 straight after.

Stanford CS229 — Machine Learning

~15 h selective (or full course)feeds L04 · L13

What you get: the derivations behind SGD, regularisation, generalisation, and the probabilistic view of losses — the material that makes “why does weight decay work” a sentence you can answer rather than repeat.

Prerequisite: the most mathematical entry here; bring real linear algebra. Watch the optimisation and regularisation lectures before L13.

Dive into Deep Learning (d2l.ai)

book + notebooks, use as referencefeeds Track A, Track B

What you get: a free interactive book where every concept has an executable notebook — the closest thing to a lab manual for this course. Its attention and transformer chapters map almost one to one onto Track A, and its optimisation chapter is a useful second opinion on L04.

Prerequisite: none, but do not read it front to back; open the chapter that matches the lesson you are on. Use it as the reference you reach for when a lesson’s math needs a second presentation.

2 · Build-it-yourself #

Code typed on camera, from an empty file. The only genre of video that changes what you can do, because you can pause it, write the line yourself, and get the same error. Six entries.

Karpathy — Neural Networks: Zero to Hero · YouTube

~12 h video, 30–40 h to actually keep upfeeds L03 → L19

What you get: the single most efficient path from “I can use PyTorch” to “I understand what PyTorch is doing”. Builds micrograd (a scalar autograd engine), then makemore, then a character-level GPT — the same path Track A takes, with the debugging visible.

Prerequisite: Python fluency. This is the recommended spine for L01 through L19; do not watch episode N+1 until the code from episode N runs and you can explain each line.

“Let’s build GPT: from scratch, in code” — Karpathy

~2 h video, ~6 h to reimplementfeeds L19

What you get: a decoder-only transformer built and trained end to end in one sitting — tokenizer, attention, residual stream, the training loop, then sampling. The companion repo is github.com/karpathy/ng-video-lecture.

Prerequisite: finish the zero-to-hero episodes on micrograd and makemore first. Watch it before L19 and treat L19’s build as your own reimplementation, not a copy.

nanoGPT · build-nanogpt

~4 h video series + 1 week of reading the repofeeds L24 · L25

What you get: the reference minimal GPT training codebase, plus a video series that builds it while reimplementing the GPT-2 paper’s claims — a worked example of exactly the “reproduce the paper” discipline this course grades. It is the codebase to have read before you read any production training stack.

Prerequisite: L19. Annotate every file and produce one diagram of the data flow for L24 — that annotation is the deliverable, not the watching.

llm.c

~1 week, optionalfeeds L21 · L39

What you get: GPT-2 training in plain C/CUDA with no framework — the clearest demonstration that the machine in L21 is all there is. Reading the forward and backward passes here makes every framework abstraction feel thin.

Prerequisite: comfortable with C and pointers. Do this after nanoGPT, never before, or you will learn C and transformers simultaneously and learn neither.

minGPT · micrograd

2–3 eveningsfeeds L03

What you get: the two smallest readable artifacts in the field — a ~100-line autograd engine and a ~300-line GPT. Implement micrograd yourself from the video, then diff against the repo; the diff is your lesson plan.

Prerequisite: none. This is the correct first day of the course.

LLMs from Scratch — book code & notebooks

~20 h, chapter-parallel to Track Afeeds L11 · L17

What you get: a chapter-by-chapter implementation path with a book attached — attention, normalisation, activation (GELU/SwiGLU), the training loop, then fine-tuning and sampling. It complements Karpathy’s videos by being written rather than spoken, which is better for going back to check a detail.

Prerequisite: PyTorch basics. Use it as the second source for any Track A lesson where the math did not land the first time.

3 · Paper walkthroughs #

A person reading the actual paper, out loud, and stopping where the notation is bad. Use these when a paper’s central section resists you after two attempts — not as a substitute for attempt one. Four entries.

Yannic Kilcher — paper readings

60–90 min per paperfeeds every entry on the papers page

What you get: the longest-running and most critical paper-reading channel — he reads the figures, questions the experiments, and names the ablations that are missing. His readings of the foundational transformer and scaling papers are a functional replacement for a reading group, and he is reliably willing to say a famous paper is not convincing.

Prerequisite: read the abstract and figures yourself first. Watch with the PDF open and pause to verify a claim — the value is in the disagreement, not the summary.

Stanford MLSys Seminar

~1 h per talk, 2–3 per monthfeeds L44 · L42 · L43

What you get: the speakers are the people who built the systems — serving stacks, distributed training, kernels, agent infrastructure — and the format is technical with audience questions included. This is the closest available approximation of sitting in the room where the cost models in Track B were argued.

Prerequisite: Track B through about L44. The seminar assumes you know what a KV cache is and will not define it.

Machine Learning Street Talk

2–3 h per episode, selectivefeeds L48 · L49

What you get: long adversarial interviews with researchers, often about interpretability and the limits of current methods. It is the one long-form channel that reliably pushes back on a guest’s claims — useful training for how you should be questioning your own agent results.

Prerequisite: none technically, but it only pays off once you have opinions to defend. Watch the interpretability and RL episodes after L49.

Two Minute Papers

~5 min per video, background onlyfeeds discovery, no specific lesson

What you get: fast, enthusiastic summaries of recent papers — genuinely useful as a discovery layer for which papers exist, and explicitly not as an explanation of any of them. Treat every claim as a pointer to a PDF, never as a finding.

Prerequisite: none. Cap it at a few minutes a week; this is the entry on the page most likely to turn into passive consumption.

4 · Systems — GPU, kernels, serving #

The layer where the money and the latency are. These are performance-engineering lectures, not ML lectures — they assume you have already accepted that the bottleneck is memory traffic. Four entries.

GPU MODE — lecture series

~1–2 h per lecture, 8–10 lecturesfeeds L21 · L38 · L39

What you get: the community’s kernel-engineering curriculum — CUDA memory hierarchy, tiling, profiling, then attention kernels — with assignments and recorded sessions from practitioners at the labs that write these kernels. This is the highest-value video material on this page for the systems half of the course.

Prerequisite: L21, and access to a GPU. Do not start before you can state the bytes of a KV cache for a given model on paper.

Triton — tutorials

~4 h to do the fused-attention tutorial properlyfeeds L39 · L38

What you get: the tutorial sequence from vector add to fused softmax to flash attention, in Python-level syntax — the shortest route to writing a real kernel yourself. The step where you fuse softmax and see the memory traffic drop is the actual lesson of L38.

Prerequisite: L38 read first. If you only do one item from this page’s systems group, do this one.

vLLM — blog & design posts · docs

~3 h reading + 1 deployment experimentfeeds L44 · L45

What you get: the design rationale for paged attention, continuous batching, and prefix caching, written by the team that shipped them — plus documentation authoritative enough to size a deployment from. Read a design post, then reproduce the throughput claim on your own machine.

Prerequisite: L09 and L44. If you can explain why prefill and decode have different bottlenecks, you are ready.

PyTorch — channel (tutorials & conference talks)

selective, 1–2 hfeeds L42 · L20

What you get: the authoritative walkthroughs for the framework you will actually use — FSDP/distributed sharding, compile, mixed precision, and the performance-tuning talks from their conferences. When the docs are ambiguous about a numerics or sharding behaviour, the talk usually is not.

Prerequisite: L20. Search the channel for the distributed-training and compile sessions specifically.

5 · Agent & course material #

Structured courses for the parts of practice that are better taught than discovered — evaluation, orchestration, retrieval. Short courses are the highest-density format here, precisely because they are forced to be concrete. Five entries.

DeepLearning.AI — short courses

1–2 h each, pick threefeeds L77 · L89

What you get: short, tool-specific courses built with the vendors — agent patterns, tool calling, evaluation, RAG, and judging. The value is the working notebook at the end, not the lecture. Be aware they are vendor co-branded, so they teach one implementation of each pattern; cross-check against the pattern papers on the papers page.

Prerequisite: after L76. Prioritise the evaluation and orchestration courses over the “intro to prompting” ones — you are past that layer.

Hugging Face — course hub

~8 h for the NLP course; smol-course ~4 hfeeds L52 · L55

What you get: the practical transformers curriculum plus the smol-course — their hands-on post-training material covering instruction tuning and preference tuning end to end. This is the fastest legitimate route from “I have read about DPO” to “I have trained with it”.

Prerequisite: L24 for the transformers library. Do the smol-course in the same week as L55, and log the loss curves — that log is your artifact.

smol-course — repository

~4–6 h + computefeeds L51 · L66

What you get: the notebooks behind the course, organised by post-training stage — instruction tuning, preference alignment, evaluation. Because it is a repo you can fork it, break it, and measure what breaks, which is the whole point.

Prerequisite: L52. Start with the instruction-tuning notebook and compare its masking decisions against the spec you wrote in L52.

Machine Learning Systems — open book

reference, ~6 h targeted readingfeeds L44 · L43

What you get: a structured treatment of the systems stack — data engineering, model compression, hardware, serving — that matches Track B’s framing almost exactly. Use its chapters as the third opinion when a Track B lesson’s cost model needs checking.

Prerequisite: none, but read it alongside L43 and L44, not before.

How to Scale Your Model — JAX/TPU scaling book

~8 h, the best single resource on parallelismfeeds L42 · L43

What you get: the arithmetic of distributed training laid out properly — rooflines, communication cost, sharding, and how to choose a parallel configuration for a given job. It targets JAX/TPU but the math is hardware-agnostic, and it is the reference behind L42’s config-selection exercise.

Prerequisite: L43’s FLOPs and MFU material. If you cannot compute MFU by hand, read that lesson first.

6 · How to consume this #

This block is identical on all three resource pages, deliberately. The cadence is the same whether you are reading a paper, a blog post, or watching a talk, because the failure is the same — and it is worse in video, because video is the most comfortable form of not working.

The 30-minutes-a-day cadence

00–05 — orient. For video: read the description and the chapter list, then write one sentence on the claim. For a paper or post: abstract, figures, last paragraph.

05–25 — the load-bearing part. One section, one derivation, one build step. Never a whole course in one sitting. Pause at every code line and type it yourself.

25–30 — the tag. One line in a log: claim · evidence · what I would have to build to check it. A talk you cannot summarise in one sentence was background music.

The skim-versus-read rule

Read deeply only if you will implement something from it within two weeks. That is the whole rule, and it applies harder to video: watch at 1x only when you are typing along.

About to write a kernel → watch the GPU-MODE and Triton material slowly, with a GPU open. About to pick a serving stack → read the design posts, skip the conference talk. About to “learn about agents generally” → you are already past it; go build the harness.

If the two-week window is imaginary, it is a skim. Mark it as one and do not feel bad about 2x speed.

Reading without a build is entertainment. Watching without a build is worse, because it feels more like learning. The only defence is a hard gate: a source earns a place in your log when a number in a repo you own moved because of it — a latency, a memory line, a loss curve, a pass rate. If nothing moved, you were watching.
