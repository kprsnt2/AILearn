RESOURCES · PAPER CANON

The Paper Canon — 60 papers, graded

You cannot audit a system you cannot cite. This is the set of primary sources the other 100 lessons are built on, each with a verdict, a difficulty, and — where the field has since changed its mind — a 2026 note. Reading without building is entertainment; the 6-week order at the bottom is the only part of this page that matters operationally.

scaling laws MoE routing KV compression GRPO DPO agent evals retrieval reasoning traces

Link policy on this page. Every arXiv identifier here returned HTTP 200 from arxiv.org at build time (Oct 2026). Where a canonical artifact is a weights release or a GitHub repo rather than a paper, the link points at the repo — a paper index page that lies about a model is worse than no link. Verdicts are editorial: READ means read it end to end once in your life, SKIM means figures, tables, and the abstract, SKIP-UNLESS means read only when a specific lesson sends you there. SUPERSEDED marks a paper whose central claim did not survive contact with later work — read it to understand the mistake, not to use the number.

1 · The 10 you must actually read #

If you read only this section of the whole course, you would still be ahead of most people shipping agents in 2026. These ten are ordered by dependency, not by fame. Each carries the instruction that matters more than the abstract: where inside the paper the load-bearing claim lives.

Attention Is All You Need

Vaswani et al. · Google Brain / Google Research · 2017
●●●○○○○~90 minREAD

Taught the field that attention alone, without recurrence, is enough to model sequences — and that the whole thing is one matmul-shaped pipeline that a GPU can saturate. Every later efficiency paper is a footnote arguing about which of its three matmuls to skip.

Read §3.2.1 (scaled dot-product) line by line, then §3.5 (positional encoding), then Table 3's row for head=8, d_k=d_v=d_model/64. Skip §5.3 unless you train on translation data. Skim §4 (training) — the schedule is obsolete but the warmup idea is not.

GPT-3: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners

Brown et al. · OpenAI · 2020
●●○○○○○○~60 minREAD

Taught the field that capability bought with scale can be spent through the prompt instead of through gradients. It is also the origin of the eval hygiene problem: the paper is careful about train/test contamination in ways most later papers stopped being.

Read §2.1 (“In-context learning”) and §3 (the 42 tasks) — specifically the contamination discussion in §3.x. Then figure 1.1's few-shot curve: performance is flat until it isn't. That knee is what the entire next five years of scaling work tried to explain.

Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

Kaplan et al. · OpenAI · 2020
●●●○○○○~60 minSUPERSEDED

Taught the field that loss is a power law in params, data, and compute — so you can size a run before you run it. Also the source of the most expensive correction in the field's history (see Chinchilla below).

Read §3 (power-law form) and figure 1. Then read only the appendix on fitting procedure — the fitting discipline is still correct; the exponent for data is not.
2026 note: the practical recipe here — “scale parameters fastest, data second” — was overturned by Chinchilla for compute-optimal pretraining. But the newer regime has quietly re-validated parts of Kaplan from the other side: if you are willing to pay more training compute to get a cheaper model forever, over-training small models on far too many tokens is now standard, so the exponents still matter, just with a different objective (cost per query, not loss per FLOP).

Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla)

Hoffmann et al. · DeepMind · 2022
●●●○○○○~70 minREAD

Taught the field that almost everyone had been training models that were too large on too little data, and that tokens and parameters should scale together. It converted “bigger” into a solvable optimisation problem with a budget line.

Read the abstract, then §3's three independent estimation methods (fixed-model, IsoFLOP, parametric) — the fact that three different methods agree is the actual contribution. Then the table that shows a smaller model trained longer beating a larger one at the same compute.
2026 note: the “~20 tokens per parameter” rule of thumb was a compute-optimal snapshot, not a law. Inference-aware economics pushed production models far past it, and the 2026 open-model generations routinely train on orders of magnitude more tokens per parameter than Chinchilla would prescribe. Quote the rule with its assumptions or not at all.

Switch Transformers

Fedus, Zoph, Shazeer · Google Brain · 2021
●●●○○○○~50 minREAD

Taught the field that you can decouple parameter count from FLOPs per token, and then taught it the tax: routing instability, load imbalance, and the auxiliary loss you bolt on to make experts share work.

Read §2.1 (the routing equation) then the auxiliary-loss definition, then the stability tricks (§2.5: selective precision, smaller init, dropout on the router). Those three tricks are the difference between a MoE that trains and one that collapses to one expert.

DeepSeek-V2 (Multi-head Latent Attention)

DeepSeek-AI · 2024
●●●●○○~80 minREAD

Taught the field that the KV cache — not the weights — is the binding constraint on long context, and that you can cache a low-rank latent instead of per-head keys and values. This is the paper that made “how many bytes per token in cache” a first-class engineering question.

Read §2.1's MLA formulation and the cache-size table next to GQA and MQA. Skip the pretraining data section entirely. Then go do the memory arithmetic in L33 — the paper's argument only lands when you compute it yourself.

DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report

DeepSeek-AI · 2024
●●●●○○~2 hREAD

Taught the field that a frontier-class model could be trained for a number worth publishing, and that the training-time savings live in specific mechanisms: auxiliary-loss-free load balancing, multi-token prediction, and FP8 mixed precision. It is the most reproducible systems paper of the decade.

Read §2.1.1 (auxiliary-loss-free balancing with the bias term) and §2.2 (MTP) first — those two sections are the transferable ideas. Then the FP8 training section, then the cost table in §3. The pretraining-curve appendix is worth ten minutes for the shape alone.
2026 note: V3's auxiliary-loss-free balancing (bias offsets instead of an aux loss) has become the default for new sparse models; papers that still describe a classic Switch-style aux loss are describing a paper-generation-older design. Also: V3's architecture has since been succeeded twice inside the same lab (V3.2's sparse attention, then the V4 family) — read V3 as the training recipe, not as the current architecture.

DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning via RL

DeepSeek-AI · 2025
●●●○○○○~90 minREAD

Taught the field that long reasoning chains can be grown by RL against a purely verifiable reward — the R1-Zero result — and that the resulting model needs a second, SFT-shaped pass to be usable. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing.

Read §2.2 (R1-Zero, GRPO with rule-based rewards), then §2.3.1's cold-start data and the language-mixing failure it fixes, then §4 on distillation into smaller dense models. Note that distillation beat small-model RL from scratch — that is an operational lesson, not a footnote.
2026 note: the pure-RL-from-base-model path (R1-Zero) is a research landmark but not the production path; every practical pipeline in this course follows the R1 shape — cold-start SFT, RL against verifiable rewards, then a final alignment pass. See L62 and L66.

ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting

Yao et al. · Princeton / Google · 2022
●●○○○○○○~40 minREAD

Taught the field the actual shape of an agent: interleaved thought — action — observation, where the observation is the only ground truth. Everything you have built as a loop-and-budget harness is a ReAct loop with better instrumentation.

Read §2 (the ReAct formulation) and the HotpotQA / Fever sections. Then read the failure analysis — specifically the reasoning loops and the hallucinated observations. That is the page that predicts your production incidents.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP

Lewis et al. · Facebook AI Research · 2020
●●●○○○○~50 minREAD

Taught the field that a parametric model plus a non-parametric index is a different object than a bigger model — updateable, attributable, and auditable. The attribution part is why your moat is retrieval, not fine-tuning.

Read §2 (RAG-Sequence vs RAG-Token) and the passage-retrieval setup. Then read §5's ablation on retrieval quality, because it is the first honest statement that a RAG system is a retrieval system with a generator attached.

Ten papers, one afternoon each, and you can walk into any architecture review in 2026 and say which knob each of the last five years of optimisations turned.

2 · Track A — Foundations #

What these teach, collectively: the transformer is a stack of linear maps with one pairwise-interaction op, and every stability and speed property of the thing comes from the handful of non-matmul choices around it — the normaliser, the initialiser, the positional scheme, the optimiser.

ULMFiT: Universal Language Model Fine-tuning

Howard & Ruder · fast.ai / DeepMind · 2018
●●○○○○○○~35 minSKIM

Taught the field that pretrain-then-fine-tune beats task-specific training even on small data, and gave it two tricks still in every fine-tuning script: discriminative learning rates and gradual unfreezing.

Read §3.2 (slanted triangular LR, discriminative LRs). Ignore the ULMFiT architecture — nothing here is about the LSTM.

BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers

Devlin et al. · Google AI · 2018
●●○○○○○○~40 minSKIM

Taught the field that masked-token prediction could build representations good enough to retarget at any classification task — the decade's first mass-adoption pre-training recipe, and the origin of the encoder half of every encoder-decoder system still shipped.

Read §3.1 (masked LM, and the 80/10/10 corruption rule specifically) and the fine-tuning table. Skip the GLUE-era leaderboard chasing.
2026 note: BERT-style encoder-only models are the right answer for classification and embedding/retrieval work at small scale, and the wrong answer for generation. The 2026 encoder-decoder comeback (asymmetric prefill/decode splits) is a different argument, not a BERT revival.

T5: Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning

Raffel et al. · Google Research · 2019
●●○○○○○○~50 minSKIM

Taught the field text-to-text framing, the C4 cleaning pipeline that most modern corpora are descended from, and a discipline: run the ablation matrix before you write the ablation paragraph.

Read §3.1 (C4 construction, and the sentences-with-curse-words filter that becomes a fairness problem) and the ablation in §3.7. Everything else is a 2020 leaderboard.

Layer Normalization

Ba, Kiros, Hinton · Toronto / Google Brain · 2016
●●○○○○○○~25 minSKIM

Taught the field that normalising across features instead of across the batch removes the dependence on batch size — which is the only reason sequence models could be batched by token instead of by example.

Read §C's proof sketch of the invariance properties and the section on why the mean/variance statistics cannot be over-fitted. Then read what RMSNorm keeps from it in L05.

Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization

Kingma & Ba · Amsterdam / Toronto · 2014
●●○○○○○○~30 minSKIM

Taught the field that per-parameter adaptive moments remove most of the learning-rate intuiting — and that a bad epsilon choice can blow up an entire run, which is exactly the failure you will see in a bf16 training log at 3 a.m.

Read §2's update rules and the bias-correction paragraph. Read §3 for the worse-than-SGD convergence counterexample — that is the honest part.

Mixed Precision Training

Micikevicius et al. · NVIDIA / Baidu · 2017
●●●○○○○~35 minSKIM

Taught the field why half-precision training silently produces nothing without FP32 master weights, loss scaling, and a few ops pinned to FP32. Every NaN you will ever debug in a mixed-precision run is one of those three.

Read the three techniques in isolation (§3.1–3.3), then the section on which ops stay FP32 and why. Pair with L21.

Mixout: Effective Regularization to Finetune Large-scale Pretrained Language Models

Lee et al. · NAVER · 2019
●●●○○○○~30 minSKIP-UNLESS

Taught the field that fine-tuning degrades because you are leaving the pretrained basin, and that stochastically anchoring to the pretrained weights (not the initial ones) is what keeps you in it. It is the ancestor of the low-rank preference finetuning family that arrives two years later.

Read §3.1 (the mixout operator) and the comparison against dropout. Only worth it if you are actually fine-tuning on small data.

The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration

Holtzman et al. · UW / Allen AI · 2019
●●○○○○○○~30 minREAD

Taught the field that maximum-likelihood training + beam search produces degenerate text, and that nucleus (top-p) sampling is the fix. It is also the clearest available explanation of why “just take the most likely token” is a bad operating point for any system whose output you will read.

Read §3 and figure 2 (probability-mass shape), then §4 (top-p). Two pages tell you more about sampler choice than any 2026 blog post.

Pixel Recurrent Neural Networks

van den Oord et al. · DeepMind · 2016
●●●●○○~40 minSKIP-UNLESS

Taught the field autoregressive factorisation applied to tensors rather than tokens, including masked convolutions — the idea that later reappears in every image and audio generator you will use. Read it if you touch multimodal generation; otherwise it is history.

Read the masked-convolution figure in §3.2. Nothing else is required for this course.

3 · Track B — Scaling & Architecture #

Collectively: the cost of a token is now a design variable. Sparsity buys parameters without FLOPs, latent caches buy context without bytes, and IO-awareness buys speed without either. Every one of these papers is an argument about which resource to spend.

GShard: Scaling Giant Models with Conditional Computation

Lepikhin et al. · Google Research · 2020
●●●●○○~50 minSKIM

Taught the field how to actually run a sparse model across accelerators: top-2 gating, capacity factor, auxiliary loss on expert load, and the sharding annotations that make it trainable. It is the engineering ancestor of every production MoE.

Read §2.2 (the auxiliary loss and the capacity-factor equation) and the load statistics. Skip the translation results.

GLaM: Efficient Scaling with Mixture-of-Experts

Du et al. · Google Research · 2021
●●○○○○○○~30 minSKIM

Taught the field to price a model by FLOPs per inference and energy, not parameter count — the first paper to make “cost per token” the headline metric instead of a footnote.

Read the energy/FLOPs comparison table and the ablation on the number of experts. The training-recipe chapters are skippable.

DeepSeekMoE: Towards Ultimate Expert Specialization

Dai et al. · DeepSeek-AI · 2023
●●●○○○○~55 minREAD

Taught the field to split experts finer (many small experts, top-k of them) and pin a set of shared experts that always run. It solves two different failures at once: experts that never specialise, and common knowledge that gets redundantly copied into every routed expert.

Read §2's two ideas (fine-grained expert segmentation, shared expert isolation) and the ablation that adds them cumulatively. This is the correct mental model for every DeepSeek model since, including the V4 family in L32.

DeepSeek-V3.2: Pushing the Frontier of Open Large Language Models

DeepSeek-AI · 2025
●●●○○○○~90 minREAD

Taught the field that the capability gap on long-horizon agentic tasks closes by synthesising agent trajectories at scale rather than by scaling the base model: mass data synthesis over environments, plus thinking in tool-use rather than thinking then acting.

Read the data-synthesis section first — how tasks and environment feedback are generated — then the reasoning/tool-use section. The DSA (sparse attention) material lives in the V3.2-Exp release notes: github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp. Pair with L67.
2026 note: this is the paper to read if you are building agents, because it is mostly an agent data paper wearing an architecture paper's costume. It has since been superseded on architecture by the V4 family, but the environment-synthesis method is the part you can actually copy.

DeepSeek-V4: Towards Highly Efficient Million-Token Context Intelligence

DeepSeek-AI · 2026
●●●●●~2 hREAD

Taught the field that a million-token context is a compression problem, not a memory problem: token-wise compression plus sparse attention, with active-parameter counts far below total parameter counts. This is the report the 2026 cost models of L43 and L35 are built on.

Read the architecture section on compression and sparse attention first, then the efficiency/cost tables — specifically the per-token serving cost against the previous generation. Read the pretraining section last.
2026 note: state as of 2026-10 the family has already moved again: V4.1-Flash (Sep 2026) documents a causal encoder–decoder split with asymmetric active parameters for input and output, aimed squarely at the prefill-versus-decode cost asymmetry. Read V4 for the compression argument, V4.1 for the encoder-decoder economics.

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression

DeepSeek-AI · 2026
●●●●○○~60 minREAD

Taught the field that the prefill phase and the decode phase want different architectures. An encoder–decoder split where the encoder side has far fewer active parameters for input and the decoder side has more for output, with KV cache pressure cut by roughly a factor of four in HBM and eight on SSD versus the prior generation state as of 2026-10.

Read the cache-compression accounting first, then the asymmetric active-parameter split. This is the primary source for L37.

RoFormer: Rotary Position Embedding

Su et al. · Zhuiyi / Jianlin Su · 2021
●●●○○○○~35 minREAD

Taught the field to encode position by rotating query and key vectors, so that attention score depends only on relative offset — and, critically, so that extending context is a frequency-scaling problem (you retune the base) rather than a retraining problem.

Read §3.2.2 (the rotation derivation), then the invariance argument. Then implement the rotation yourself in L08; nothing else makes this concrete.

GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models

Ainslie et al. · Google Research · 2023
●●○○○○○○~35 minREAD

Taught the field that you do not have to choose between multi-head quality and multi-query cache size: interpolate, and also uptrain an existing multi-head checkpoint into the cheaper shape for a small fraction of pretraining compute.

Read §3 (the mean-pooling uptraining recipe) and figure 2's cache-vs-quality curve. Then compute the cache sizes by hand for 8 groups in L10.

FlashAttention: Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness

Dao et al. · Stanford · 2022
●●●●○○~60 minREAD

Taught the field that the bottleneck in attention is HBM read/write traffic, not FLOPs, and that tiling plus online softmax gives you exact attention in a fraction of the memory. This is the paper that made “memory bandwidth” a phrase every LLM engineer can define.

Read §3.1 (the tiling loop) and §3.2 (recomputation). Then read the IO-complexity table — that table is the entire argument. Pair with L38.

FlashAttention-2: Faster Attention with Better Parallelism

Tri Dao · 2023
●●●●○○~35 minSKIM

Taught the field where the remaining waste was: non-matmul FLOPs, poor warp partitioning, and early softmax rescaling. A paper about the difference between a correct kernel and a saturated one.

Read the list of specific inefficiencies (§2–3) and the forward-pass pseudocode. Skip the benchmarks unless you are writing kernels.

FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony

Shah et al. · Colfax / NVIDIA / Together · 2024
●●●●○○~40 minSKIP-UNLESS

Taught the field to overlap the tensor core work with the softmax work via warp specialisation and hardware asynchrony, and how to do FP8 attention without losing accuracy. Read it only if you are actually targeting Hopper-class hardware.

Read the warp-specialisation diagram and the FP8 section. Everything else requires the hardware in front of you.

Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces

Gu & Dao · CMU / Princeton · 2023
●●●●○○~70 minSKIM

Taught the field that a recurrence with input-dependent (selective) state transitions can match attention on many sequence tasks with linear scaling — and, more usefully, that its fixed-size state is bad at exact recall, which is why hybrid architectures exist rather than pure replacements.

Read §3.2 (the selection mechanism) and the recall-vs-throughput discussion. The hardware-aware scan implementation is optional for L46.

LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models

Touvron et al. · Meta AI · 2023
●●○○○○○○~45 minSKIM

Taught the field that a model trained far past compute-optimal on public data could beat much larger models — the first widely circulated proof that inference cost, not training cost, sets the useful frontier. It also put an efficient open-weight architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) into everyone's hands.

Read §2's architecture deltas versus the transformer baseline and the over-training argument in the introduction. Skip the evaluation tables.

Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models

Touvron et al. · Meta AI · 2023
●●●○○○○~60 minREAD

Taught the field the full post-training assembly line as an industrial artifact — iterative RLHF with two reward models, and the ghost-attention discussion of reward-model drift — and it is unusually explicit about its safety evaluation and red-teaming. The best-documented RLHF system description available.

Read §3 (fine-tuning: SFT, reward model, iterative RLHF, the rejection-sampling step) and §4.2 (safety). Skim the benchmark tables.

4 · Track C — Post-training & Reasoning #

Collectively: behaviour is manufactured, not discovered. Imitation sets the format, preferences set the disposition, verifiable RL sets the depth. Almost every 2026 production gain sits in the third slot.

InstructGPT: Training Language Models to Follow Instructions

Ouyang et al. · OpenAI · 2022
●●●○○○○~55 minREAD

Taught the field that a small amount of preference data beats a lot of raw capability for perceived usefulness, and formalised the three-stage pipeline the industry still uses. It is also the clearest statement of the alignment tax and of the annotator-disagreement problem.

Read §3 (SFT, RM, PPO, with the KL penalty term) and the human-eval methodology in §4 — the methodology is the part that did not age. The “Show me a bigger model is not automatically better behaved” discussion is worth the read.

Proximal Policy Optimization

Schulman et al. · OpenAI · 2017
●●●●○○~50 minREAD

Taught the field the clipped surrogate objective — a trust region you can implement in nine lines and tune with one number. Every RLHF recipe up to and including the value-model-based families is PPO applied to tokens.

Read §3's clipped objective and the table of hyperparameter cards. Then read the discussion of what the clip actually bounds — the divergence it permits is per-ratio, not per-sequence, which is exactly the gap GRPO exploits later.

DeepSeekMath (introduces GRPO)

Shao et al. · DeepSeek-AI · 2024
●●●○○○○~70 minREAD

Taught the field to drop the value network: sample a group of answers to the same prompt, use the group's own score statistics as the baseline, and normalise the advantage within the group. Fewer models to hold in memory, and no critic to be wrong about credit assignment.

Read §4.1's GRPO objective (the group-normalised advantage) and the comparison against PPO-with-critic. Then implement it on a bandit in L56 — the estimator only becomes obvious when the group size's effect on variance is visible.
2026 note: GRPO is the default family for verifiable-reward training, and the shape in production has shifted toward group-relative methods with rule-based rewards; the value-model-heavy PPO pipeline is now mostly used where a dense, learnable reward model exists (see L64 for where each fails).

DPO: Direct Preference Optimization

Rafailov et al. · Stanford · 2023
●●●○○○○~45 minREAD

Taught the field that the RLHF optimum has a closed form, which means preference alignment collapses to a classification loss on pairs with no sampling loop, no reward model at inference, no critic. It moved alignment from a research lab activity to a script in every trainer’s toolbox.

Read §4's derivation from the KL-constrained objective to the implicit reward — specifically the step where the partition function cancels. Then §5's failure discussion: DPO optimises a proxy, and out-of-distribution preference data is where it drifts.
2026 note: DPO remains the cheap, stable default for style and disposition, but it cannot grow new reasoning depth the way verifiable-reward RL can. The practical 2026 stack uses both, in sequence, not as alternatives. See L55.

LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation

Hu et al. · Microsoft · 2021
●●○○○○○○~35 minREAD

Taught the field that fine-tuning changes the weights by a low-rank delta, so you can train two small matrices and merge them back — and, more importantly, that you can keep many adapters and swap the behaviour at serving time. The economics of fine-tuning collapsed overnight.

Read §4.1's decomposition and the “which layers to adapt” ablation. Then read the merging discussion — it is where serving-time costs hide. Pair with L41 when you decide fine-tune versus distil.

QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs

Dettmers et al. · UW · 2023
●●●○○○○~50 minSKIM

Taught the field that you can backprop into frozen 4-bit weights and still match 16-bit fine-tuning, if you add double quantisation and paged optimiser states to survive memory spikes. It is the recipe that made single-GPU fine-tuning of 30B+ models routine.

Read §3's three contributions (NF4, double quantisation, paging) and the memory arithmetic table. Then watch what a small rank does to quality when the task needs facts, not style.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning

Wei et al. · Google Research · 2022
●●○○○○○○~35 minREAD

Taught the field that intermediate tokens are compute: a model that writes its steps can solve problems it cannot answer in one pass. Every reasoning model, every scratchpad, and every token-budget argument descends from this observation.

Read §2 (the prompting format) and figure 3's scaling curves — the crossover where CoT only helps past a model-size threshold is the paper's real finding. Then read L60 for the part the paper understates: which tasks CoT cannot touch at all.

Self-Consistency Improves Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

Wang et al. · Google Research · 2022
●●○○○○○○~30 minREAD

Taught the field that sampling many reasoning paths and taking a majority vote beats greedy decoding by a large margin on tasks with extractable answers — buying accuracy with test-time compute. The ancestor of every best-of-n and vote-based verifier strategy.

Read §2 and the accuracy-vs-sample-count curves. Then read §4's limits: with no extractable terminal answer, or with correlated errors, majority vote is decoration. Pair with L61.

Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving

Yao et al. · Princeton / DeepMind · 2023
●●●○○○○~35 minSKIM

Taught the field to treat reasoning as search over partial states with an explicit evaluator and backtracking — the right frame when the cost of being wrong is high and you have a cheap way to score partial work.

Read §3 (the thought-generation / state-evaluation / search-loop abstract definition) and the Game of 24 results. Then read the failure case honestly: this only pays off with a reliable evaluator, and hallucinated evaluators turn search into confident wandering.

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback

Bai et al. · Anthropic · 2022
●●●○○○○~45 minREAD

Taught the field that you can replace a large part of human labelling with a model critiquing itself against a written list of principles, then train on the preference data that falls out. It is the origin of rubric-based judging, and of the audit question this course keeps asking: who wrote the rubric, and how do you verify the judge applied it?

Read §2 (the critique-revise loop) and §3's RL-from-AI-feedback stage. The eval table's refusal-vs-helpfulness tradeoff is the part you will re-measure yourself in L72.

Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena

Zheng et al. · LMSYS / UC Berkeley · 2023
●●○○○○○○~45 minREAD

Taught the field that a strong model is an acceptably consistent judge at scale, and then documented the three ways it is not: position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement. That failure catalogue is the checklist for anything you ship that grades its own output.

Read §3.2 (the bias taxonomy) and the agreement-with-humans numbers in §3.1. Then build the swap-augmented judge test in L89 — it is the cheapest possible protection.

5 · Track D — Agents, Evals, Systems #

Collectively: an agent is a loop with a budget, and the literature’s real contribution is the benchmarks, because they encode the definition of the task. Read the evals section of these papers more carefully than the method section — the method is what they could do, the eval is what the claim means.

Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools

Schick et al. · Meta AI Research · 2023
●●●○○○○~40 minSKIM

Taught the field that tool-use annotations can be generated by the model itself: insert an API call, keep it only if the loss on the following tokens drops. Crude by 2026 standards, and still the cleanest demonstration of self-labelled tool training.

Read §2.2 (the self-supervision filter) and the sampling decision rule. Then note what it cannot do: no multi-step, no recovery from a failed call. That gap is L70.

Reflexion: Verbal Reinforcement Learning

Shinn et al. · Northeastern / MIT / Princeton · 2023
●●○○○○○○~35 minREAD

Taught the field that a failed trial plus a written reflection carried into the next trial is a reinforcement signal without gradients. It is the cheapest self-improvement loop there is, and its failure mode — confabulated lessons that make the next attempt worse — is the one to instrument.

Read §3 (actor / evaluator / self-reflection memory) and the ablation showing which component actually supplies the gain. Then read the discussion of the memory as an unstructured text buffer; that is where it drifts. See L68.

Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior

Park et al. · Stanford / Google · 2023
●●●○○○○~50 minSKIM

Taught the field the memory architecture that most agent frameworks still copy: an observation stream, scored retrieval by recency/importance/relevance, periodic reflection, and plan revision. It is a memory paper dressed as a social-simulation paper.

Read §4 (memory stream, retrieval function, reflection) and the ablations that remove each component. Skip the believability evaluation — the sample sizes do not support the conclusion. Pair with L80.

Voyager: An Open-Ended Embodied Agent with LLMs

Wang et al. · NVIDIA / Caltech · 2023
●●●○○○○~45 minSKIM

Taught the field that an agent can write and store verified code as reusable skill, so capability compounds across episodes instead of being relearned. The skill library + execution-verified addition rule is the transferable idea; the Minecraft setting is not.

Read §2's three components (automatic curriculum, skill library, iterative prompting with environment feedback) and the ablation on the skill library. The verification-by-execution gate is the part your own promotion gates should look like.

MMLU: Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding

Hendrycks et al. · UC Berkeley / Columbia / Chicago · 2020
●●○○○○○○~30 minSKIM

Taught the field a broad knowledge screen that lasted five model generations, and then taught it how benchmarks die: saturation, contamination, and format sensitivity. Its real value in 2026 is as the canonical case study in L74.

Read §2 (construction and the subject list) and §5's discussion of why multiple-choice was chosen. Then read the contamination caveats and stop trusting any single MMLU number.

HELM: Holistic Evaluation of Language Models

Liang et al. · Stanford CRFM · 2022
●●○○○○○○~40 minREAD

Taught the field that evaluation needs a scenario × metric matrix and declared omissions, not a leaderboard column. The idea that you must report coverage alongside score is the most under-implemented good idea in applied AI.

Read §2 (the taxonomy of scenarios and metrics) and the section on reporting uncertainty. Then steal the shape for your own eval harness in L88.

Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code (HumanEval)

Chen et al. · OpenAI · 2021
●●○○○○○○~30 minSKIM

Taught the field pass@k and the execute-the-tests evaluation paradigm — the first widely used functional correctness benchmark. It also quietly set the pattern for the verifiable reward you will build a training loop around in L63.

Read §2.2 (the unbiased pass@k estimator — get the sampling math right, it is misquoted constantly) and the caveats about hand-written tests and prompt sensitivity.

SWE-bench: Resolving Real-World GitHub Issues

Jimenez et al. · Princeton / CMU · 2023
●●●○○○○~50 minREAD

Taught the field a benchmark that is hard to game: real repository, real issue, hidden tests, patch applied and executed. It moved agent evaluation from “did the transcript look right” to “did the tests pass”.

Read §3 (task construction and the test-based validation gate) and §5's error taxonomy — that taxonomy is a ready-made debugging checklist for any coding agent you operate. Skip the per-model results.

GAIA: A Benchmark for General AI Assistants

Mialon et al. · Meta AI / Hugging Face · 2023
●●○○○○○○~35 minSKIM

Taught the field a human-verifiable benchmark where the answer is a short string but getting it requires multi-step tool use, file handling, and web navigation. It made “easy for humans, hard for assistants” an explicit design criterion.

Read §2 (the question-design principles and the three difficulty tiers) and the scoring rule. Notice that the answer format — exact string — is what makes it auditable; copy that discipline.

AgentBench: Evaluating LLMs as Agents

Liu et al. · Tsinghua KEG · 2023
●●○○○○○○~35 minSKIP-UNLESS

Taught the field that agentic ability varies wildly by environment type and does not transfer from chat benchmarks. The finding has aged well; the specific model numbers have not.

Read §4's failure-mode analysis (invalid format, context limit, task limit) — these are the same three walls your harness hits in L82.

Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate and Critique

Asai et al. · UW / AI2 / IBM · 2023
●●●○○○○~45 minSKIM

Taught the field that the model should decide whether to retrieve and then whether the retrieved passage supports the claim, via trained reflection tokens. The second half — a learned groundedness critic — is the useful production idea.

Read §3 (the four reflection token types and their training data construction). Then read §5's latency costs; adaptive retrieval is not free. Pair with L87.

Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain QA

Karpukhin et al. · Facebook AI · 2020
●●○○○○○○~35 minREAD

Taught the field that a dual encoder trained on question–passage pairs beats BM25 — and, by extension, that retrieval quality is a trainable property, not a property of the embedding you happened to pick off the shelf. This is the paper that made dense RAG possible.

Read §3 (the in-batch negative trick, which is the whole training recipe) and the passage-retrieval question-construction discussion. Then read L85 on why hybrid search beats both extremes.

Longformer: The Long-Document Transformer

Beltagy, Peters, Cohan · AI2 · 2020
●●●○○○○~30 minSKIP-UNLESS

Taught the field that attention can be given a sliding-window plus global-token pattern to get linear cost on long documents — the idea that returns, generalised, in every 2026 sparse-attention design.

Read §3's attention pattern figure and the global-attention discussion. That figure is the ancestor of the DSA patterns in L34.

Are Sixteen Heads Really Better than One?

Michel, Levy, Neubig · CMU / Allen AI · 2019
●●○○○○○○~25 minSKIM

Taught the field that many attention heads can be pruned at inference with little loss while being essential for training — the first careful statement that redundancy in a transformer is task-dependent, not architecture-dependent.

Read the per-head pruning result and the layer-wise finding, then the caveat about pruned layers behaving like shallow networks on some tasks. It is the honest nuance, not the headline.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models: A Survey

Gao et al. · Tongji / Fudan · 2023
●●○○○○○○~60 min (survey)SKIM

Taught the field the naive / advanced / modular RAG taxonomy and, more usefully, the habit of judging a RAG system by its retrieval recall and faithfulness separately rather than by end-to-end vibe.

Read the naive-to-advanced-to-modular progression and the evaluation section. Use the bibliography, not the prose.

A Survey on LLM-based Autonomous Agents

Wang et al. · Renmin University · 2023
●●○○○○○○~60 min (survey)SKIP-UNLESS

Taught the field a stable vocabulary — profile, memory, planning, action — that most agent frameworks still use in their docs.

Read only the section defining those four modules and the environment-interaction loop. The paper is offline-agent-era; read it for the vocabulary, not the architecture.

6 · Reading order: the 6-week sequence #

The order is dependency-driven, not chronological. Each week ends with something you build, because a week of reading that produces no artifact produced nothing. At 30 minutes a day, six weeks is about 21 hours of reading plus the builds.

Week 1 — The machine

Goal: be able to draw the transformer block from memory and say what every term costs in bytes.

  1. Attention Is All You Need — the base op. Days 1–2.
  2. Layer Normalization then RMSNorm in L05 — why the stack is trainable at all.
  3. RoPE — position, and why RoPE survived into every 2024–26 frontier model.
  4. Adam + Mixed precision — the two things that silently break runs.
  5. The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration — closing the loop from training to the token you actually see.

Why this order: attention without normalisation is un-trainable at depth, and RoPE only makes sense once you have seen the attention score equation.

Build: L19 — the tiny GPT. No build, no week.

▼

Week 2 — The budget

Goal: be able to size a training run and defend the number without a benchmark.

  1. Kaplan scaling laws — read it while knowing it is about to be corrected.
  2. Chinchilla — the correction, and why the method mattered more than the number.
  3. LLaMA — over-training, and why inference cost rewrote the rule.
  4. GPT-3 — capability as a function of scale, and in-context learning as the payoff.

Why this order: you must read Kaplan before Chinchilla or you will not feel what it cost the field to be wrong. Then LLaMA shows the third regime.

Build: L27 compute-optimal calculator, then break it deliberately with inference costs.

▼

Week 3 — The iron

Goal: reason about the cost of any architecture change on a napkin.

  1. Switch Transformer → GShard — sparse activation and its tax.
  2. DeepSeekMoE — fine-grained plus shared experts; the design every open MoE now copies.
  3. FlashAttention → FA-2 — memory bandwidth as the real constraint.
  4. GQA → DeepSeek-V2 (MLA) — the KV cache as the object being optimised.
  5. Mamba — the alternative, and its recall limit.

Why this order: MoE and KV compression are both “spend compute differently” arguments; reading them together makes the trade space visible. FlashAttention sits between them because it changes the cost model they are all optimising inside.

Build: L33 memory table and L38 bandwidth model.

▼

Week 4 — Training a frontier-class model, and manufacturing behaviour

Goal: explain the whole production pipeline, pretraining to post-training, with the before-and-after of each stage.

  1. DeepSeek-V3 — the reproducible systems recipe; two days.
  2. InstructGPT — the canonical three-stage pipeline.
  3. PPO → DeepSeekMath (GRPO) — why dropping the critic was the unlock.
  4. DPO — the closed-form shortcut, and what it cannot teach.
  5. LoRA + QLoRA — the economics of actually doing it.
  6. deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V2 style releases of weights — read a model card against the paper once. See huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3.2.

Why this order: V3 first gives you the substrate; InstructGPT then shows the smallest pipeline that turns it into an assistant; PPO-before-GRPO is non-negotiable because GRPO is defined as the removal of PPO's critic.

Build: L55 DPO loss in numpy, L56 GRPO on a bandit.

▼

Week 5 — Reasoning

Goal: know exactly which mechanism buys reasoning depth, and which merely buys length.

  1. Chain-of-Thought → Self-Consistency — thought as compute, then thought as sampling.
  2. Tree of Thoughts — thought as search, with its evaluator dependency.
  3. DeepSeek-R1 — RL against verifiable rewards grows a long chain; distillation moves it into small models.
  4. DeepSeek-V3.2 — reasoning inside tool use, plus environment synthesis at scale.
  5. DeepSeek-V4 — what this all costs when the context is a million tokens.

Why this order: prompting-era CoT establishes the vocabulary; R1 shows the training-time version of the same insight; V3.2 and V4 show where the 2026 frontier spends it.

Build: L61 budget allocator; measure the crossover yourself.

▼

Week 6 — Agents and proof

Goal: ship an audited agent and be able to answer “how do you know it works” with a harness, not an anecdote.

  1. ReAct — the loop.
  2. Reflexion → Generative Agents → Voyager — memory, advantage, and skills that persist.
  3. RAG → DPR → Self-RAG — the retrieval stack.
  4. SWE-bench → GAIA → MMLU — execution-based, exact-string, and saturated: three generations of eval design in one sitting.
  5. HELM → LLM-as-judge — how to report a score with its coverage and its biases.

Why this order: agent method papers are meaningless without the eval papers that define success. Ending on HELM and the MT-Bench judge paper means you finish with the audit lens, which is the point of the course.

Build: L88 eval harness with confidence intervals, then L99.

The trap in this page. Sixty papers is about 80 hours of reading and it will feel like progress. It is not progress until a number changes in a repo you own. The 6-week sequence is designed so that every week ends in an artifact — if you find yourself skipping the build weeks, you have converted a curriculum into a podcast.

7 · How to consume this #

This block is identical on all three resource pages, deliberately. The cadence is the same whether you are reading a paper, a blog post, or watching a talk, because the failure is the same.

The 30-minutes-a-day cadence

00–05 — orient. Title, abstract, all figures, all tables, conclusion. Write one sentence: what claim is this artifact making, and what would falsify it?

05–25 — the load-bearing part. One section, chosen by the verdict above — never page one to page N. Take notes as questions, not summaries.

25–30 — the tag. One line in a log: claim · evidence · what I would have to build to check it. This log is your citable memory; a paper you cannot cite from memory did not get read.

The skim-versus-read rule

Read deeply only if you will implement something from it within two weeks. That is the whole rule. Everything else is a skim, permanently, and that is correct — the abstraction is what you need, not the derivation.

Concretely: implementing a loss function (DPO, GRPO) → read deeply, derive it on paper, reproduce the loss. Citing a scaling constant → skim, keep the table. Choosing an attention variant → read the memory arithmetic section only, and compute it.

If the two-week window is imaginary — you are “reading up” for a project that does not exist — it is a skim. Mark it as such and move on without guilt.

Reading without a build is entertainment. It produces the feeling of competence and none of the artifacts. The only defence is a hard gate: a paper earns a place in your log when a number in a repo you own moved because of it — a latency, a memory line, a loss curve, a pass rate. If nothing moved, you were watching.
