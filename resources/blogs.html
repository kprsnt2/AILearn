RESOURCES · MAINTAINED SOURCES

Blogs & Newsletters — 25 sources, four kinds

Papers tell you what was proved; engineering blogs tell you what broke. The failure mode of following blogs is that you cannot tell a primary source from a person who read three tweets about it. This page separates the two, marks the noise honestly, and gives every source a topic to start on instead of a deep link that will rot.

25 sources 4 tiers links verified 2026-10 + a skip list
primary engineering researcher craft practitioner signal docs-as-blog hype filter

How to read the links here. Each source links its root or its index — the stable thing — and the “start here” line names a topic to search for on that site rather than a deep URL that will 404 in eighteen months. Where a deep URL is stable and checked, it is linked inline. Every root on this page returned HTTP 200 at build time (Oct 2026); deep links are flagged inline and were separately checked.

1 · First-party engineering #

The lab’s own account of its own systems. Highest density of detail nobody else can supply — actual failure modes, actual cost numbers, actual post-mortems — and also the most marketing-shaped, so read the engineering write-ups and ignore the launch posts. Seven sources.

Anthropic — Engineering

FIRST-PARTY · AGENT SYSTEMS

Why it matters: the only first-party source that documents agent harness design — tool interfaces, context management, multi-agent orchestration, evaluation infrastructure — as patterns rather than announcements. One entry is effectively required reading for this course: the piece on building effective agents (workflows vs agents, orchestrator-worker, evaluator-optimizer) at anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents.

Topic: context engineering and agent evaluation — how they keep a long-running agent’s context from degrading. Maps to L79 and L81.

OpenAI — News & Research

FIRST-PARTY · MODELS & SYSTEMS

Why it matters: system cards and model-index pages are where the honest hedged claims live — eval methodology, refusal rates, the known-limitations section. A system card is the closest thing the industry has to an audit document; read it the way you would read a 10-K, for the footnotes.

Topic: system cards and eval methodology. Every launch, read the limitations section before the benchmark chart. Maps to L74.

Google DeepMind — Blog

FIRST-PARTY · SCIENCE

Why it matters: the research-lab voice — Gemini technical reports, proof-search systems, and longer science write-ups — is the best available demonstration of how a serious lab reasons about verification, search, and formal rewards. It is also where the technical reports behind most “new capability” claims are published.

Topic: formal verification and search-augmented reasoning — the write-ups behind math-proof systems. Maps to L63.

DeepSeek — Releases, Docs & Model Repos

FIRST-PARTY · MOST OPERATIONAL

Why it matters: DeepSeek publishes what other labs redact — training cost, the load-balancing trick, inference kernel choices — in technical reports and repo READMEs. For this course it is the single most useful first-party source, because architecture, serving economics, and agent data synthesis are all documented at a level you can reimplement.

Topic: the release notes that accompany each generation — in particular the V3.2-Exp notes on sparse attention at github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp and the API docs at api-docs.deepseek.com for the serving model (thinking mode, reasoning effort, peak/off-peak pricing). Maps to L34, L45, L69.

Meta AI — Blog

FIRST-PARTY · OPEN WEIGHTS

Why it matters: the Llama model cards and the posts around them are the most complete public description of an open-weights release process — data-filtering decisions, acceptable-use policy, red-team results. When you fine-tune an open model, this is the document that tells you what you may ship.

Topic: the Llama model cards — specifically the red-team and data-handling sections, not the benchmark table. Maps to L52.

Mistral AI — News

FIRST-PARTY · EFFICIENCY

Why it matters: the clearest writing from a lab whose entire thesis is efficiency — small active-parameter sparse models that are cheap to serve, document models, and the argument for cost per task over peak capability. Useful as a counterweight to pretraining-scale narratives.

Topic: sparse models and cost per token — read a MoE announcement beside a napkin calculation from L43.

Google Research — Blog

FIRST-PARTY · METHOD

Why it matters: the institutional home of the papers this course rests on — the transformer, T5, the MoE line — and the posts that explain the experimental design behind each result. When a paper’s ablation leaves you guessing what they varied, the accompanying post usually says.

Topic: the post that accompanies each scaling or MoE paper. Read it immediately after the paper and compare what the post claims with what the ablation shows.

2 · Researcher blogs #

One person, thinking carefully, with the machinery in front of them. Highest signal-per-minute on the internet, because there is no marketing department and no release date to hit. Where a blog post and a technical report disagree, read the post first, then the report — and assume neither is lying. Seven sources.

Lilian Weng — Lil’Log

RESEARCHER BLOG · THE BEST INDEX IN THE FIELD

Why it matters: the field’s best survey writer. Each post builds an entire taxonomy for a young area — mechanisms, formulas, failure modes, citations — without hype and without omitting the awkward cases. Two posts have served as whole teams’ syllabus: the agent post at posts/2023-06-23-agent (planning, memory, tool use) and the reward-hacking post at posts/2024-11-28-reward-hacking. The hallucination post is the best plain-language account of why grounded systems still confabulate.

Topic: start with the agent post, then reward hacking, then hallucination. Maps to L76 and L75.

Sebastian Raschka — Blog · Ahead of AI

RESEARCHER BLOG · BUILD-FROM-SCRATCH

Why it matters: the person who most consistently turns a paper into runnable code and then tells you which parts of the paper the code cannot reproduce. His architecture and fine-tuning explainers are the clearest bridge from equation to training script, and the companion repo (github.com/rasbt/LLMs-from-scratch) is the one to actually run.

Topic: the “implement this from scratch” series — attention, LoRA, preference tuning. Maps to L06 and L55.

Jay Alammar — Visualizing Machine Learning

RESEARCHER BLOG · MECHANICAL INTUITION

Why it matters: the diagrams that taught a generation what attention actually computes. Not current research, and not trying to be — it is the fastest way to make the transformer’s tensor shapes permanent in your head, which is a prerequisite for reading any of the efficiency papers on the papers page.

Topic: the illustrated transformer and illustrated GPT-2 posts (both checked live at illustrated-transformer and illustrated-gpt2). Maps to L06 and L19.

Andrej Karpathy — Blog

RESEARCHER BLOG · FIRST PRINCIPLES

Why it matters: long gaps between posts and every one worth the wait. The unpublished-transformers essay on how the unreasonable effectiveness of recurrent networks is really about training a program, and the essay on deep reinforcement learning and the policy-gradient family, are the clearest explanations of “what is the neural net an implementation of” anywhere.

Topic: the essays on RNN effectiveness and on deep RL — both checked live at 2015/05/21/rnn-effectiveness and 2016/05/31/rl. For the video side of the same material, see videos. Maps to L03.

Eugene Yan — Blog

RESEARCHER BLOG · APPLIED SYSTEMS

Why it matters: the rare writer who documents applied systems with evaluation numbers attached — RAG decompositions, recommendation and ranking pipelines, and the engineering discipline of writing down what you measured. His “patterns” write-ups are the ones that survive contact with a production system.

Topic: RAG and recommender-system evaluation write-ups — search the site for “patterns”. Maps to L85 and L87.

Chip Huyen — Blog

RESEARCHER BLOG · ML SYSTEMS

Why it matters: writes about ML as a systems and operations problem — serving, data distribution shift, inference optimisation, evaluation in production — which is precisely the layer your agent projects live in. The ML-interview book at huyenchip.com/ml-interviews-book is a free, unusually rigorous checklist of the systems knowledge expected of someone at your level.

Topic: inference optimisation and data distribution shift. Maps to L44 and L91.

Transformer Circuits Thread (Anthropic)

RESEARCH PUBLICATION · MECHANISTIC INTERPRETABILITY

Why it matters: the primary venue for mechanistic interpretability — not a blog but a serialized research object, with interactive toy models you can run in the browser. The “toy models of superposition” piece at transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model and the induction-heads piece at 2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads are the two that change how you debug a model rather than how you talk about one.

Topic: superposition, then induction heads. Maps to L49 and L48.

3 · Practitioner media #

Where the field argues with itself. Faster than papers, less verifiable than papers, and often the first place a real production failure gets named. Read these for signal about what is breaking, not for facts you will cite. Six sources.

Simon Willison — Weblog

PRACTITIONER · HIGHEST FREQUENCY, HIGHEST DISCIPLINE

Why it matters: the best-documented practitioner log in the field. Writes daily, experiments in public, and — unusually — labels his own uncertainty and corrects himself on the record. His posts are the fastest reliable way to find out that a model or tool you were about to bet on has a specific, reproducible problem.

Topic: prompt injection and tool-permission attacks, and the running commentary on agent security. Maps to L93 and L84.

Latent Space — Newsletter & Podcast

PRACTITIONER · AI ENGINEERING

Why it matters: the only outlet whose beat is explicitly “AI engineer” rather than “researcher” — shipping, serving, evaluating, pricing. The interview format is where practitioners say the operational thing they would not put in a paper: what the eval actually cost, what the latency actually was, what they had to rip out.

Topic: episodes on inference economics and agent infrastructure. Listen at 1.5× and check one number per episode against the vendor’s own docs. Maps to L44.

The Gradient

PRACTITIONER · LONG-FORM EXPLAINERS

Why it matters: commissioned long-form explainers written by people who talk to the authors — the closest thing to a per-paper companion essay with the surrounding context reconstructed. Their metrics-explainer work is the best plain-language treatment of why a benchmark number is not a capability.

Topic: their explainer on evaluation metrics for language models — checked live at thegradient.pub/understanding-evaluation-metrics-for-language-models. Maps to L88.

Interconnects — Nathan Lambert

PRACTITIONER · POST-TRAINING & POLICY

Why it matters: the most consistent public analysis of post-training — preference data, RLHF/RLVR tradeoffs, why some labs’ recipes work and others quietly fail — written by someone who worked on the inside. It is the commentary layer over Track C, and it is honest about the parts nobody benchmarks.

Topic: RLVR vs RLHF and the state of the post-training stack — browse the archive at interconnects.ai/archive by tag. Maps to L64.

Alignment Forum

PRACTITIONER · ADVERSARIAL TECHNICAL DEBATE

Why it matters: threaded technical argument with the norms of a research forum — claims get challenged, authors reply, and the critique is part of the artifact. This is the venue for questions your production system will ask you later: what does the model actually want, what does a refusal mean, what breaks containment.

Topic: interpretability and deception-adjacent evaluation threads. Read the objection replies, not the top-level post. Maps to L93.

LessWrong

PRACTITIONER · MIXED SIGNAL

Why it matters: the origin of most of the vocabulary the safety literature uses — specification gaming, Goodhart, mesa-optimisation — and still the best place to find carefully argued operationalisations of those ideas. It is also a general-purpose community, so signal rate varies enormously by author; filter hard.

Topic: specification gaming and reward-gaming case lists — these are directly reusable as your adversarial reward tests. Maps to L75.

4 · Docs-as-blogs #

Not blogs in tone — release notes, tutorials, and design docs that happen to be published as posts. This is where the thing you are actually going to run is described, and where the honest performance numbers usually live. Six sources.

Hugging Face — Blog

DOCS-AS-BLOG · THE REFERENCE LIBRARY

Why it matters: the largest free library of worked examples in the field — family-by-family model write-ups, trainer and quantisation recipes, and detailed architecture explainers. The magnum opus is the mixture-of-experts explainer at huggingface.co/blog/moe, which is the clearest companion to the MoE papers in L30.

Topic: MoE explained, then the TRL fine-tuning posts (preference optimisation, GRPO). Maps to L30 and L56.

PyTorch — Blog

DOCS-AS-BLOG · THE FRAMEWORK LAYER

Why it matters: release notes are where distributed-training reality is documented — FSDP/DTensor sharding changes, compile behaviour, numerics changes that break your reproducibility. When a training job behaves differently after an upgrade, the answer is usually a paragraph here. Their inference-performance posts are also unusually concrete about memory-bandwidth accounting (see pytorch.org/blog/flash-decoding).

Topic: FSDP/DTensor release notes and anything on quantisation numerics. Maps to L42 and L40.

vLLM — Blog · vllm.ai

DOCS-AS-BLOG · SERVING PRIMITIVE

Why it matters: the reference for how a modern inference server actually schedules work — paged attention, continuous batching, prefill/decode disaggregation, prefix caching. Read the design posts before you dimension a deployment, because they contain the numbers your own latency model needs to be calibrated against.

Topic: paged attention and continuous batching, then prefill/decode disaggregation. Maps to L44 and L45.

SGLang — Project & Docs

DOCS-AS-BLOG · STRUCTURED & AGENT SERVING

Why it matters: the serving stack built around the assumption your workload is an agent, not a chatbot — radix-tree prefix reuse across shared system prompts, constrained/structured decoding, and multi-turn scheduling. If most of your tokens are cacheable instructions, the design notes here are worth more than any benchmark table.

Topic: radix attention / prefix caching and constrained decoding, from the repo docs. Maps to L45 and L91.

Triton — Language & Tutorials

DOCS-AS-BLOG · KERNELS

Why it matters: the shortest honest path from “I understand the math” to “I wrote the fused kernel”. The tutorial that goes from a vector add to a fused softmax to a flash-attention implementation — at triton-lang.org tutorials — is the best executable answer to “why is attention memory-bound?” anywhere.

Topic: the fused-softmax and fused-attention tutorials, in that order. Maps to L39.

Modal — Blog · Examples

DOCS-AS-BLOG · GPU OPS

Why it matters: writes about the unglamorous half of shipping GPU workloads — cold starts, container images, batch scheduling, cost per run — with reproducible code. The examples index is a working library of serving and fine-tuning patterns you can lift for a weekend experiment instead of a cluster.

Topic: cold-start elimination and serverless GPU cost accounting. Maps to L91 and L44.

Together AI — Blog

DOCS-AS-BLOG · KERNELS & INFERENCE

Why it matters: the most kernel-level writing of the inference providers — their FlashAttention-3 work and the memory-bandwidth arguments that go with it — plus concrete throughput/latency reports for open models. Read it when you need to know what a GPU can actually do, not what a benchmark suite says it can do.

Topic: attention kernel design and long-context inference cost. Maps to L38 and L35.

5 · Skip these, honestly #

No names, because the specific outlets change every quarter and the shape of the noise does not. These are neutrally described: nothing here is malicious, it just costs you time and gives you false confidence. If you have a finite reading budget, the categories below are where it leaks.

“#1 on MMLU” leaderboard aggregators

Sites that rank models by scraped public benchmark scores. The scores are usually real; the ranking is not a measurement of your task. Saturated benchmarks plus undocumented contamination produce confidently ordered lists of models that differ by less than the run-to-run noise. Use them as a pointer to what exists, never as evidence.

“Top 10 AI tools / prompts” listicles

Written for distribution, not for use. They optimise for search traffic and affiliate links, contain no failure analysis, and are obsolete within a release cycle. The information you were looking for is in the docs.

Mid-length “X is dead / AGI by Thursday” think pieces

Fast, engaging, and unfalsifiable. Their predictions are not scored, so there is no accountability gradient; the argument is usually an analogy rather than a measurement. Interesting as sociology of the industry, worthless as input to an engineering decision.

Vendor-neutral “best LLM” comparisons

Third-party posts that benchmark many models on a handful of prompts without a reproducibility package. The method is usually under-specified (which temperature, which version, how many trials, what cost), so the numbers cannot be re-derived. Prefer a benchmark you can run yourself, or the vendor’s own system card and its stated limitations — those at least answer to someone.

Aggregate “AI news” newsletters

Daily digests that summarise press releases. They keep you aware of announcements and cost you twenty minutes a day. If you need awareness, one weekly skim of a first-party source is cheaper and more accurate than a daily summary of a summary.

The test that filters everything on this page. Before you spend twenty minutes on any source, ask: if this were wrong, how would I find out? If the answer is “I would not” — because there is no method, no number, no response to criticism — it is not a source, it is a claim. Spend the twenty minutes building the thing it was about instead.

6 · How to consume this #

This block is identical on all three resource pages, deliberately. The cadence is the same whether you are reading a paper, a blog post, or watching a talk, because the failure is the same.

The 30-minutes-a-day cadence

00–05 — orient. Title, opening claim, all figures, and the last paragraph. Write one sentence: what claim is this making, and what would falsify it?

05–25 — the load-bearing part. One section or one argument, never the whole thing. For a blog post that is usually the failure mode or the numbers; for a paper it is the ablation. Take notes as questions, not summaries.

25–30 — the tag. One line in a log: claim · evidence · what I would have to build to check it. A source you cannot cite from memory did not get read.

The skim-versus-read rule

Read deeply only if you will implement something from it within two weeks. That is the whole rule. Everything else is a skim, permanently, and that is correct — you need the abstraction, not the derivation.

Concretely: about to write a DPO or GRPO loss → read deeply. About to pick an inference server → read the scheduling design post and the cost accounting, skip the rest. About to cite an architecture → skim, and keep the memory arithmetic.

If the two-week window is imaginary — you are “staying current” — it is a skim. Mark it as one and move on without guilt.

Reading without a build is entertainment. Following a hundred blogs produces the feeling of competence and none of the artifacts. The only defence is a hard gate: a source earns a place in your log when a number in a repo you own moved because of it — a latency, a memory line, a loss curve, a pass rate. If nothing moved, you were watching.
