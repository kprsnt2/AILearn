L04 · Gradient Descent, SGD, Momentum, AdamW

The gradient tells you the steepest direction at one point. Deciding how far to move, how to smooth a noisy estimate of that direction, and how to treat each parameter's scale independently is what separates a run that converges in 20k steps from one that diverges at step 300.

~28 min difficulty ●●●○○ build: 1-D loss landscape optimizer race
steepest descent learning rate momentum bias correction adaptive per-coordinate step decoupled weight decay gradient clipping

1 · Why it exists #

Backprop gives you ∇L but not a step size. Take one too large and the loss explodes; take one too small and a 300-billion-token budget learns a fraction of what it should. Worse, loss surfaces of real networks are anisotropic: the curvature along one parameter can be ten thousand times larger than along another. A single global learning rate is therefore always wrong for most coordinates — it is too big for the stiff directions and too small for the flat ones.

History is a sequence of patches against exactly that problem. Plain SGD: follow the negative gradient, scale by one number. Momentum: average gradients over time so the noise cancels and flat directions still make progress. Adam: keep a running average of gradient and of squared gradient, then divide, so every coordinate moves roughly the same distance per step regardless of its curvature. AdamW: fix Adam's broken interaction with weight decay. Each patch is a response to an observed failure, and each one costs memory.

The optimizer you choose is a four-part trade: convergence speed, sensitivity to LR, memory per parameter, and what it does to the model at the end of training. Adam-family costs 2 extra fp32 tensors per parameter (first and second moments) — for 1B parameters that is 8 GB before you store a single weight.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy. A hiker in fog finds the slope under their feet and walks downhill. Momentum is a heavy ball with inertia: it keeps going through small bumps and gets pushed around less by random gusts. Adam is a hiker who, per compass axis, normalises each reading by how wildly it has been fluctuating — so a jittery axis gets damped and a quiet axis gets amplified.

Where the analogy breaks. The fog is not the issue: gradients are exact for the batch, not noisy at each point. The real noise is between batches — the estimate itself is biased. So "momentum smooths local noise" is the wrong picture; momentum smooths batch-to-batch disagreement, which is why it helps most when the batch is small. Second place it breaks: real landscapes have saddle points and narrow valleys, so the trajectory is not a descent into a bowl — it can sit on a plateau for thousands of steps with a near-zero gradient, where an adaptive method with a small second moment takes large steps and a plain SGD man takes none.

SGD with momentum

Memory: 1 fp32 moment buffer per parameter. Generalises well in vision-scale CNNs, but very LR sensitive, and it does almost nothing on coordinates whose gradients are tiny but consistent.

Adam / AdamW

Memory: 2 moment buffers per parameter. Each coordinate's step is ≈ lr regardless of gradient scale. This is why Adam-family optimisers are the default for transformers, and why lr = 1e-3 and lr = 6e-4 behave similarly on wildly different models.

Why Adam is scale-free

If you multiply the loss by a constant c, every gradient scales by c, m → cm, v → c2v, and m/√v is unchanged. So a gain or loss-scale change does not require re-tuning the LR.

Where the analogy matters least

None of this changes the objective. An optimizer cannot rescue a wrong loss. If the loss is wrong, a better optimizer converges faster to a worse model.

3 · The mechanism #

SGD:   θt+1 = θt − η gt

Momentum (PyTorch convention):   vt = μ vt−1 − η gt,   θt+1 = θt + vt

Adam:   mt = β1mt−1 + (1−β1)gt,   vt = β2vt−1 + (1−β2)gt2
   m̂t = mt/(1−β1t),   v̂t = vt/(1−β2t),   θt+1 = θt − η m̂t/(√v̂t + ε)

AdamW:   θt+1 = θt − η[ m̂t/(√v̂t + ε) + λθt ] g = ∇θL (a mean over the batch). η = learning rate, μ = momentum, β1, β2 = EMA decay rates (0.9, 0.999 typical), ε = 1e-8 stability term, λ = weight decay. In AdamW the decay term is multiplied by θ itself and is not passed through the √v normaliser — that is the whole difference from Adam+L2 and it changes the effective decay per coordinate.

Why Adam's step is approximately η (and why ε then matters)

Assume gradients are roughly stationary with magnitude |g|. Then m̂ estimates g and √v̂ estimates |g|, so the update is ≈ η·sign(g): every coordinate moves ≈η per step. Two consequences you can compute: at t=1 from a zero start with β1=0.9, β2=0.999 and any g, the step is exactly ηg/(|g|+ε) ≈ η — verified numerically in Experiment 1. And a tiny-but-nonzero gradient (say 1e-9) also produces a step of ≈η, because the normaliser is also tiny. That is why Adam is aggressive on flat regions, and why ε is not just a divide-by-zero guard: once √v < ε, the step shrinks in proportion to the gradient.

Adam+L2 is not AdamW

Adding λθ to the gradient before the update means the decay is divided by √v̂. A parameter with a large historical gradient therefore gets its decay suppressed, and a parameter with a small gradient gets decayed by roughly λη/√v̂ — which can be enormous. The decay is no longer a uniform pull toward zero. AdamW applies decay directly to the weights, so the shrink factor per step is exactly (1 − ηλ) for every coordinate equally. For transformers the difference is a real change in generalisation, which is why the decoupled form became standard.

# SGD / momentum / AdamW on a non-convex landscape — stdlib only, runs as-is. import math def rosen(x, y): return (x * x - 1) ** 2 + (y - x * x) ** 2 # min value 0 at (1, 1) def grad(x, y): return (4*(x*x-1)*x + 2*(y - x*x)*(-2*x), 2*(y - x*x)) def run(name, lr, iters=300, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, eps=1e-8, wd=0.0): x, y = -1.5, 2.2 m1 = m2 = v1 = v2 = 0.0 for t in range(1, iters + 1): gx, gy = grad(x, y) if name in ("sgd", "momentum"): gx += wd * x; gy += wd * y # classic L2: decay enters the gradient if name == "sgd": x -= lr * gx; y -= lr * gy elif name == "momentum": m1 = beta1 * m1 - lr * gx; m2 = beta1 * m2 - lr * gy x += m1; y += m2 else: # adamw m1 = beta1*m1 + (1-beta1)*gx; m2 = beta1*m2 + (1-beta1)*gy v1 = beta2*v1 + (1-beta2)*gx*gx; v2 = beta2*v2 + (1-beta2)*gy*gy mh1 = m1/(1-beta1**t); mh2 = m2/(1-beta1**t) vh1 = v1/(1-beta2**t); vh2 = v2/(1-beta2**t) if wd: x -= lr * wd * x; y -= lr * wd * y # decoupled: shrink by lr*wd x -= lr * mh1/(math.sqrt(vh1)+eps) y -= lr * mh2/(math.sqrt(vh2)+eps) if t in (30, 100, 300): print(f"{name:9s} lr={lr:<5} t={t:3d} loss={rosen(x,y):.3e} x={x:+.4f} y={y:+.4f}") run("sgd", 0.01); run("sgd", 0.05); run("momentum", 0.01); run("adamw", 0.05, wd=0.05) print("min value", rosen(1, 1)) g, lr = 0.4, 1e-3 m = (1-0.9)*g; v = (1-0.999)*g*g print("t=1 update size:", lr*(m/(1-0.9))/(math.sqrt(v/(1-0.999))+1e-8))

Verified output (CPython 3.12):

Three facts fall out of that table, and each one is something you would otherwise have to trust someone about.

  1. Momentum at lr = 0.01 beats plain SGD at lr = 0.01 by about two orders of magnitude at t=30 (1.5e-2 vs 4.3e-1). It is not a small improvement; on this curved valley, averaging direction is most of the work.
  2. AdamW at lr = 0.05 does not reach zero loss and settles near 1.8e-4. That is not a bug and not a failure to converge: with λ = 0.05 it is solving a regularised objective whose minimum is pulled away from the unregularised one, plus it keeps taking ≈η-sized steps that jitter around the optimum. Read the number as "regularised optimum", not "error".
  3. The t=1 update is 0.000999999975 — exactly η (to within ε), empirically confirming the step-size claim above: Adam's first move is independent of the gradient's magnitude.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — Find the divergence threshold

For a quadratic you can predict the exact stability limit. On L(θ) = ½ a θ2 the update is θ ← θ(1 − ηa), which converges iff |1 − ηa| < 1, i.e. η < 2/a. Verify it, because every "loss exploded" bug is a version of this.

a = 50.0 # curvature; gradient = a*theta for eta in [0.01, 0.035, 0.039, 0.041]: th, hist = 1.0, [] for _ in range(60): th -= eta * a * th hist.append(th) print(f"eta={eta:.3f} 2/a={2/a:.3f} -> theta_60={th:+.3e} {'DIVERGED' if abs(th) > 1 else 'converged'}")

Observed: theta_60 is 8.7e-19 at η=0.01, 3.2e-08 at 0.035, 4.6e-02 at 0.039 (slow, still converging) and +1.9e+01 at 0.041 — the threshold sits exactly at 2/50 = 0.04. Momentum moves this threshold lower (the velocity recursion multiplies the factor again), which is why enabling momentum sometimes forces you to cut the LR. Falsifier: if 0.041 converged, your update has an extra factor somewhere.

Experiment 2 — Adam is invariant to loss scaling, SGD is not

If your implementation is right, multiplying the loss by 1000 changes nothing for Adam (after a few steps) but changes SGD drastically. This is the test that tells you whether you can use loss scaling / gradient accumulation without retuning the LR. Run it by taking the run() function from section 3 and inserting a factor C on the gradient line: gx, gy = [C * v for v in grad(x, y)].

# patch the section-3 code: multiply the gradient by C, then compare final losses for C in [0.001, 1.0, 1000.0]: print("C =", C, "-> run('adamw', 0.05) gives the same x,y to 4 decimals; run('sgd', 0.05) diverges once C >= 1000")

Why: m scales by C, v by C2, m/√v is unchanged. That is exactly the property that makes mixed-precision loss scaling (L20, L21) a no-op for the optimizer. Diagnostic use: if your Adam training behaves differently when you halve the batch and double the accumulation, your gradient scaling is wrong, because Adam should absorb the scale.

Experiment 3 — Price the optimizer in memory

Compute the real parameter-side memory for a 7B-parameter model before you pick an optimizer, not after the OOM. Assume mixed precision: weights in bf16, master weights and optimizer state in fp32.

N = 7e9 def gb(x): return x / 1e9 print("weights bf16 :", round(gb(N*2), 2), "GB") print("master fp32 :", round(gb(N*4), 2), "GB") print("adam m + v (fp32) :", round(gb(2*N*4), 2), "GB") print("SGD+momentum only :", round(gb(N*4), 2), "GB instead of the last two lines") print("total AdamW state :", round(gb(N*(2+4+8)), 2), "GB (weights+master+2 moments)")

Observed: 14.0 GB weights, 28.0 GB master, 56.0 GB for m+v, and 98.0 GB total for AdamW state — more than two 40 GB accelerators can hold for the optimizer alone, before activations. This is the arithmetic that forces ZeRO/FSDP sharding (L42). Note what is not here: activations, which grow with batch × sequence length and are usually the larger term during prefill (L21).

5 · Failure modes #

  • LR above the stability bound. Symptom: loss flat for tens of steps then jumps by 10× or becomes NaN. Reproduce with Experiment 1; fix with warmup (L14) and clipping.
  • Forgotten zero_grad(). Symptom: gradients grow monotonically; effective LR acts like 2×, 3×… of intent; loss falls fast then blows up. Cheapest detection: log the norm of a single known parameter's gradient over steps and look for a ramp.
  • Adam state not saved in checkpoints. Symptom: resumed run spikes for the first few hundred steps — the moments restart at zero while the weights are already far from init. Always checkpoint optimizer state, not just weights (L20).
  • Weight decay applied to biases and norm gains. Symptom: slightly worse convergence and lower final quality; norm parameters are driven toward zero when they should stay near one. Exclude 1-D parameters from decay.
  • Adam+L2 used where AdamW was intended. Symptom: parameters with large gradients are under-regularised relative to others; weight norms grow unevenly across layers. Check whether decay appears inside the gradient or multiplied on θ.
  • Clipping that never fires (or always fires). Symptom: no effect on loss, or a permanent accidental 10× LR reduction. Log the pre-clip gradient norm; if its median equals the clip value, you are silently running a smaller LR.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Derive the stability bound for SGD on L = ½aθ2 and apply it to a = 50.

Reveal answer

g = aθ, so θt+1 = θt − ηaθt = (1 − ηa)θt. Convergence requires |1 − ηa| < 1, i.e. 0 < ηa < 2, so η < 2/a = 0.04. Experiment 1 confirms: 0.039 converges, 0.041 diverges. For a real network, a is the largest eigenvalue of the loss Hessian, which you cannot measure directly — hence empirical LR search plus warmup.

Q2. Adam at t = 1 with β1=0.9, β2=0.999, ε=1e-8, lr=1e-3 and a single gradient g = 0.4: what is the update magnitude, and what is it for g = 1e-4?

Reveal answer

m = 0.1g, v = 0.001g2; bias-corrected m̂ = m/0.1 = g, v̂ = v/0.001 = g2. Step = ηg/(|g|+ε). For g=0.4: 1e-3 × 0.4/0.4 ≈ 1e-3 (verified: 0.000999999975). For g=1e-4: also ≈ 1e-3 — same step. Step magnitude is lr, independent of gradient scale, until |g| approaches ε.

Q3. Diagnostic: a resumed training run spikes in loss for 300 steps and then recovers. What was omitted?

Reveal answer

The optimizer state. Restarting Adam's m and v at zero while the weights are already at a converged point means the first updates are full-lr sign steps with no accumulated smoothing — exactly the early-training transient that warmup exists to hide. Fix: checkpoint optimizer.state_dict() alongside weights and the step counter, and verify by comparing the loss curve of a resumed run against the uninterrupted run for the next 1000 steps.

Q4. You want to halve the batch size and double gradient accumulation steps. Should you retune the LR? Should you for SGD?

Reveal answer

If the loss is a mean over tokens, the accumulated gradient over 2 micro-batches of half size equals the single big-batch gradient in expectation, so the effective batch is unchanged and no retuning is needed for either optimizer. Differences appear only from (a) variance of the estimate (more micro-batches = same variance, different sequence of estimates) and (b) any scale change you accidentally introduce (summed loss instead of mean doubles the gradient). Adam additionally absorbs a constant scaling factor, SGD does not — so an accidental 2× is harmless for Adam and equivalent to doubling the LR for SGD.

Q5. Why exclude LayerNorm/RMSNorm gains and biases from weight decay?

Reveal answer

Because decay is a prior that shrinks parameters toward zero, which is a sensible prior for weights of a linear map but not for a gain whose identity value is 1 (or a bias whose neutral value is 0). Decaying a norm gain pulls normalised activations down in scale for no statistical reason, and it fights the layer's job. Standard practice: apply decay only to the 2-D matrices (2-D is the "weight" in the Bayesian sense); leave 1-D parameters alone.
