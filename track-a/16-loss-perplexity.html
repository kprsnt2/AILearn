L16 · Cross-Entropy, Perplexity, Bits-per-Byte

Every training run optimises one scalar, and every model comparison quotes a number derived from it. Getting the units wrong — nats versus bits, token versus byte, mean versus sum — produces comparisons that are off by large factors and look perfectly plausible. This page makes the conversions explicit and checks them numerically.

~26 min difficulty ●●○○○ build: compute PPL from logits by hand
cross-entropy perplexity nats vs bits bits-per-byte log-sum-exp mean vs sum normalisation tokenizer-dependent PPL

1 · Why it exists #

Next-token prediction is a classification problem over a vocabulary of V tokens at every position. The natural loss is negative log-likelihood of the correct token: you want the model to assign high probability to what actually came next. That is cross-entropy, and it is the only objective a base language model is trained on. But the raw number is opaque: is 1.5 good? Compared to what? Perplexity (eloss) gives a rough reading — "the model is as uncertain as if choosing uniformly among this many tokens" — which is why it is quoted in papers and then misquoted in vendor comparisons.

The failure this page is built to prevent is real and expensive: two models with different tokenizers report perplexities that cannot be compared, but the numbers look comparable because they are both "perplexity". A model with a larger vocabulary has fewer tokens to predict, so its per-token perplexity is naturally higher (or lower, depending on the exact trade) and no amount of careful reading of the datasheet will fix it. Bits-per-byte normalises to the underlying text and is comparable across tokenizers — that is the unit you should insist on.

Three numbers, one objective: cross-entropy in nats/token (the training loss), perplexity = exp(that), and bits-per-byte = nats/token × tokens/byte / ln2. Only the third can compare models with different tokenizers, and it is the one to put in a vendor decision.

2 · The mental model #

Analogy. Cross-entropy is the price of a bet: the model buys probability mass on the correct token, and the loss is −ln(probability it assigned). Assign 1.0 to the truth and pay nothing; assign 0.1 and pay 2.30 nats. Perplexity is that price converted into "how many equally likely options did you effectively leave open". Bits-per-byte converts it into "how many bits does it cost you per character of the original text", which is a property of the language and model, not of the tokenizer.

Where the analogy breaks. (1) The bet is not scored on one guess: the loss is the average over every token, and the average hides the distribution. A model with low average loss can be confidently wrong on the cases you care about, and the average will not show it — which is why task evals exist alongside perplexity. (2) Perplexity is not "the number of options": it equals the number of options only for a uniform distribution. A skewed distribution with perplexity 10 is one where the model has 10 options in a geometric-mean sense. (3) Bits-per-byte compares tokenizers only if you compute it on the same bytes; compare on different corpora and you have re-introduced the problem in a different unit.

The three units

nats/token = training loss. bits/token = nats/ln2. perplexity = enats/token. bits/byte = bits/token ÷ bytes/token.

The baseline to remember

A uniform predictor over V tokens has loss ln V. For V = 50,257 that is 10.82 nats and perplexity 50,257. Any model worth deploying must be far below its own ln V — and a model with a bigger vocabulary starts from a higher baseline.

The normalisation trap

Loss must be the mean over predicting tokens. Summing instead of averaging scales the gradient by the number of tokens — a 4096× effective LR change at seq_len 4096. This is the most common loss bug in a hand-written loop.

The numerical trap

Always compute the loss via log-sum-exp on shifted logits. Computing exp first overflows for logits above ~709 in float64 (and far earlier in fp16), and the overflow silently produces inf or NaN rather than a large-but-correct loss.

3 · The mechanism #

pi = softmax(z)i = ezi / ∑jezj
ℓ = −ln ptarget = −(ztarget − logsumexp(z)),   logsumexp(z) = max(z) + ln∑jezj − max(z)
L = (1/T) ∑t ℓt   (mean over predicting tokens)
PPL = eL  ·  bits/token = L/ln2  ·  bits/byte = L/(ln2 × bytes/token)
Baseline: z = 0  ⇒  pi = 1/V  ⇒  ℓ = ln V The max-subtraction in logsumexp is exact (it cancels) and it is what makes the computation stable. Note the shape of the gradient: dℓ/dz = softmax(z) − onehot(target), derived in L03. That single line is the entire backward pass of the output head.
# Cross-entropy, perplexity and bits-per-byte from logits — stdlib only, runs as-is. import math def softmax(z): m = max(z); e = [math.exp(v - m) for v in z]; s = sum(e); return [v / s for v in e] def logsumexp(z): m = max(z); return m + math.log(sum(math.exp(v - m) for v in z)) logits = [2.0, 0.5, -1.0, 3.0] # a 4-token vocabulary target = 1 p = softmax(logits) nll = -math.log(p[target]) print("probs:", [round(v, 5) for v in p]) print("p(target) =", round(p[target], 5), " nll =", round(nll, 5), " bits =", round(nll / math.log(2), 5)) print("stable form -> -(z_t - lse) =", round(-(logits[target] - logsumexp(logits)), 5)) batch = [([2.0, 0.5, -1.0, 3.0], 1), ([0.1, 0.2, 3.0, 0.0], 2), ([1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0], 0)] losses = [-math.log(softmax(l)[t]) for l, t in batch] mean = sum(losses) / len(losses) print("per-token nll:", [round(v, 5) for v in losses], "mean:", round(mean, 5)) print("perplexity:", round(math.exp(mean), 4), " bits/token:", round(mean / math.log(2), 4)) def bpB(nll_nats_per_token, bytes_per_token): return nll_nats_per_token / (math.log(2) * bytes_per_token) for name, nll in [("uniform V=50k", math.log(50000)), ("2.0 nats/token", 2.0), ("1.5 nats/token", 1.5)]: print(f"{name:18s}: {bpB(nll, 4.3):.3f} bits/byte at 4.3 bytes/token")

Verified output (CPython 3.12):

probs: [0.25055, 0.05591, 0.01247, 0.68107] p(target) = 0.05591 nll = 2.88409 bits = 4.16086 stable form -> -(z_t - lse) = 2.88409 per-token nll: [2.88409, 0.15325, 1.38629] mean: 1.47455 perplexity: 4.3691 bits/token: 2.1273 uniform V=50k : 3.630 bits/byte at 4.3 bytes/token 2.0 nats/token: 0.671 bits/byte at 4.3 bytes/token 1.5 nats/token: 0.503 bits/byte at 4.3 bytes/token

Four things are being verified at once. (1) The two loss formulas agree to five decimals (2.88409), so the stable log-sum-exp form is correct. (2) The third example has uniform logits, so its loss is exactly ln 4 = 1.38629 — and the arithmetic returns precisely that, which is a free unit test for any implementation. (3) mean 1.47455 → PPL 4.3691 → 2.1273 bits/token: all three numbers describe the same model, and they are not interchangeable. (4) The last block shows why bits-per-byte matters: a uniform 50k-vocabulary predictor is 3.63 bits/byte (worse than nothing, as it should be), while 2.0 nats/token converts to only 0.671 bits/byte. Same model, two units, numbers that differ by a factor of 5.4.

Why perplexity cannot cross tokenizers

Suppose model A has 50k tokens and model B has 100k tokens, and both are equally good at the underlying task. B's vocabulary can represent roughly twice as much text per token, so B faces a harder per-token prediction problem: the same text becomes half as many, harder predictions. Its per-token perplexity can look worse while its per-byte quality is equal or better. Only bits-per-byte — which divides by the actual bytes of text covered — is invariant to that choice:

bits/byte = (nats/token) / (ln2 × bytes/token)    bytes/token = total UTF-8 bytes / total tokens You must measure bytes/token on the SAME evaluation text for both models. A hand-waved "about 4 characters per token" is an approximation that can easily be wrong by 20%, which is enough to reorder two close models. Count the bytes.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — Unit-test your loss against ln V

The cheapest possible correctness check: feed uniform logits and confirm you get exactly ln V. Then add a large constant to every logit and confirm the loss does not change (softmax is shift-invariant). Both are structural properties, not approximations.

import math, random random.seed(0) def lse(z): m = max(z); return m + math.log(sum(math.exp(v - m) for v in z)) for V in [1000, 32000, 100277, 128000]: z = [0.0] * V print(f"V={V:7d}: loss={-z[0]+lse(z):.4f} ln(V)={math.log(V):.4f} match={abs(-z[0]+lse(z)-math.log(V))<1e-12}") z = [random.gauss(0, 2) for _ in range(8)]; t = 3 print("shift invariance:", round(-(z[t]-lse(z)), 9), round(-((z[t]+1000)-lse([v+1000 for v in z])), 9)) print("naive exp without max-subtraction at large logits:", end=" ") try: e = [math.exp(1000)]; print("ok") except OverflowError: print("OverflowError -> the log-sum-exp subtraction is not optional")

Observed: for every vocabulary size the uniform-loss check matches ln V to better than 1e-12 (6.9078, 10.3735, 11.5157, 11.7598), the shift-invariance check returns the same value twice, and the naive exponential raises OverflowError at logit 1000. Falsifiers: if the uniform check fails, your normalisation is wrong (maybe you divided by V twice); if the shift check fails, you are computing the loss on unnormalised scores somewhere.

Experiment 2 — The normalisation bug, quantified

Summing instead of averaging is the single most common loss bug in hand-written training loops, and it does not crash. Here is exactly what it does to your effective learning rate.

for T in [512, 2048, 4096, 8192]: print(f"seq_len={T:5d}: summed loss multiplies the gradient by {T:5d}x -> same lr now behaves like {T}x the intended lr") print("mean over tokens is invariant to batch and sequence length; that invariance is the point") print("if you use gradient accumulation with SUM, also divide by the number of accumulation steps")

The observable symptom is precise: the run with sum-loss behaves as if the LR were T× larger, so it either diverges immediately or trains at an LR you never chose. And because accumulation changes T, the same code can be stable with batch 4 × 1024 and explode with batch 2 × 2048. The fix is one division, and the test is that loss magnitude should be roughly independent of batch size for a fixed model.

Experiment 3 — Convert a published perplexity into bits-per-byte

Make a cross-model comparison honest. Take your own eval text, tokenize it with both candidate models' tokenizers, and compute bytes/token for each; then convert the reported per-token numbers.

import math # fill these in from your own tokenized eval split def compare(name, ppl_tokens, bytes_per_token): nats = math.log(ppl_tokens) print(f"{name:22s} PPL={ppl_tokens:8.2f} nats/tok={nats:6.3f} bytes/tok={bytes_per_token:5.2f} -> {nats/(math.log(2)*bytes_per_token):6.3f} bits/byte") compare("model A (50k vocab)", 12.0, 4.30) compare("model B (100k vocab)", 18.0, 5.20) compare("model C (byte-level)", 3.0, 1.00)

Observed: A = 12.0 PPL → 2.485 nats over 4.30 bytes → 0.834 bits/byte; B = 18.0 PPL → 2.890 nats over 5.20 bytes → 0.802 bits/byte; C = 3.0 PPL (looks spectacular!) → 1.099 nats over 1 byte → 1.585 bits/byte, the worst of the three. So the model whose perplexity looks best is the worst model, and the model whose perplexity looks worst (B, 18.0) is the best. That reversal is the entire content of this page: do not rank models by perplexity across tokenizers. Falsifier: if the ordering by bits/byte matched the ordering by perplexity on your data, your tokenizers happen to be very similar — recheck on multilingual or code data, where the difference is largest.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Sum instead of mean. Symptom: loss magnitude scales with seq_len and batch; an LR that worked at one config diverges at another. Verify by checking that loss is roughly constant when you double batch size at fixed model.
  • Comparing perplexity across tokenizers. Symptom: the "better" model by PPL loses on task evals; the ranking reverses when you compute bits/byte. Fix the unit, not the model.
  • Nats/bits confusion. Symptom: a 0.693 factor of error everywhere (ln 2); reported bits/token are 1.44× too high. If a conversion looks suspiciously close to 0.69, this is why.
  • Loss computed on padding. Symptom: loss depends on batch composition and the model is trained to emit pad tokens. Count loss terms — they must equal real predicting tokens (L15).
  • Off-by-one on the shift. Symptom: loss is fine at first then plateaus oddly, or the model learns to predict the current token (loss collapses to 0 in a way that is too good). Targets must be inputs shifted by one.
  • Exp before max-subtraction. Symptom: inf/NaN loss after a spike, or a loss that is exactly 0 for one batch and huge for the next. Use log-sum-exp.
  • Evaluating with dropout or in train mode. Symptom: val PPL varies between runs by a percent or two with identical weights. Set eval mode explicitly (L13).
  • Ignoring the tail. Symptom: average PPL improves while the specific behaviour you care about regresses. Average log-likelihood is dominated by easy tokens; report per-domain slices, not just the global mean.

7 · Self-check #

Q1. Compute the loss and perplexity for logits [2.0, 0.5, −1.0, 3.0] with target 1, both in nats and bits.

Reveal answer

softmax = [0.25055, 0.05591, 0.01247, 0.68107]; p(target) = 0.05591. Loss = −ln(0.05591) = 2.88409 nats = 2.88409/ln2 = 4.16086 bits. Perplexity = e2.88409 ≈ 17.89 for this single token — i.e. the model is as uncertain as choosing among ~18 options, on a 4-token vocabulary whose baseline is ln 4 = 1.386. This one token is a much-worse-than-baseline prediction.

Q2. Model X reports PPL 9.0 with 3.9 bytes/token; model Y reports PPL 14.0 with 5.6 bytes/token. On the same text, which is better and by how much?

Reveal answer

X: ln 9.0 = 2.197 nats/token → 2.197/(ln2 × 3.9) = 0.813 bits/byte. Y: ln 14.0 = 2.639 nats/token → 2.639/(ln2 × 5.6) = 0.680 bits/byte. Y is better by 0.133 bits/byte (16%) even though its perplexity is 56% higher. This is the reversal the page is about, and it is why "PPL 9 vs PPL 14" is not a comparison.

Q3. Diagnostic: reported loss drops from 3.2 to 0.0008 after a refactor. What do you check?

Reveal answer

Almost certainly a target/label shift bug: the model is being scored on predicting a token it can already see, or the loss is being computed over a slice that excludes the hard positions, or the mean is over the wrong axis so most positions cancel. Check (a) targets == inputs shifted by one, (b) the count of loss terms equals real predicting tokens, (c) the loss is computed in log space. A genuine 3.2 → 0.0008 improvement is not physically plausible in one refactor.

Q4. Why is "mean over tokens" preferred to "mean over sequences"?

Reveal answer

Because the objective is per-token likelihood, and averaging per sequence gives each sequence equal weight regardless of length — a 10-token document would count as much as a 4,000-token one, which biases the model toward short sequences and makes the gradient depend on length distribution. Mean-over-tokens is the maximum-likelihood estimator and is invariant to batching and packing. The exception is when you want per-document fairness (e.g. balanced domains), which is a deliberate re-weighting that must be documented as such.

Q5. A model's global val PPL improves by 3% but your task accuracy drops. What is the most likely explanation, and what do you report?

Reveal answer

The average is dominated by easy, high-frequency tokens (function words, formatting), so a small global gain can coexist with a regression on the rare, task-relevant patterns. Report per-domain and per-token-frequency-bucket NLL alongside the global number, and check the task-specific slice. Also possible: prompt-format mismatch between the perplexity eval corpus and the task format, in which case the PPL number was never measuring what the task needs.
