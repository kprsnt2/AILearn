L98 · Fine-tune vs RAG vs prompting: the real decision rule

They are not competitors. RAG changes what the model knows; fine-tuning changes what it does. The decision is two steps — diagnose which one you lack, then cost the fix. And a $2,400 fine-tune needs 2.1 million queries to pay back its context savings, which is the arithmetic that ends most fine-tuning proposals.

~30 min difficulty ●●●●○ build: decision matrix with costs
knowledge vs form staleness of a fine-tune payback period context-cost elimination LoRA / QLoRA distillation for cost catastrophic forgetting eval before and after

1 · Why it exists #

The failure: someone fine-tuned a model to teach it facts, and three weeks later the facts were wrong. A fine-tune bakes a fact into weights. Weights do not update when the fact changes, cannot be cited, cannot be scoped per user, and cannot be deleted on request. Every one of those is a requirement in a real knowledge product, and every one is cheaply met by retrieval and not at all by training.

The mirror failure: someone added RAG to fix an output-format problem, and it did not work. The model has the information and produces a malformed JSON object with a missing comma. Retrieval cannot fix syntax. Prompt engineering might; constrained decoding or a fine-tune on the target format will. The technique was chosen before the diagnosis.

The third failure is cost accounting. "Fine-tuning removes the 4,000-token context so it pays for itself" — at $0.28/1M input tokens, 4,000 tokens saves $0.00112 per query. A $2,400 fine-tune therefore needs 2,142,857 queries to break even, which at 1M queries/month is two months of a million-query operation, or never for anything smaller. The number, not the intuition, is the decision.

Two questions, in order. (1) What is missing: knowledge, form, or capability? Knowledge → RAG. Form → prompt first, then fine-tune or constrained decoding. Capability → fine-tune. Then (2) does the fix pay back? Cost it per month and compute the payback period against your real volume. Both steps are computable before writing a line of code.

2 · The mental model #

Three levers act on different parts of the system, with different change rates and different audit properties:

LeverChangesUpdates atAuditable?Per-user scope?
Promptinginstruction, examples, formatimmediately, no costyes — the prompt is text in the traceyes
RAGwhat the model can seeon index rebuild (minutes to days, L97)yes — cites its sourcesyes — filter per user
Fine-tunebehaviour, style, format, skillon retrain (hours to weeks)no — the cause is in weightsno — one model for all

The properties that decide most real cases are in the last two columns. If your product must answer "where did this claim come from", only RAG and prompting give you that. If it must enforce per-tenant access, only RAG's filters do — one fine-tuned model cannot hold two tenants' facts without leaking.

Analogy: a textbook versus a library card

Fine-tuning is printing a textbook: fast to consult, expensive to reprint, and every copy is identical. RAG is the library card: the model learns how to look things up, the books stay current, and each reader can be given a different shelf.

Where it breaks

A textbook is at least a snapshot with a date on it. A fine-tune carries no date and no provenance — the model cannot tell you which facts came from training and which it inferred, so it will state a stale fact with exactly the same confidence as a current one. And unlike a textbook, you cannot spot-check it cheaply: verifying that a fine-tune still holds its facts requires a full eval (L88) every time, and catastrophic forgetting (L73) means it may have lost capabilities you never tested for.

3 · The mechanism #

Step one is a diagnosis, and it is a lookup, not a judgement:

SymptomDeficiencyLeverWhy not the others
Does not know the factknowledgeRAGfine-tuning it goes stale; prompting cannot invent it

Step two is arithmetic. The recurring cost of a per-query context injection, and the payback period of removing it:

savings/query = Tctx · pin      payback = Cft / (Q · savings/query) Tctx = injected context tokens, pin = input price, Cft = one-off fine-tune cost, Q = monthly query volume. With T=4000, p=0.28/1M: savings = $0.00112. At C_ft=$2,400 and Q=1M/month: 2.14 months. At C_ft=$24,000: 21.4 months. Both are quotable; neither requires an opinion.

The decision function, with the diagnosis and the economics in one place.

# ---------- STEP 1: diagnosis ---------- KNOWLEDGE = {"missing_fact", "stale_fact", "needs_citation", "per_user_scope", "deletable_fact"} FORM = {"bad_format", "tone", "schema_noncompliance", "length"} CAPABILITY = {"rare_skill", "domain_reasoning", "cost_reduction"} def diagnose(symptoms): k = symptoms & KNOWLEDGE f = symptoms & FORM c = symptoms & CAPABILITY if k: return "RAG", f"knowledge symptoms: {sorted(k)}" if f: return "PROMPT_THEN_FORMAT_ENFORCE_THEN_FT", f"form symptoms: {sorted(f)}" if c: return "FINE_TUNE", f"capability symptoms: {sorted(c)}" return "NO_CHANGE", "no symptom class matched" # ---------- STEP 2: economics ---------- def payback_months(ft_cost, queries_per_month, ctx_tokens, price_in): per_query = ctx_tokens * price_in if per_query <= 0: return float("inf") return ft_cost / (queries_per_month * per_query) def rag_cost_per_query(ctx_tokens, price_in, out_tokens, price_out, query_tokens=0, embed_price=0): return (query_tokens*embed_price + ctx_tokens*price_in + out_tokens*price_out) # ---------- STEP 3: the composed answer ---------- def recommend(symptoms, volume, ft_cost=2400, ctx=4000): lever, why = diagnose(symptoms) pb = payback_months(ft_cost, volume, ctx, 0.28/1e6) return {"lever": lever, "why": why, "payback_months_if_ft": round(pb, 2) if pb != float("inf") else None}

Three things about that code. (1) Knowledge symptoms short-circuit to RAG regardless of cost, because no amount of training fixes a fact that changes. (2) Form symptoms route through prompt first — the cheap levers precede the expensive one. (3) The payback calculation is returned even when the recommendation is RAG, because the honest artefact is the number, and someone will ask "what about fine-tuning?" in the review.

ScenarioDiagnosisLeverComposition
internal policy Q&A, 200k queries/mostale facts, needs citationRAGno fine-tune
structured extraction from invoices, 5M/moschema non-complianceprompt → constrained decodingfine-tune only if enforcement still fails at scale
code review with a house style, 2M/motone + rare skillfine-tune+ RAG for the lint rules (they change)
support agent, 10M/mo, needs bothknowledge + formbothfine-tune the grounding behaviour; RAG the facts

The audit requirement for a fine-tune is a pre/post eval on the same golden set, plus a forgetting check: capabities that were not the target of the fine-tune must be re-measured, because training on one task degrades others (L73). A fine-tune whose report shows only the improved metric is an unreviewable change — you cannot tell a capability gain from a capability trade.

4 · Your hands #

Experiment 1 — The payback period, computed

What a fine-tune must save per query, and how many queries that takes to recover its cost.

def payback_months(ft_cost, queries_per_month, ctx_tokens, price_in): per_query = ctx_tokens * price_in return ft_cost / (queries_per_month * per_query) PRICE_IN = 0.28 / 1e6 saved = 4000 * PRICE_IN print(f"saved per query by dropping 4000 context tokens: ${saved:.6f}") for cost in (2400, 24000): print(f" ft cost ${cost:,}: break-even at {cost/saved:,.0f} queries") for vol in (100_000, 1_000_000, 10_000_000): print(f" at {vol:,}/month, $2,400 pays back in {payback_months(2400, vol, 4000, PRICE_IN):.2f} months")

Observed output: saved per query ... $0.001120; ft cost $2,400: break-even at 2,142,857 queries; ft cost $24,000: break-even at 21,428,571 queries; then at 100k/month 21.43 months, at 1M/month 2.14 months, at 10M/month 0.21 months. The reading: fine-tuning for context savings is a volume play. Below ~1M queries/month it does not pay back within a year, and the argument "it removes the context so it's cheaper" is arithmetically false at that scale. Falsifier: if your fine-tune also lets you drop to a much cheaper model, the real saving is the price ratio, not the context — recompute with the small-model price and the payback can improve by 10×.

Experiment 2 — The diagnosis routes differently for the same surface symptom

Three symptom sets that all present as "the answers are wrong", routed by class.

KNOWLEDGE = {"missing_fact", "stale_fact", "needs_citation", "per_user_scope", "deletable_fact"} FORM = {"bad_format", "tone", "schema_noncompliance"} CAPABILITY = {"rare_skill", "domain_reasoning", "cost_reduction"} def diagnose(symptoms): if symptoms & KNOWLEDGE: return "RAG" if symptoms & FORM: return "PROMPT -> CONSTRAIN -> FT" if symptoms & CAPABILITY: return "FINE_TUNE" return "NO_CHANGE" cases = { "policy Q&A gives outdated numbers": {"stale_fact", "needs_citation"}, "extraction emits invalid JSON": {"schema_noncompliance", "bad_format"}, "won't reason about our DSL": {"rare_skill", "domain_reasoning"}, "too slow, too expensive": {"cost_reduction"}, } for desc, sym in cases.items(): print(f" {desc:38s} -> {diagnose(sym)}")

Observed output: policy Q&A gives outdated numbers → RAG · extraction emits invalid JSON → PROMPT -> CONSTRAIN -> FT · won't reason about our DSL → FINE_TUNE · too slow, too expensive → FINE_TUNE (the cost path; in practice route/cache first, then distill). Four "the answers are wrong" complaints, four different fixes. A team that fine-tunes all four has spent three unnecessary training runs and will still be serving stale policy numbers. Falsifier: if a system has both knowledge and form symptoms (common in production), the diagnosis returns RAG because knowledge short-circuits — and that is correct ordering, not a claim that form needs no work. Do the retrieval fix first because it is measurable with citations, then assess whether format problems remain.

Experiment 3 — Why the fine-tune goes stale (copy-paste)

Model the fact-drift of a fine-tune versus a retrieval index, over time, with the same update rate.

def ft_validity(days_since_train, fact_changes_per_day, n_facts): # each fact changes independently; a changed fact is stale in the weights changed = min(n_facts, fact_changes_per_day * days_since_train) return 1 - changed / n_facts def rag_validity(days_since_rebuild, fact_changes_per_day, n_facts, rebuild_every): # retrieval is stale only within the current rebuild window (L97) window = days_since_rebuild % rebuild_every changed = min(n_facts, fact_changes_per_day * window) return 1 - changed / n_facts N, RATE = 500, 2 # 500 facts, 2 change/day print("day fine-tuned RAG(daily rebuild)") for d in (0, 30, 90, 180, 365): print(f"{d:4d} {ft_validity(d, RATE, N):11.3f} {rag_validity(d, RATE, N, 1):.3f}")

Observed output: day 0 → 1.000 / 1.000 · 30 → 0.880 / 1.000 · 90 → 0.640 / 1.000 · 180 → 0.280 / 1.000 · 365 → 0.000 / 1.000. The fine-tuned arm decays monotonically; the RAG arm stays at ~1.0 forever. With 500 facts changing twice a day the fine-tune is 50% stale at day 125 and fully stale at day 250, while retrieval never exceeds one day of drift. That is the structural argument: weights are a snapshot with no refresh mechanism, so any fact that changes makes a fine-tune progressively less true. Falsifier: if your "facts" are actually stable conventions (a DSL grammar, a house style, a schema), their change rate is near zero and fine-tuning them is sound — that is the form/capability case, which is exactly where fine-tuning belongs.

5 · Failure modes #

  • Fine-tuning for facts. Weights bake in knowledge with no refresh path. Symptom: quality decays monotonically after deployment; answers cite numbers that were correct in training data.
  • RAG for format. Retrieval cannot fix syntax. Symptom: the model has the right information and still emits malformed output; retrieval scores look fine.
  • Fine-tuning before prompting. A prompt or constrained decoder would have solved it. Symptom: a training run whose gains a prompt variant achieves for free.
  • Payback not computed. "It removes context so it's cheaper." Symptom: the monthly bill is higher after the fine-tune if the small model is not actually cheaper, or the payback period exceeds the project lifetime.
  • Forgetting. Training on one task degrades others. Symptom: target metric up, unrelated capabilities down; discovered by users, not by the eval (L73).
  • Per-tenant leakage. One fine-tuned model serving all tenants. Symptom: cross-tenant facts surface; no filter exists to prevent it because the facts are in weights.
  • No deletion path. A "delete my data" request against fine-tuned weights. Symptom: the only remedy is a full retrain; compliance cannot be satisfied cheaply.
  • Unevaluated deployment. Fine-tune shipped on training loss. Symptom: loss down, task pass rate flat or worse (L88).

7 · Self-check #

Q1. A product needs per-tenant knowledge, citation, and deletion-on-request. Which lever, and why is the alternative disqualified?

Reveal answer

RAG. Three independent disqualifications of fine-tuning: (a) per-tenant — one model holds all tenants' facts in weights with no filter, so cross-tenant leakage is possible and unfixable by configuration; (b) citation — weights carry no provenance, so the model cannot say which document supports a claim; (c) deletion — removing a fact from weights requires retraining, whereas removing it from an index is a row delete. Each of these is a hard requirement that retrieval satisfies by construction, which is why the diagnosis routes to RAG regardless of cost.

Q2. Fine-tune cost $1,800, context injection 3,000 tokens at $0.30/1M, volume 400,000 queries/month. Compute the payback period and the decision.

Reveal answer

Savings per query = 3000 × 0.30/1e6 = $0.0009. Monthly savings at full volume = 400,000 × 0.0009 = $360. Payback = 1800/360 = 5.0 months — or equivalently 1800/0.0009 = 2,000,000 queries. Decision: at 400k/month that is a 5-month payback on context savings alone, which is not compelling unless the fine-tune also buys something else (a cheaper model, better format compliance). If it does not, RAG or prompt-level context reduction is the better spend; if it also enables a 3× cheaper model tier, recompute with the model savings included and the case may flip.

Q3. Why is a forgetting check mandatory in a fine-tune's eval report?

Reveal answer

Because training on a new distribution shifts the model's behaviour everywhere, not only on the target task (L73). A report showing only the improved metric cannot distinguish "gained the target capability" from "gained the target capability and lost two others" — and the lost capabilities are typically the ones nobody thought to test because they were assumed stable. Practically: keep a small regression suite covering the pre-existing capabilities and run it before and after, and report both. The most common surprise is degraded instruction-following or format compliance on out-of-distribution prompts.

Q4. Diagnostic: after fine-tuning on your support transcripts, the model answers in the right tone but invents product details. What went wrong and what do you do?

Reveal answer

The fine-tune taught form (tone, phrasing, structure) from transcripts, but transcripts also contain facts — so the model learned to produce plausible product details with high confidence and no source (this is the knowledge case being baked in without a refresh path). Fix: keep the behavioural gain and restore factual grounding by combining the fine-tune with RAG for facts plus a grounding instruction (“state only details present in the provided context; otherwise say you do not have it”). Then re-measure faithfulness separately from tone (L87). Training on transcripts is fine for style; it is not a knowledge-injection mechanism, because the facts in the transcripts are already stale by the time you train.

Q5. When is fine-tuning unambiguously the right first choice?

Reveal answer

When the deficiency is a capability or behaviour that is stable over time and cannot be induced by prompting or retrieval: emitting a specific structured output reliably at scale, a reasoning pattern over a proprietary DSL, a house style requiring many examples, or cost reduction via distillation to a smaller model with high volume. The common thread is that the thing being learned changes slowly or not at all (a grammar, a style, a skill), so the snapshot property of weights is not a defect. The opposing test: if the thing being learned changes weekly, weights are the wrong medium no matter how good the fine-tune is.
