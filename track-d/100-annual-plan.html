TRACK D · THE PLAN

L100 · The twelve months from here to level 100

Ninety-nine lessons describe the machine. This one is about your calendar — a full-time job, a family, and roughly ten hours a week. The plan assumes you will miss weeks. Its gates are written so that missing a week costs you nothing, and skipping a gate costs you everything.

~40 min difficulty ●●●●○ build: 5 published artifacts + 1 verifiable claim
time budget evidence gates extend not restart one variable per month pre-registered test external citation quarterly audit decay risk

1 · Why it exists #

The failure this plan is designed to prevent is specific, and it is not laziness. It is the curriculum that dies at week six. You start with energy, read lessons 1–8 in the first weekend, build something small, then a work deadline lands, and the tabs stay open for three months. Nothing was wrong with your discipline. The plan was wrong: it was organised around topics instead of around artifacts and dates, so there was never a moment at which the absence of progress became visible.

The second failure it prevents is subtler and it is the one that would hurt more: learning that produces nothing you can show. Twelve months of study with no repository, no measurement, no published claim is indistinguishable — from the outside and, eventually, from the inside — from twelve months of videos. Every month below ends with a gate that names an artifact. If the artifact does not exist, the month did not happen.

The plan is not "do 100 lessons in 12 months." It is five published artifacts and one verifiable claim, which happen to require most of the 100 lessons to produce honestly. The lessons are the path. The artifacts are the proof. The claim is the point.

You already know this pattern. A CMS audit does not accept "we reviewed the enrollment transactions" — it accepts a workpaper with a sample size, a selection method, a result and a reviewer sign-off. The plan below is the same discipline applied to your own learning, which is the only place you have probably never applied it.

2 · The time budget #

The honest constraint: a full-time job, a family with young children, and a side habit of shipping large agent projects. That is not a schedule with room for twenty hours a week. It is a schedule with room for ten focused hours and a margin for the weeks that collapse.

reading & derivation — 3 h/wk building — 3 h/wk measuring — 2.5 h/wk writing up — 1.5 h/wk
SlotWhenWhat
Daily 20 mincommute / before workFlashcards only. The deck is useless if it is not daily, and it is the cheapest way to not lose what you built.
Two 90-min blocksone weekday evening, one weekend morningReading + derivation, then building. Never both in one block — context-switching inside a block is how the hours disappear.
One 2-hour blockweekendMeasuring and writing. This is the block people skip, and it is the one that produces the artifact.
Monthly 1 hourend of monthGate check: does the artifact exist? Re-score calibration. Write three lines in a log.

The 60% rule. Plan for 60% of the hours you hope to have. If you plan ten and achieve six, you will still finish the year. If you plan twenty and achieve six, you will quit in month two. Every estimate below already assumes the 60% outcome.

3 · The rule: extend, don't restart #

You already have four production systems and a harness. The single biggest efficiency in this plan is that most months add a measurement layer to something that already runs, rather than starting a new toy project. A toy project teaches you the concept; a measurement added to a live system teaches you the concept and produces an artifact nobody else has.

4 · Quarter 1: the arithmetic quarter #

Theme: close the two blind spots that block everything else — scaling arithmetic and RL objectives. This quarter is deliberately front-loaded with Track B and Track C because your profile says those are where the gap is widest, and because they are the most immediately defensible in a review.

MONTH 01Scaling laws as a cost model~40 h
W1L26, L27 — Kaplan and Chinchilla. Read the papers alongside, fit the law yourself in numpy before reading the fit.
W2L28, L29 — over-training and emergence. Then open the scaling lab and reproduce two of its claims by hand.
W3L33 (MLA, out of order on purpose) — do this before L09/L10 so the compression math lands before the intro version.
W4Build: a single-page cost model. Inputs: architecture, workload. Outputs: training GPU-hours, serving cost per 1M queries, binding constraint. Verify against DeepSeek-V3's published 2.788M H800-hours.
Reproduce the 33.1% MFU figure from V3's published numbers without looking at L43. Write the one-line derivation.
MONTH 02Sparse architectures and the KV problem~40 h
W1L30, L31 — MoE and auxiliary-loss-free balancing. Implement a toy router, watch it collapse, then fix it.
W2L32, L34 — DeepSeekMoE and sparse attention. Compute the crossover length where sparsity stops paying.
W3L35, L45 — long context and caching. Then use the KV calculator to price a real workload you care about.
W4Build: a written architecture review of one 2026 model. Publishable as a blog post. The claim must be something a reader could falsify.
Publish the cost model page or the architecture review — one, not both. A published artifact with a number in it.
MONTH 03Post-training objectives, implemented~40 h
W1L51, L52, L55 — post-training map, SFT, DPO. Implement the DPO loss in numpy and verify it on a hand-built preference pair.
W2L56 — GRPO. Do not move on until you can derive the group baseline without notes. This is the month's hardest gate.
W3L57, L58 — PPO and reward modelling. Write down why PPO needs four models and what each is for.
W4Build: a toy RL loop with a verifiable reward on a task you invented. Then attack your own reward function three ways.
Derive GRPO's advantage and explain zero-variance groups on a whiteboard, from memory. Then run a quarterly calibration audit.

Quarterly checkpoint: re-score calibration. Expect L7 still — you have one published artifact and one verified derivation, which moves Measure and Teach a little but not a level. That is correct. Levels are earned quarterly, not weekly.

5 · Quarter 2: the machine quarter #

Theme: actually build the thing. Track A's out-of-order path — attention, cache, then the rest — ending in a trained transformer. This quarter is where the arithmetic from Q1 becomes intuition you cannot unlearn.

MONTH 04Attention, from scratch~40 h
W1L03, L04 — backprop and optimizers. Write micro-autograd by hand; this is the blind spot named in your profile.
W2L06, L07 — self-attention and multi-head. Get the mask right, then verify with a unit test that a padding token cannot attend.
W3L09, L10 — KV cache and GQA. Now reread L33 with the code in front of you.
W4L19 — assemble the full transformer. Train it on a corpus small enough that you can read all of it.
A training run that converges, with a loss curve you can explain point by point, and no unexplained spikes.
MONTH 05The training loop as an engineering artifact~40 h
W1L05, L08, L11–L14 — normalization, position, activations, init, regularization, schedules. Skim the rote parts, derive the stability parts.
W2L15, L16, L20 — masking, loss, and the loop. Build a resumable training harness — this is data-engineering muscle you already have.
W3L17, L18 — sampling and decoding. Implement speculative decoding and measure the actual speedup; the theory says it can also slow you down.
W4L21–L24 — numerics, vector search, data, reading a repo. Then L25, the capstone.
Reproduce a GPT-2-small-scale curve, or document precisely why your hardware will not allow it and what the scaling says you would need.
MONTH 06Reasoning: verifiers and test-time compute~40 h
W1L60–L62 — chain-of-thought, test-time compute, long CoT. Do the arithmetic on when extra samples stop paying.
W2L63, L64, L75 — verifiable rewards, RLVR boundaries, and reward hacking. This is your home turf: generalise the promotion gate.
W3L66, L67 — reasoning distillation and synthetic data. Build a rejection-sampling loop end to end.
W4Build: a reward-hacking test suite — 8 seeded violation classes, in the same shape as JobAgents' anti-fabrication guard.
Publish artifact #2: the reward-hacking suite, or a post on verifier design. Include the failure classes it catches and, honestly, the ones it does not.

Quarterly checkpoint: you should now be at L7–L8. Two published artifacts, one reproduced training curve, and real Measure evidence. If calibration says otherwise, the gap is in the evidence channel, not the knowledge — go look at which checkboxes are empty.

6 · Quarter 3: the measurement quarter #

Theme: the quarter that produces the claim. Everything here exists to move one number from "point estimate" to "estimate with an interval, obtained by a stranger from a written procedure." This is the quarter that calibration calls the difference between L8 and L9.

MONTH 07Evals with statistical power~40 h
W1L88 — alone, for a full week. Wilson intervals, sample sizing, pass@k vs pass^k. Your single most important lesson.
W2L89 — judges and arbiters. Calibrate a judge against human labels; measure its position and verbosity bias rather than assuming them away.
W3L74, L87 — contamination and RAG evaluation. Then design the experiment for month 09 and pre-register it: hypothesis, n, test, falsifier, written down before you look.
W4Build: an eval harness that reports intervals, not point estimates. Retrofit it onto JobAgents or pRash.
A harness that produces an interval. Then apply it to one number you have already published elsewhere and see whether it survives.
MONTH 08The RAKE harness~40 h
W1L95, L90 — the RAKE harness and observability. Design before building; the spec is the deliverable.
W2Build: the run manifest, the immutable artifact bundle, the hash-chained event log outside the writable area, and cost attribution per phase.
W3L96, L99 — the 16 acceptance criteria. Score one of your existing systems against them honestly. Expect a poor score; that is the finding.
W4Build: the reviewer script — a stranger drops in a bundle, gets a pass/fail per criterion, with no conversation.
Hand the bundle to one person who has never seen your harness. They must be able to recompute the result. Count every question they ask — each one is a defect.
MONTH 09Run the pre-registered experiment~40 h
W1Run it. Do not change the design. If it breaks, note the break and run the plan anyway.
W2Analyse honestly. Report the interval, the failures, and what would have changed your mind. A null result is a result.
W3L91–L94 — cost, reliability, safety and determinism. These fill in the failure modes your experiment probably exposed.
W4Write it up as a report, not a blog post: procedure, n, result, interval, threats to validity, and the raw bundle.
Publish artifact #3: a pre-registered experiment with a result, a confidence interval, and a public evidence bundle.

Quarterly checkpoint: this is where L9 becomes reachable. L9 requires evidence that people reproduce when you are in the room. If month 08's stranger needed zero questions, you may already be there.

7 · Quarter 4: the claim quarter #

Theme: turn the result into something that travels without you. This is the FABLE and SKILLA quarter, and it is the only part of this plan that is genuinely novel rather than well-taught.

MONTH 10Make it reproducible by strangers~40 h
W1Write the spec so completely that a competent stranger needs no conversation: environment, seed, procedure, expected numbers, tolerances.
W2Recruit two independent reproducers. People you have not corresponded with on this. Give them the document and nothing else.
W3Fix what they stumbled on without explaining it to them. That is the whole exercise.
W4L97, L98 — production RAG and the fine-tune decision rule, as the practical back half of this track.
Two strangers reproduce your number from the document alone, inside your declared interval, without asking you a question.
MONTH 11Run FABLE on your own systems~40 h
W1Grade 20 existing runs from AgentSwarm and BugAgents against the nine FABLE questions by hand. Baseline first.
W2Upgrade one harness from R2 to R3: oracle verdicts written into the hash chain, re-runnable by a verifier.
W3Re-score. The before/after is the result.
W4Publish honestly, including the unflattering starting score. A negative result about your own systems is more credible than any positive claim.
Publish artifact #4: the FABLE rubric and your own systems' scores, before and after.
MONTH 12The claim, and the year after~40 h
W1Assemble everything: 5 artifacts, 1 pre-registered experiment, 2 independent reproductions, the FABLE scores. Write the paper or the long post.
W2Submit / publish with a stable identifier and a versioned release tag. A DOI or a tagged release, not a moving link.
W3Engage critically: answer the falsification questions in the open. Update the work if someone breaks it.
W4Plan year two around what actually happened, not what you hoped. Re-score calibration. Decide whether to continue SKILLA, pivot to FABLE-only, or go deeper on measurement.
An accurate citation from someone with no prior connection to you. That is the L10 condition, and it is not something you can award yourself.

9 · Failure modes #

  • The silent stop. Symptom: three weeks pass with no flashcard activity and no commit. Cause: the plan was never instrumented. Fix: the daily 20-minute deck is the only non-negotiable, because it is small enough to survive a bad week and it makes the absence visible.
  • Artifact capture. Symptom: you did the work but cannot show it. Cause: the write-up was scheduled for "later." Fix: the artifact gets its own 2-hour weekend block, every week. If the work and the write-up compete, the write-up wins.
  • Scope inflation. Symptom: month 02's project is still open in month 05. Cause: a learning project grown into a product. Fix: every month's build has a hard scope line, and an artifact is published whether or not you are proud of it.
  • Publishing only wins. Symptom: every post is a success story. Cause: the negative results were quietly dropped. Fix: pre-registration is not optional. It is what makes the publication mean anything, and it is the one habit your audit background gives you that most researchers lack.
  • Level-chasing. Symptom: you fill checkboxes to move the number. Cause: the rubric became a game. Fix: the calibration page's own anti-deception rules — every point needs a resolvable pointer, and decay handles the rest. If you have to argue for the point, it is zero.
  • The year that produces nothing novel. Symptom: at month 12 you are a well-informed practitioner with no claim of your own. Cause: months 01–06 are all consumption. Fix: if by month 07 you have no pre-registered experiment, stop the curriculum and run one, even a small one.

10 · The loop that keeps it alive #

Plans fail in the gap between the intention and the calendar. This is the smallest possible instrumentation that closes that gap.

# 10 minutes, every Sunday. Write it in a file. Do not improvise it. WEEK 34 hours_planned 6 # 60% rule: 10 intended -> 6 realistic hours_actual 4.5 lessons_done L61, L62 artifact_moved rake-bundle/schema.md # must name a file blocked_by none next_week wire oracle verdicts into the hash chain flashcard_streak 34 days GATE THIS MONTH a stranger recomputes the result [ ]

The single most important field is artifact_moved. If it says "none" for two consecutive weeks, the month's gate is already at risk and you need to cut scope immediately — not increase effort.

What level 100 actually means. Not that you know everything on the list. It means: one other person, with no connection to you, reads something you wrote, reproduces a number you claimed, cites it correctly, and the citation is still accurate a year later. Everything in this course is scaffolding for that one sentence — and you already have most of the discipline it requires.

Final accounting, in your own idiom. The plan commits to: 10 hours a week for 48 working weeks (~480 hours), 5 published artifacts, 1 pre-registered experiment, 2 independent reproductions, and 1 claim a stranger can check. That is the workpaper. The sign-off is someone else's.
