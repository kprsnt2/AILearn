1 · The one-paragraph thesis #

Most people who learn about AI agents are catching up on both halves of the problem at once: they are reading about transformer internals and learning, for the first time, what it means to be accountable for a record you produced. Prashanth arrived having already done the second half for seven years under federal regulation, and having already done the first half so many times — a published reference list of shipped apps, a fine-tuned 20B model, an autonomous run that lasted 1,441 turns unattended — that the failure points are no longer hypothetical to him.

That changes what a lesson has to do. You do not explain what a promotion gate is to someone who ran a zero-tolerance quality gate on CMS enrollment transactions. You explain why the gate you already built is the correct form of the thing, and how it fails at the level below it — a hash-chain that verifies structure but not provenance, a deterministic scorer that is auditable but not accurate, a measurement that has a number but no confidence interval around it.

The learner is not a beginner. He is a specialist on one axis — evidence and accountability — who has a measurable, specific deficit on two others: the mathematics underneath the machinery, and the statistics that turn a number into a finding. The whole course is shaped to protect the first and repair the other two.

3 · The moat: seven years of regulated audit #

Here is the argument, stated as an audit would state it.

An agent system is easy to build. A loop that calls a model, parses a tool result, and writes a file runs in fifty lines. What is hard is the thing that surrounds the loop: how do you know the loop is telling you the truth? Every serious agent failure in production is a truth failure, not a capability failure — the agent said it fixed the test, the test was not run; the agent said the finding is critical, the proof-of-concept does not reproduce; the agent said the migration succeeded, and nothing exists on disk. Building the capabilities is a weekend. Building the machinery that can contradict the agent on demand is a discipline.

Most agent builders cannot design that machinery. Not because they lack skill, but because they have never been personally accountable for a wrong record in a regulated system. That accountability is not knowledge — it is a prior on what "done" means. It produces three design instincts that are extremely hard to teach from reading:

A promotion gate, not a confidence score

In a CMS audit, a record is either compliant or it is a finding. There is no 0.87. Someone asks "show me the evidence for this line" and if the evidence does not exist, the line is not true. Translating that into machinery produces a gate that promotes an artifact only when independent conditions are all satisfied — and refuses otherwise, regardless of how strongly the agent believes it succeeded.

A tamper-evident ledger, not a belief

Regulated records have a custody chain because the person who wrote them can lie, and so can the process that wrote them. An agent that logs its own success is the accused writing the verdict. The audit instinct is to move the ledger outside the agent's writable workspace and chain each entry to the previous one so that a silently rewritten history becomes detectable rather than deniable.

A zero-false-positive criterion, not an accuracy number

An auditor does not accept "92% accurate". They ask what happens on the 8%, and who signs for it. For an unattended system that files security reports or touches records, one false positive is a credibility event, so the acceptance criterion gets written as a count of false positives = 0 with a measurement procedure attached — a much harsher and much more useful bar than a percentage.

Falsifiability as a habit, not a concept

"A claim without evidence is not a finding" is a sentence you learn in week one of an audit job and then apply ten thousand times. Applying it to an LLM's self-report is the same reflex one layer down. This is why the calibration rubric on this site refuses self-attested points — it is the same rule, applied to the learner.

This is why the course's recurring question is "how do you prove this is true?" rather than "how do you build this?". The build is table stakes for this learner. The proof is the moat — and it is also the part of the market where genuinely few people can compete. Anyone can fine-tune a model. Very few can hand you the eval harness, the ledger, the gate, and the false-positive count.

Where the moat does not help. Being right about accountability does not make a loss curve shallow. It does not explain why GRPO's group-relative baseline reduces variance, why an attention kernel is memory-bandwidth bound rather than FLOP bound, or how large an evaluation has to be before a 2-point improvement is distinguishable from noise. Those are real deficits and section 5 names them.

4 · Production systems, with numbers #

Every figure below is the one published on kprsnt.in state as of 2026-10. Where a number has a structural detail that matters more than the number — zero LLM calls in a scoring path, a ledger outside the writable workspace, a validation against official results — the detail is stated, because that is the part the course will attack in later lessons.

JobAgents — deterministic cost gate

7,613 roles ingested across multiple sources. A five-factor scoring function ranks every role in zero LLM calls. The scoring path is deterministic, which means it is auditable line by line and costs nothing per query; the models are reserved for the parts that genuinely need language.

Course attack surface: L91 cost gates, L94 determinism. Question to answer: a deterministic scorer is reproducible; is it accurate, and against what labelled set?

BugAgents — promotion gate + arbitration

Two autonomous research agents on live bug-bounty scopes, arbitrated by a dual-LLM arbiter, gated by a 4-point promotion rule (scope match, commit match, non-empty reproduction, physical evidence verification). Across 15,317 tool calls and $0.067 total spend, eight reports were promoted with zero false positives.

Course attack surface: L89 judge calibration, L96 production checklist. Question: zero false positives out of eight — what is the upper bound on the true false-positive rate at 95% confidence?

AgentSwarm — forensic arena

Multi-agent research arena with a hash-chained forensic ledger, 32/32 intact, stored outside the agents' writable workspace. An independent adversarial oracle checked veracity over 1,254 tool calls: 0 of 2 self-assessing agents overclaimed, 22 of 25 quantitative claims verified exact.

Course attack surface: L83 swarms, L90 tracing. Question: 0/2 is not a rate. How many self-assessing agents would you need before "zero overclaims" is a finding rather than an anecdote?

Project Awakening — long-horizon autonomy

1,441 turns, 1,553 commits from an unprompted start; 28 architectural modules, 50 generated browser applications, and a 330-turn API quota blackout survived with CI/CD continuity throughout.

Course attack surface: L82 failure compounding. Question: did the system continue working, or continue committing? Those are different claims and only one of them is in the evidence.

Agent Cosmos — dialectic evolution

Multi-agent evolution across 100 epochs on local CLI harnesses; agents diagnosed a broken markdown renderer in their own frontend and built a dedicated viewer — a self-directed repair loop rather than a scripted one.

Course attack surface: L65 self-play and dialectic, L68 self-feedback. Question: what stopped the loop from a 101st epoch, and was that a capability boundary or a budget one?

mSeat — validated against reality

18,000+ medical aspirants, discrete allocation simulator over a 7-dimensional reservation matrix, validated against official 2026 Phase 1 allotment results within a two-college preference delta.

Course attack surface: this is the single strongest evaluation pattern in the whole portfolio — an external, non-negotiable ground truth with a stated tolerance. The calibration rubric treats "tolerance declared before the run" as the difference between an evaluation and a demo.

BrandXY — controlled fine-tune

Fine-tuned a GPT-OSS-20B model to recommend fictional brands; measured 76.47% vs 25.49% baseline — a 50.98-point absolute gap, on an AMD MI300X.

Course attack surface: L52 SFT, L74 contamination. Question: 76.47% against 25.49% — on how many evaluation prompts, and with what interval? If the site does not publish the n, the honest reading is "large effect, unknown precision", which is exactly the Measure channel problem stated in your own portfolio.

MyLocalCLI — orchestration surface

Local-first coding CLI: 26 tools, 5 sub-agents, 6 model providers. Hand-coded tool schemas and subagent scopes — the part of agent engineering that is actually product design.

Course attack surface: L77 tool schemas, L81 subagent scopes.

pRash — the benchmark with a control

A six-way controlled benchmark of coding CLIs on an identical app specification over two rounds, deliberately isolating same model / different CLI and same CLI / different model as separate variables, with static code audits and headless-browser checks.

Why it counts: this is the portfolio's one true experiment — a design with controlled variables and a documented second-round regression — and a controlled variable is the same thing as an audit control. L88 and calibration §2 build directly on this instinct.

What is not claimed. No revenue figures, no user counts, no uptime percentages, no ranking against other engineers. The numbers above are self-published engineering metrics, which are exactly the kind of evidence the calibration document gives partial credit to: real, linkable, and not independently reproduced. That distinction is the entire subject of L94 and the Reproduce channel in the scoring formula.

Geetha — bilingual Gita platform

A bilingual Bhagavad Gita platform covering 700 verses, with verse search, chapter navigation and daily AI-generated reflections. Not a demo: it runs on Vercel and is used by his family and community state as of 2026-10.

Kids' learning apps

A reading buddy for phonics and blending, a phonics app, an interactive chess learning game, a personalized story generator, and a CBSE Grade 10 study platform — a family of small apps with children as the literal, non-negotiable users.

Why this belongs in a profile section. Because it changes the failure model. A researcher can ship a model that is wrong 1% of the time; the distribution of that 1% is the rest of the discussion. A parent shipping a phonics app to their own child cannot absorb a 1% failure quietly — the child is the acceptance test, it fails out loud, and it fails repeatedly. Shipping to his parents and his children is what turns "evaluate the model" from a checkbox into a personal obligation.

It also produces the correct instinct about who the user is. An agent that writes a good technical answer is not finished; an agent that a 60-year-old can use without a manual, in Telugu and English, is finished. Half the Track D material on observability and reliability is about that distance.

6 · What you already know: the 100-lesson map #

Three grades, used honestly and without hedging: COMFORT ZONE — you could skip the intro paragraphs and go straight to the lab; STRETCH — you will understand it but it will cost real study; BLIND SPOT — your prior knowledge will actively mislead you, so it comes first and gets the most minutes.

The basis for each grade is the evidence in sections 2–4: what you have shipped, and what you have not. It is a hypothesis, and the self-assessment tool is how you test it.

Four named blind spots, with the mechanism of the blind spot stated, because "I'm bad at math" is not actionable:

  • Gradient-level derivations (L03, L55–57). You can read loss.backward() and you cannot write what it computes. Consequence: RL losses look like arbitrary code rather than consequences of an objective. Repair: L03 micro-autodiff, then L55's DPO loss by hand, before L57.
  • Kernel-level GPU thinking (L38, L39, L42). You reason in "memory and tokens", not in "tiles, shared memory and bandwidth". Consequence: you cannot tell why FlashAttention is faster, so you cannot tell which efficiency claims are plausible. Repair: L38's bandwidth model, then attempt L39 once, even if badly.
  • RL objective derivations (L56, L57, L71). You know what GRPO does empirically and not what it optimises. Consequence: you cannot predict which of your agentic loop designs will train stably. Repair: L56's toy bandit, then derive the advantage term before reading L57's code.
  • Statistical power in evaluation design (L88, L58, L74, and every "X% vs Y%" claim above). Consequence: your own strongest claims — 76.47% vs 25.49%, zero false positives out of eight — are currently point estimates without intervals, which is precisely the failure an auditor would flag in your own work. Repair: L88 first, then re-derive your three headline numbers with intervals in the Measure channel.

7 · How to study this course given this profile #

The manifest order is the right order for a computer science graduate. It is the wrong order for you, for one structural reason: you need the mechanism before the theory. A derivation of scaling laws lands on nothing if you have never fit a curve to points; a derivation of KV-cache memory lands on nothing if you have never watched a context cost that. So the prescribed order front-loads the blind spots, uses builds as the entry point everywhere, and pushes your comfort zones to the end where they become revision rather than study.

Phase 1 — Track B, lessons 26–28 and 33 (weeks 1–3)

  1. L26 · Kaplan scaling laws — rationale: curve fitting on measurements is the closest thing in deep learning to an audit procedure. It teaches you the form of a quantitative AI argument using a skill you already have.
  2. L27 · Chinchilla — rationale: turns the curve into a decision (where to spend compute), and the compute-optimal calculator gives you an artifact with a number in it on day one.
  3. L28 · Overtraining — rationale: this is the cost-per-query argument you already make in agent design, written down. It is the bridge from L27 into the decisions you make daily.
  4. L33 · MLA — rationale: compression of a cache is the one architecture lesson whose economics you have already lived; learning the math on a problem you already understand intuitively is the cheapest possible way to train the missing math muscle. Do it before L09–L10, not after.

Phase 2 — Track C, lessons 55–59 (weeks 4–7)

  1. L55 · DPO — rationale: an implementable loss with no policy-gradient scaffolding. It proves to you that a loss function is just algebra and a sign, which is the precondition for L56.
  2. L56 · GRPO — rationale: the toy bandit is deliberately small so the derivation is the only hard part. This is where blind spot #3 is attacked directly, with a runnable artefact as the exit condition.
  3. L57 · PPO — rationale: hardest lesson in the course for you. Do not start it until you can derive L56's advantage term without notes. Budget three sessions; write the PPO-vs-GRPO trade-off table even if the derivation is incomplete.
  4. L58 · Reward modelling — rationale: Bradley–Terry is small math, but calibration is the first place your measurement gap becomes visible while you build. The reward-hacking surface maps directly onto your adversarial oracle.
  5. L59 · Process rewards — rationale: step-level verification is a promotion gate applied to reasoning. This is the moat, one layer down. It also teaches you to notice that outcome-only verification produces exactly the overclaiming your oracle was built to catch.

Phase 3 — Track A, lessons 06 / 09 / 10, deliberately out of order (weeks 8–10)

You need these three before the rest of Track A, and the manifest puts them seventh and ninth. Run them first, then go back to L01–L05.

  1. L06 · Self-attention — rationale: every architecture lesson in Track B assumed this matrix product. L26–L34 worked only because they stayed at the systems level; going deeper requires it. Writing numpy attention is the moment "attention is a memory lookup" becomes "attention is a weighted sum of values".
  2. L09 · KV cache — rationale: the bridge back into the systems you live in. Prefill vs decode throughput is the one model in this course you will use weekly to predict a bill. Do L09 straight after L06 while the shapes are fresh.
  3. L10 · GQA / MQA / MLA — rationale: this is the payoff of the L33 detour — you will have done the math version of the memory table before the introductory version, which is the correct order for you. If L33 was hard, do L10 immediately; if L33 was easy, L10 becomes revision and you should move on.

Phase 4 — the highest-value single lesson: L88 (week 11)

L88 · Evals with confidence intervals is scheduled early, alone, and out of track order, because it is the one lesson that changes the credibility of work you have already shipped. Exit condition: you re-report your three headline numbers (BrandXY 76.47/25.49, zero false positives from eight promotions, 22/25 exact claims) with sample sizes and 95% intervals. If any of those intervals crosses zero, that is not a failure — that is your first real finding, and it is worth more than the rest of the course.

Phase 5 — breadth, in manifest order (months 4–10)

Everything remaining. Your comfort zones (L76–84, L91–96) can be read at 1.5× speed using the "audit it against your own incident history" mode — for each claim, name an outage or near-miss in your own systems that it predicts. Everything else at reading speed. Track D's capstone chain (L95–100) is where the level gates get cashed in, so leave four full weeks for it.

What to protect: your audit discipline. It is already the best thing in this portfolio and it is the scarce skill in the market. What to repair: the four blind spots, in the order listed. Every reordering above exists to serve those two sentences.
